Valvular Heart Disease

The last of the three inflammatory, infective, and valvular voiceovers. The instructor says you really need to know your valves: where they are and what they do.

Valve Review

What the valves do

  • Valves let blood leave one chamber and go into the next. When the next chamber contracts, the valve slams shut, keeping blood moving in a unidirectional path. Stenosis, prolapse, or regurgitation messes with that and causes backflow.
  • AV valves: tricuspid and mitral. Semilunar valves: pulmonic and aortic.
  • The mitral lies between the left atrium and left ventricle; the aortic lies after the left ventricle, before systemic circulation.
  • Left heart valves take more of a beating, because the left side is the high-pressure system. A repaired or replaced valve is, more times than not, the mitral or the aortic.

Manifestations

They start to look like heart failure

  • Lewis has a long list; you don't need to memorize all of it. Stick with the classic ones below.
  • Regardless of the cause, blood backs up, and the patient shows left and right heart failure.
  • Backup from the right side goes to the periphery; backup from the left side shows up in the lungs.
  • Classic manifestations: exertional dyspnea, weakness, fatigue, possibly chest pain, maybe a murmur.

Repairing the Valve

If surgery is needed, we would rather repair the patient's own valve than replace it. Percutaneous options include a commissurotomy, a transluminal balloon valvuloplasty, or a percutaneous valve replacement (such as an aortic valve replacement).

RepairWhat It Does
Commissurotomy"-otomy" means opening: the provider opens up a stenosed valve.
Balloon valvuloplastyFor a stiff valve: a balloon is inflated to open it up. Like a commissurotomy, the goal is to open a valve that isn't as open as it should be, without replacing it.
AnnuloplastySurgery to repair and reshape the valve, using annuloplasty rings so it works better.

Replacing the Valve: Mechanical vs Biologic

Mechanical valve

  • More durable; lasts longer.
  • Needs lifetime anticoagulation: the body sees the mechanical pieces as foreign, and clots might form. That is the disadvantage.
  • For someone who will be compliant with lifetime anticoagulation.
  • You will hear a click (it is metal). Some say you can hear it from the bedside without a stethoscope.

Biologic valve (usually porcine, a pig valve)

  • Less durable; doesn't last as long (less than 8 to 10 years).
  • No need for anticoagulation.
  • For someone older with a shorter life expectancy, or someone unwilling or unable to adhere to anticoagulation.
  • No click; you hear only the closure of the valve.
Adventitious heart sounds so far: a rub, a murmur, a gallop, and now a click. Who has a click? Someone with a mechanical valve.

After Valve Replacement

Recovery and anticoagulation

  • Recovery is similar to a CABG; sometimes valves are replaced at the same time as the bypass ("killing two birds with one stone").
  • A prosthetic valve means anticoagulation. On warfarin (Coumadin), the INR is monitored for a lifetime.
  • Per the lecture: normal INR 0.8 to 1.1; goal INR on warfarin 2.5 to 3.5. Thin the blood, but not too thin; any higher and they could have issues.
  • Antidote for bleeding: vitamin K.
