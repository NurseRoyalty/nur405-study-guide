Inflammation Markers: CRP & ESR

CRP (C-reactive protein)

  • Indicates inflammation in the body; non-specific (it doesn't say where).
  • More sensitive than the ESR.
  • Peaks within 18 to 72 hours after an MI; failure to normalize might indicate ongoing damage to the heart muscle.
  • With an MI there is inflammation in addition to heart cell death.
  • Monitors more closely for the acute picture.

ESR (sed rate)

  • Also a non-specific indicator of inflammation; could possibly be infection.
  • More useful for monitoring chronic inflammation.
  • CRP and ESR often go hand in hand.
  • Neither tells you where in the body the inflammation is (it could be an arthritis flare, for instance).

BNP & Pro-BNP

Brain natriuretic peptide

  • Despite the word "brain," the main source of BNP is the ventricles of the heart.
  • Helps distinguish cardiac versus respiratory causes of dyspnea: a short-of-breath patient in the ED, where the provider wants to know if it is heart failure or respiratory failure.
  • An elevated BNP indicates left ventricular dysfunction.
  • Pro-BNP is what the instructor sees most often at UK. Plasma concentrations of BNP and pro-BNP are similar, but with left ventricular dysfunction (left-sided heart failure) pro-BNP rises much more significantly than BNP.
  • No ranges given: you don't need to memorize numbers for this, and NCLEX won't expect you to.
Rule of thumb from the lecture: BNP or pro-BNP elevated, think heart failure. Troponin elevated, think myocardial infarction.

Cholesterol & Lipoproteins

Total cholesterol

  • The main lipid associated with cardiovascular disease.
  • We eat it and we also manufacture it; high cholesterol can be inherited (familial).
  • Done as part of a lipid profile; not a totally accurate predictor of heart disease by itself.
  • Diurnal variation: a morning reading can differ from an afternoon reading.
  • Affected by malnutrition: if it is very low, also look at the serum albumin to see how malnourished the patient is.

HDL and LDL

  • An accurate predictor of heart disease.
  • HDL = the "good" cholesterol; removes cholesterol in the body.
  • LDL = the "bad" cholesterol; deposits cholesterol in the peripheral tissue.
  • We like high HDLs and low LDLs.
  • The values help determine whether the patient is low, moderate, or high risk for CAD.
The one number to know: total cholesterol less than 200. The instructor does not ask you to memorize the LDL, HDL, or triglyceride numbers; a test question would speak in general terms.

12-Lead EKG vs Telemetry

12-lead EKG

  • 12 perspectives at once.
  • A snapshot in time.
  • Can diagnose an MI.

Telemetry

  • A one-way perspective.
  • Continuous and ongoing.
  • Monitors only; cannot diagnose.

Ejection Fraction

Definition and values

  • The amount of blood pumped out of the left ventricle with each contraction; an indication of how effectively the heart is pumping.
  • Measured with an echocardiogram, and (per the lecture) also with a right-sided heart cath.
  • Normal: 55% to 70% of the blood sitting in the ventricle at the end of diastole is ejected (about half or a little more).
  • Low values, such as 40%, are seen in heart failure. A really, really low ejection fraction can mean the patient might need a heart transplant.
  • Very important information for a patient who might have heart failure.

Left vs Right Heart Cath

Left heart cath

  • Enter an artery (femoral, radial, or brachial).
  • The catheter goes retrograde up into the ascending aorta / arch of the aorta.
  • The goal is always to get to the coronary arteries; this is the absolute only way to reach them.
  • Used when a patient has chest pain or angina and the question is coronary occlusion.
  • Places examined: the aorta, right coronary artery, left anterior descending, circumflex, and left main coronary artery; the left ventricle sits at the apex.

Right heart cath

  • Enter the venous system.
  • Can measure ejection fraction and look at the pressures of the right side of the heart.
  • Cannot look at the coronary arteries.
  • Sometimes only one side is done; a lot of times both are done.

Before the Heart Cath

Pre-procedure

  • NPO; meds with sips of water may be allowed (provider decision).
  • Signed consent (definitely invasive); the patient and family need to understand the procedure and what to expect.
  • Very little pain, because blood vessels don't have nerve fibers.
  • Warn the patient about the warm flush / hot flash when the contrast dye is injected; it is an expected finding, so they don't think something is wrong.
  • Pre-procedure checklist; monitor BUN and creatinine (dye can be nephrotoxic); empty the bladder before going.
  • Local anesthesia and likely IV sedation to keep them from being anxious.

Contrast Nephrotoxicity

Acute kidney injury after dye

  • An acute kidney injury (AKI) that happens after a dye injection; a major cause of AKI in the hospitalized patient.
  • Increased risk with existing renal problems, diabetes, or fluid volume deficit.
  • Acetylcysteine, and sometimes sodium bicarbonate, are given before the procedure to offset the elevated BUN and creatinine.
  • Encourage the patient to force fluids afterward to dilute the dye going through the kidneys.

Part 2 — CAD & Acute Coronary Syndrome

From CAD & Acute Coronary Syndrome. Built from the three Week 1 coronary voiceovers: coronary heart disease and chronic stable angina (part 1), acute coronary syndrome and MI (part 2), and caring for the patient with ACS, including PCI, thrombolytics, and CABG (part 3).

Coronary Arteries & Terminology

The basics

  • Coronary heart disease, coronary artery disease (CAD), and ischemic heart disease are used interchangeably.
  • The coronary arteries perfuse the heart, getting oxygen to the heart muscle. We want patent coronary arteries.
  • You can predict what area of the heart an artery perfuses from the anatomy. The instructor is not going to ask you to memorize the arteries, but the providers look at a few main ones: the left main, the left anterior descending (LAD), the circumflex, and the right coronary artery.
  • The left main and LAD perfuse the left ventricle, the powerhouse chamber. Complete blockage there is sometimes called the "widow maker", because those patients often never make it to the hospital.
  • Atherosclerosis ("hardening of the arteries") is responsible for almost all cases of CAD, regardless of type.

The CAD Continuum

Think of coronary artery disease as a continuum, from chronic ischemic heart disease to acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Once it starts looking like unstable angina, it is collectively called ACS, because time is of the essence if it advances to an MI.

Least Severe  Most Severe
Stable anginaUnstable anginaNSTEMISTEMI
Chronic ischemic heart disease; the patient lives with it.ACS. No heart damage.ACS. Heart damage.ACS. More extensive heart damage.

"A STEMI is worse than a non-STEMI, a non-STEMI is worse than unstable angina, and unstable angina is worse than stable angina."

Risk Factors for CAD

Non-modifiable

  • Age: the older someone gets, the more likely CAD is.
  • Gender: males are at greater risk than females.
  • Family history: especially relatives who developed CAD younger.

The four most firmly established modifiable

  • Elevated serum lipids (cholesterol, triglycerides); we want high HDLs and low LDLs.
  • Hypertension: untreated, the increased arterial pressure causes damage over time (the hardening). It can be silent and asymptomatic, so people don't perceive it as a big deal. Patients with CKD or diabetes have an even lower BP goal.
  • Tobacco use: risk is proportional to the number of cigarettes smoked.
  • Physical inactivity.

Other modifiable risk factors

  • Obesity: apple-shaped people (rotund in the belly) are at higher risk than pear-shaped people (hips wider than the abdomen).
  • Diabetes mellitus: poor glucose control aggravates coronary heart disease.
  • Depression or prolonged stress.

Chronic Stable Angina

Angina means chest pain from a temporary loss of oxygenation to the heart muscle, more often than not from an occluded coronary artery (but you can't know without the tests).

Where the pain can be

  • Substernal, possibly radiating up to the neck.
  • The classic: substernal, radiating down the left arm. It can radiate down both arms.
  • Epigastric (troponins help rule cardiac in or out; sometimes it is just gastric reflux).
  • Mid-scapular. The instructor once had a patient with pain in the elbows: it is not exactly predictable.

What makes it "stable"

  • Occurs with moderate to prolonged exertion, in a pattern familiar to the patient.
  • Frequency, duration, and intensity stay pretty much stable.
  • Only slight limitations in activity; regulated on medication.
  • Usually associated with fixed atherosclerotic plaque.
  • Relieved by rest and/or nitroglycerin.
  • Managed with drug therapy; rarely requires aggressive therapy.

Diagnosing Stable Angina

Workup

  • Physical exam and complete history; EKG.
  • Exercise stress test, or a pharmacological stress test if they can't use the treadmill or bike: a drug with positive inotropic and chronotropic effects (increased contractility and heart rate) to stress the heart on purpose.
  • Low and intermediate risk results: manage symptoms without an intervention.
  • High risk results: more testing, such as cardiac catheterization followed by revascularization (PCI or CABG), and/or an echo or TEE.

Goals and Medications for Stable Angina

Goal: decrease the workload on the heart and increase the oxygen supply, targeting the modifiable risk factors as appropriate.

Teaching about sublingual nitro

  • Take it at the beginning of chest pain, or prophylactically before activity known to cause it (walking, bike riding, sexual activity).
  • Works in less than 3 minutes; keep it accessible (in your pocket).
  • Protect it from light (brown bottle); replace the bottle every 6 months.
  • It should tingle as it dissolves; no tingle may mean it has expired. Continued pain or no tingle: activate EMS.
  • Never combine with erectile dysfunction drugs; this can be fatal.

Side effects (all from vasodilation)

  • Headache (not a reason to skip the nitro).
  • Lowered BP: fainting and postural hypotension.
  • Flushing.
Always know the blood pressure before giving nitroglycerin, because it is going to lower it. A patient with a low baseline (for example 90/60) can plummet. If the BP is too low, no more nitro is given; something different, like IV morphine, may be used.

Unstable Angina

How it differs from stable angina

  • Often occurs at rest, unannounced and unprecipitated; usually lasts more than 20 minutes.
  • New onset that markedly limits physical activity (stops the person in their tracks).
  • Increasing angina: more frequent, longer, with less exertion than the patient's previous stable angina.
  • Poorly relieved by rest or nitroglycerin.
  • Unpredictable and unstable. Angina is an emergency because unstable angina precedes an MI.

Atypical Presentations

Women and people with diabetes

  • Symptoms are sometimes atypical: no classic radiation down the left arm.
  • Fatigue is a more prominent manifestation (a very big symptom); shortness of air; indigestion; anxiety.
  • Older patients may also present atypically.
  • A silent MI is one the person didn't know they had. Most MIs are not silent.

Angina Pain vs MI Pain

Angina pain

  • Precipitated by exertion or stress.
  • Relieved by rest and/or sublingual nitroglycerin.
  • Lasts less than 15 minutes.

MI pain

  • Occurs without a cause; sometimes wakes people from early-morning sleep (the instructor has read 4 to 6 a.m. is peak, possibly tied to the normal morning cortisol surge).
  • Relieved only by opioids (morphine).
  • Lasts 20 minutes or longer.
  • Often with nausea, diaphoresis, shortness of breath, and marked anxiety; can be associated with dysrhythmias.
  • Severe and immobilizing; not relieved by rest, position change, or nitrates.
  • Described as heaviness, pressure, tightness, burning, constriction, crushing ("an elephant sitting on my chest").
Ask about pressure, not just pain. A cardiac patient may say no to "pain in your chest" and yes to "pressure in your chest." Choose words the patient understands.

How an MI Evolves

Time is muscle

  • Cells can stand about 20 minutes of ischemia before cell death starts.
  • The subendocardial layer is affected first; it takes about 4 to 6 hours for the entire thickness of the heart to necrose. (Chest pain at noon means full-thickness damage around 4 to 6 p.m.)
  • That is why ACS patients need to activate EMS and get to the hospital: waiting costs heart muscle.
  • The location of the MI correlates with the blocked coronary artery (an LAD blockage damages the apex, where the left ventricle is).
  • MIs are named by location: anterior, lateral, inferior, septal, posterior (for example, an inferior or inferolateral MI). You don't need to know this for the test; it is lingo you may hear.

Diagnosing ACS: EKG Changes

The ST segment

  • We look for changes in the QRS, the ST segment, and the T wave (more in the dysrhythmia content).
  • At rest the ST segment should sit on the isoelectric line.
  • ST elevation = above the isoelectric line; with a positive troponin, that is a STEMI. Points to infarction (cell death, permanent).
  • ST depression = below the isoelectric line; sometimes happens with ischemia, sometimes infarction. Points to ischemia (temporary).
  • A STEMI shows ST elevation in two anatomically contiguous leads (contiguous = sharing a common border, touching each other).
  • A STEMI cannot be diagnosed by telemetry; it takes a 12-lead EKG.
  • Serial EKGs may be ordered because ischemia and infarction can change over a few hours.

Initial Care of the ACS Patient

Assess and stabilize

  • Consider ACS with chest discomfort, shortness of air, or other suggestive symptoms; remember atypical presentations in women, older patients, and patients with diabetes.
  • 12-lead EKG within 10 minutes of arrival; repeat every 10 to 15 minutes if not definitive and MI is still suspected.
  • Airway, breathing, circulation. Sit the patient upright to support ventilation; supplemental oxygen per nasal cannula.
  • Baseline vital signs ASAP, including O2 saturation.
  • Telemetry ASAP to monitor for dysrhythmias (the number one complication) and ST changes. If you notice ST elevation on tele, the next best nursing action is a 12-lead EKG (cardiac nurses usually have a standing order).
  • IV access, for emergency or code medications.
  • Aspirin to chew and swallow (antiplatelet).
  • Assess pain with PQRST.

Pain Relief in ACS

Nitroglycerin first

  • 0.4 mg sublingual is pretty standard, one at a time, spaced 5 minutes apart for persistent chest pain.
  • Check BP before each dose; it is likely to drop (a potent coronary vasodilator lowers peripheral vascular resistance).
  • If BP gets too low, switch to morphine.

Morphine

  • Eases the workload on the heart.
  • Decreases preload and afterload.
  • Helps with anxiety.
  • Patients often tolerate IV morphine better than nitroglycerin.

Labs and Ongoing Assessment

What to monitor

  • Cardiac markers: troponin is the most specific to cardiac muscle (some providers still order CK-MB or myoglobin).
  • Electrolytes, especially potassium, calcium, and magnesium: imbalances can be the root cause of dysrhythmias.
  • Hemoglobin and hematocrit; coagulation status (bleeding risk).
  • Heart sounds: listen for a murmur, gallop, or rub.
  • Lung sounds: an infarction in the left ventricle can lead to heart failure, and crackles could point to left-sided heart failure.

PCI (Angioplasty, the "Balloon Procedure")

What happens

  • The heart cath shows whether blockages exist, where, how bad, and whether there is collateral circulation (little vessels the body grew around the blockage to perfuse the other side). It also evaluates left ventricular function.
  • It is a left heart cath (through the brachial or femoral artery) to reach the coronary arteries.
  • A guide wire and balloon catheter go to the occlusion; the balloon inflates and smashes the plaque back against the wall, then deflates and is removed.
  • Sometimes a stent is placed to reestablish patency.

Advantages over open-heart surgery

  • Percutaneous, through the vascular system; no cracking the chest.
  • Local anesthesia, not general.
  • Ambulatory shortly after.
  • Significantly shorter hospital stay; back to work and life sooner.
  • More PCIs are performed than CABGs.

PCI Nursing Care

What the CAD lecture adds for PCI

  • Monitor closely for MI progression: chest pain, EKG changes, new or different dysrhythmias, hemodynamic instability (unstable BP and heart rate).
  • Dual antiplatelet therapy: aspirin plus a drug like clopidogrel (Plavix). Possibly IV heparin during the procedure. It is a delicate balance; we don't want the patient to bleed out.
  • The sheath is usually removed 4 to 6 hours after the procedure; an arterial puncture needs manual pressure for a good 20 minutes.
  • Assess distal to the puncture: for a left groin site, compare the left foot's DP and PT pulses and cap refill to the right foot.
  • Watch for bleeding externally (the dressing) and internally: a heart rate creeping up can be the body compensating for lost blood volume.

Thrombolytic Therapy ("Clot Busters")

The drugs

  • Fibrinolytics: they lyse fibrin to dissolve thrombi in the coronary arteries and restore blood flow.
  • Examples: tPA and reteplase.
  • Per the lecture, given during the cardiac catheterization.
  • PCI is more commonly performed than thrombolytic therapy.
  • Monitor closely for bleeding after administration.

Who should NOT get them

  • History of a brain bleed or stroke, particularly hemorrhagic stroke.
  • Recent abdominal surgery (bleeding risk).
  • Active bleeding.
  • Coagulation studies that are out of range.

CABG (Coronary Artery Bypass Graft)

The procedure

  • The surgeon sews (anastomoses) a graft proximal and distal to the blockage, literally bypassing it. Up to six grafts (for example "CABG times one," "CABG times three").
  • The patient's own vessels are used, such as the internal mammary artery or the saphenous vein (the long vein from the groin down the medial leg to the ankle). Veins have valves, so the graft is turned in favor of blood flow. Which vessel is the surgeon's preference; nothing you need to know as the nurse.
  • Valve replacement is sometimes done at the same time.

Ongoing Care & Recovery

After the procedure

  • Continuous telemetry; rest, comfort, activity restriction for a while; good pain control.
  • Expect anxiety; teach at the level where the patient is, since everyone is in a different place. The nurse can be a game changer.
  • Timing matters for emotional and behavioral reactions. Example: resuming sexual activity, especially if that is what brought them in. Have those conversations before discharge so they understand their limitations.
  • Needs at home span physiological, psychological, mental, spiritual, economic, and vocational areas.

Part 3 — Pericarditis

From Pericarditis. The first of three voiceovers on inflammatory, infective, and valvular heart disease. The friction-rub details from the CAD lecture (where pericarditis came up as an MI complication) are gathered here too, so the rub lives in one place.

The Pericardium

Layers and the potential space

  • The pericardium covers the heart. "-itis" means inflammation or infection: with pericarditis think inflammation (endocarditis is more about infection).
  • The fibrous pericardium is the outside; it circles back around into the visceral and parietal pericardium, with the pericardial space in between.
  • The pericardial space is a potential space holding usually 5 to 10 mL of fluid, just enough to keep the heart lubricated and smooth while it pumps.
  • Beneath are the myocardium and the endocardium.

Causes

CauseWhat the Lecture Says
IdiopathicOften we really don't know why it happens.
Infective organismsA variety sometimes precede pericarditis.
After an MIA complication of an MI; usually shows up a few days after, from the MI's inflammation.
Connective tissue diseasesThe pericardium is connective tissue. Examples: rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and lupus.

Pericarditis Pain

How it presents

  • Substernal / precordial (behind the breastbone), radiating to the left side of the neck, shoulder, and back.
  • A grating, oppressive feel (oppressive: hard to catch your breath).
  • Aggravated by breathing (mainly inspiration), coughing, and swallowing.
  • Worse lying down; better sitting up and leaning forward (leaning forward minimizes the stretch on the pericardium). A positional thing.

Pericarditis Pain vs MI Pain

After an MI you have to tell the two apart, since pericarditis is an MI complication.

Pericarditis pain

  • Onset usually more sudden.
  • Substernal, left precordial; may radiate.
  • Sharp and stabbing, or aching and dull; oppressive.
  • Worse on inspiration.
  • Body movement can increase it; leaning forward makes it better.
  • Can wax and wane.
  • Sublingual nitroglycerin won't help: nitro is a coronary vasodilator, and pericarditis has nothing to do with coronary vasodilation.

MI pain

  • Position is not going to matter.
  • Breathing isn't going to impact the pain.
  • Ischemia is what nitroglycerin addresses.

The Pericardial Friction Rub

The hallmark finding of pericarditis is the pericardial friction rub.

What it sounds like and why

  • A "squeaky heart"; a grating, continuous grinding sound, like two pieces of sandpaper rubbing together.
  • The visceral and parietal pericardium normally have a little fluid between them. Inflammation takes away that fluidity, and the two layers rub (CAD lecture).
  • Same idea as a pleural friction rub, when the visceral and parietal pleura are inflamed (for example, pleurisy).

How to listen

  • Stethoscope at the lower left sternal border.
  • Patient leaning forward (brings the heart closer to the chest wall).
  • Ask the patient to hold their breath to eliminate breath sounds and focus on the heart.
  • From the CAD lecture: if the grating disappears when the patient holds their breath, it is not cardiac in origin (it is respiratory).

Complications: Pericardial Effusion & Tamponade

Pericardial Effusion

Fluid in the pericardial space

  • Pericardial effusion is to the pericardium what pleural effusion is to the pleural space: third spacing, a collection of fluid that serves absolutely no purpose.
  • Symptoms come from the fluid squeezing the heart.
  • Cues: cough, tachypnea, difficulty breathing, and heart sounds that aren't crisp (fluid between the stethoscope and the heart).
  • The pericardial space doesn't expand much. As it fills, the heart gets squished, can't contract and release like it should, and cardiac output suffers.
  • Seen on an echocardiogram (or TEE), which delineates the heart's structures.

Cardiac Tamponade

Tamponade is time-sensitive and can be life-threatening. It develops when a pericardial effusion increases in volume.

Narrowed pulse pressure

  • Pulse pressure = systolic minus diastolic.
  • 120/80 gives a pulse pressure of 40: pretty average.
  • In tamponade it narrows, such as 110/90: a pulse pressure of 20.

Pulsus paradoxus

  • BP normally drops a little with breathing, but not significantly. Significant = greater than 10 mmHg.
  • Systolic BP is 10 mmHg or more higher on expiration than on inspiration. (Lewis says it one way, UpToDate the other: 10 mmHg lower on inspiration. Same thing.)
  • For the skilled practitioner; the instructor has assessed it when the patient has an arterial line (systolic waxes and wanes with breathing). You won't get checked off on it.

Part 4 — Infective Endocarditis

From Infective Endocarditis. With pericarditis think inflammation; with endocarditis think infection. "Infective endocarditis" is the longer name, but it is usually just called endocarditis.

What It Is

The endocardium and the valves

  • The endocardium is the inside of the heart, which is a cue to where the problem is.
  • The valves are a very movable part of the heart, and a lot of the manifestations of endocarditis relate to the valves.
  • Endocarditis occurs when blood flow within the heart lets the organism infect previously damaged valves or another endothelial surface.

Subacute vs Acute

Subacute

  • Affects those with pre-existing heart problems.
  • Typically milder in presentation.
  • Can be more chronic.

Acute

  • Affects healthy valves.
  • Manifests itself quicker.
  • Therapy needs to be prompt and vigorous.

Who Gets It & What Causes It

Two things have to happen

  • A history of damaged epithelium, such as a valve replacement or a defect.
  • A port of entry for infection to reach the endocardium: a sore, a dental procedure, IV drug use.

Causative organisms

  • Most common: Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus viridans (bacterial).
  • Other possible pathogens: fungi and viruses, so not all endocarditis is bacterial.

Vegetations & Emboli

Why vegetations matter

  • Infection on the moving valves develops vegetations, for example mitral valve vegetation between the left atrium and left ventricle. Bacterial endocarditis of the mitral valve covers it with large, irregular vegetations.
  • The valves are constantly opening and closing, so vegetations can slough off and become emboli, dispersed into the vascular system.

Left-sided vegetation

  • Leaves the heart into the organs.
  • Can cause a limb infarction or an infarction in any organ of the body.

Right-sided vegetation

  • Blood from the right side goes to the lungs.
  • Like a pulmonary embolus.

Manifestations

Very non-specific at first

  • Timing: the transcript says it typically occurs "within 10 two weeks" of the infectious organism (unclear wording in the transcript, so no firm number is given here).
  • Low-grade fever, chills, malaise, fatigue, anorexia, joint pain, muscle pain. If you didn't know better, you'd think it was the flu.
  • Clubbing of the fingers is the odd one out, and it comes later in the disease process, not early.

Clinical Manifestations

Heart failureThe most common complication of infective endocarditis. Damaged valves leak blood back instead of letting it go one direction, the blood backs up, and the patient develops left- and right-sided heart failure.

History & Diagnostic Tests

ItemWhat to Look For
HistoryRecent dental, urological, or gynecologic procedure; IV drug use.
Blood culturesThree cultures drawn over an hour apart from three different sites; positive results are indicative of endocarditis.
EchocardiogramEvidence of endocardial enlargement.

Prophylactic Antibiotics

Who gets prophylaxis

  • A mechanical heart valve.
  • A history of endocarditis.
  • A history of congenital heart disease.
  • A heart transplant.

Before which procedures

  • Any dental visit.
  • A biopsy of any kind.
  • Tonsil and adenoid removal.
  • A wound infection or urinary tract infection.

Treatment & Nursing Care

Treatment

  • Typically hospitalized initially; medications usually given IV.
  • Complete eradication may take weeks upon weeks; relapses are common.
  • Home care may include going home with a PICC line for IV antibiotics.

Nursing care and teaching

  • Identify and educate high-risk individuals so they can avoid infections.
  • Good rest; good oral hygiene (the mouth is where bacteria can enter).
  • Let the provider know before any dental procedure.
  • Refer IV drug users to drug rehab.
  • Monitor for the non-specific signs and symptoms that indicate a recurrence.

Part 5 — Valvular Heart Disease

From Valvular Heart Disease. The last of the three inflammatory, infective, and valvular voiceovers. The instructor says you really need to know your valves: where they are and what they do.

Valve Review

What the valves do

  • Valves let blood leave one chamber and go into the next. When the next chamber contracts, the valve slams shut, keeping blood moving in a unidirectional path. Stenosis, prolapse, or regurgitation messes with that and causes backflow.
  • AV valves: tricuspid and mitral. Semilunar valves: pulmonic and aortic.
  • The mitral lies between the left atrium and left ventricle; the aortic lies after the left ventricle, before systemic circulation.
  • Left heart valves take more of a beating, because the left side is the high-pressure system. A repaired or replaced valve is, more times than not, the mitral or the aortic.

Manifestations

They start to look like heart failure

  • Lewis has a long list; you don't need to memorize all of it. Stick with the classic ones below.
  • Regardless of the cause, blood backs up, and the patient shows left and right heart failure.
  • Backup from the right side goes to the periphery; backup from the left side shows up in the lungs.
  • Classic manifestations: exertional dyspnea, weakness, fatigue, possibly chest pain, maybe a murmur.

Conservative Management

Initially we try not to do surgery; treatment escalates if the patient becomes symptomatic enough.

Repairing the Valve

If surgery is needed, we would rather repair the patient's own valve than replace it. Percutaneous options include a commissurotomy, a transluminal balloon valvuloplasty, or a percutaneous valve replacement (such as an aortic valve replacement).

RepairWhat It Does
Commissurotomy"-otomy" means opening: the provider opens up a stenosed valve.

AnnuloplastySurgery to repair and reshape the valve, using annuloplasty rings so it works better.

Replacing the Valve: Mechanical vs Biologic

Mechanical valve

  • More durable; lasts longer.
  • Needs lifetime anticoagulation: the body sees the mechanical pieces as foreign, and clots might form. That is the disadvantage.
  • For someone who will be compliant with lifetime anticoagulation.
  • You will hear a click (it is metal). Some say you can hear it from the bedside without a stethoscope.

Biologic valve (usually porcine, a pig valve)

  • Less durable; doesn't last as long (less than 8 to 10 years).
  • No need for anticoagulation.
  • For someone older with a shorter life expectancy, or someone unwilling or unable to adhere to anticoagulation.
  • No click; you hear only the closure of the valve.
Adventitious heart sounds so far: a rub, a murmur, a gallop, and now a click. Who has a click? Someone with a mechanical valve.

After Valve Replacement

Recovery and anticoagulation

  • Recovery is similar to a CABG; sometimes valves are replaced at the same time as the bypass ("killing two birds with one stone").
  • A prosthetic valve means anticoagulation. On warfarin (Coumadin), the INR is monitored for a lifetime.
  • Per the lecture: normal INR 0.8 to 1.1; goal INR on warfarin 2.5 to 3.5. Thin the blood, but not too thin; any higher and they could have issues.
  • Antidote for bleeding: vitamin K.
