Hemoglobin & Hematocrit

Commit these to memory "forever" (the instructor's words): Hemoglobin women 12 to 16, men 14 to 18. Hematocrit women 37 to 47%, men 42 to 52%.

Definitions

  • Hemoglobin: the iron-containing pigment of the RBC.
  • Hematocrit: the percentage of total blood volume made up of RBCs. The hematocrit is roughly three times the hemoglobin (a hemoglobin around 12 goes with a crit of 36).
  • A low crit is anemia (anemia doesn't cause it); the cause might be bleeding, bone marrow failure, dietary deficiency, renal disease. Too many RBCs = polycythemia.
  • Trend, don't read stand-alone values: a post-op H&H of 9.2 and 28.1 that was lower yesterday is trending up; keep an eye on it.

False lows and false highs

  • Fluid retention (heart failure, kidney failure): extra plasma lowers the percentage = a false low hematocrit, not true anemia.
  • Dehydration: less plasma raises the percentage = a false high hematocrit, not true polycythemia.
The bus analogy again: RBC = bus, hemoglobin = seats, iron = what the seats are made of, oxygen = passengers. O2 sat = how full the buses are (we want 95% or higher). Hematocrit = how much of the highway is buses.

Melena vs iron stools

  • Melena: dark, tarry, very malodorous, sticky stool from digested blood. Significant.
  • Stool on iron is also black, but that is expected. Teach the difference: blood is very malodorous and tacky.

Anemia

Blood cell terms

  • Leukopenia = low WBCs; neutropenia = low neutrophils (big trouble: the first responders are gone); leukocytosis = too many WBCs (infection).
  • Thrombocytopenia = low platelets (thrombocytes). Erythrocytes = RBCs. "-penia" = low or deficient.
  • Pancytopenia = low RBCs, WBCs, and platelets (aplastic anemia).

Causes of Anemia

CauseExamples From the Lecture
Decreased RBC productionNot enough hemoglobin synthesis (iron deficiency); impaired DNA synthesis (B12 or folic acid: the megaloblastic anemias); decreased RBC precursors (aplastic anemia, chemotherapy, chronic disease). Chronic kidney failure = decreased erythropoietin = fewer RBCs. Liver issues = decreased iron availability.
Blood lossAcute (ruptured spleen, ruptured aneurysm, surgery): no time to compensate; quick cardiac instability and a rising heart rate. Chronic (heavy menstrual periods, GI bleeding such as a peptic ulcer or ulcerative colitis): insidious; someone can walk around with a hemoglobin of 10 and be barely symptomatic.
Increased RBC destruction (hemolysis)Sickle cell anemia (peds); a hemolytic transfusion reaction from incompatible blood.

Post-op patients commonly come back with a low H&H; that is expected. GI bleeding is always on the list for anemia of unknown origin.

Write this down: CO = HR × SV. Cardiac output (blood ejected from the left ventricle per minute) = heart rate × stroke volume (blood ejected per beat). On med surg you measure it with vital signs: blood pressure is an indirect measure of cardiac output.

Nursing Care of the Anemic Patient

Nursing problems

  • Activity intolerance (probably the most common): fine at rest, but heart rate up, palpitations, anxiety, and short of breath with activity. Fatigue is being tired even lying in bed.
  • Nutrition (not taking in or not absorbing nutrients).
  • Aplastic anemia: risk for bleeding (thrombocytopenia), infection (neutropenia), and fatigue (anemia), all in one patient.

Ongoing care

  • Trend labs; watch for hypoxemia; vital signs and O2 sat; neuro checks (B12).
  • Oxygen for the very anemic patient (fewer buses need all the help they can get).
  • Rest periods.
  • Keep them warm, but no heating pads (paresthesia risk).
  • Possible PRBC transfusion.
  • Treat the cause: nutrition, stop alcohol, supplements, find and stop the bleeding.

Folic acidBy mouth; no real adverse effects; a big vitamin in pregnancy.

Food teaching (a favorite select-all-that-apply topic)

  • Iron-rich foods; most books say give vitamin C with iron (the literature is mixed).
  • B12 is only in animal products: strict vegans need supplements.
  • Folic acid: green leafy vegetables, legumes, grains, orange juice, nuts.

Polycythemia

An abnormally high H&H and RBC count. Three classifications.

TypeCause
RelativeDecreased plasma volume or dehydration: the false high hematocrit; not a true increase in RBCs.
Primary (polycythemia vera)A genetic change during the person's lifetime. Not preventable; never goes away.
SecondaryCompensation for tissue hypoxia: COPD (the most common cause overall) or a lifetime at high altitude. "My body compensates by making more buses."

Manifestations

  • Thicker, more blood: hypertension, headache, trouble concentrating.
  • Ruddy (dusky red) complexion; possible cyanosis.
  • Clots or hemorrhage: angina, MI, TIA, stroke. The most common serious acute complication is a stroke.
  • Hypermetabolic: night sweats, weight loss.
  • Pruritus (itching) worsened by a hot bath is classic; pain in the fingers and toes.

Collaborative care

  • Phlebotomy is the mainstay for chronic polycythemia: 300 to 500 mL every other day at first, eventually every few months.
  • Goal: hematocrit below 42 in women, below 45 in men.
  • Hydration (the blood is so viscous).
  • Anticoagulants (clot risk); mobility matters but clot risk is high.
  • Antihistamines (diphenhydramine) for serious itching.
  • Ongoing evaluation and support.

Central Venous Catheters

Why a central line

  • TPN: very hypertonic (10 to 20% dextrose instead of 5%); peripheral veins can't handle it.
  • Chemotherapy: often vesicant (serious tissue damage if it infiltrates) and long-term.
  • Long-term antibiotics (for example, weeks for endocarditis).
  • Loss of peripheral access.

Complications of central lines

  • Bleeding at the site, air embolus, pneumothorax.
  • CLABSI (central line-associated bloodstream infection): a huge share of hospital-acquired infections; can cause sepsis and death.
  • CHG-impregnated dressings help prevent CLABSI; a StatLock keeps the catheter from moving (wiggling enlarges the insertion hole).

Removing a non-tunneled CVC or PICC (RN)

  • Check the INR first (especially on anticoagulants).
  • Patient supine or Trendelenburg (PICC: lying down, hand below heart level).
  • Valsalva (bear down or hum) during removal so air isn't sucked into the vessel.
  • PICC: withdraw slowly, inch by inch (too fast can spasm the vein).
  • Pressure 3 to 5 minutes; occlusive dressing.
  • Measure the catheter against the charted length so nothing is left in.
Nursing students at UK HealthCare may not provide direct CVC care (CLABSI bundle): no accessing or flushing ports, no pushing through a central line, no hanging primary fluids, no dressing changes. The one exception: hanging a secondary medication with the instructor or RN when the primary line is already established.

Blood Products

Blood Types

Compatibility

  • Two antigen sets: ABO (A, B, both, or neither) and Rh (have it or don't).
  • You can only receive blood with antigens you already have; a foreign antigen causes a hemolytic reaction.
  • O negative = universal donor (no A, B, or Rh). AB positive = universal recipient.
  • Examples: B positive can receive O negative, O positive, B negative, B positive. A negative can receive only O negative and A negative.
  • You don't have to memorize the chart, but you must understand it well enough to answer, for example, a select-all-that-apply on which types an AB negative patient can receive. (Most common types, O positive and A positive, are not on the test.)

Transfusion Reactions

Suspect a reaction? Stop the transfusion first. Then hang a new bag and tubing of normal saline at the same IV site (don't just switch to the saline on the blood tubing; that tubing is full of blood). Keep the line open, assess and take vital signs, notify the provider and the blood bank, monitor urine output, save the bag and tubing for the blood bank, collect specimens, complete the transfusion reaction report, and document.
ReactionSignsWhat to DoPrevention
Acute hemolytic (the most life-threatening)Wrong (incompatible) blood. Immediate: facial flushing, fever, headache, low back pain (kidneys), hematuria, dyspnea, dropping BP, cardiac arrest.Support the BP (it is shock; colloids such as albumin); blood samples; urine for hematuria; Foley to monitor output; send unit, tubing, and filter to the lab.Meticulous bedside verification; it is preventable.
Febrile non-hemolytic (the vast majority of fevers)A gradual temperature rise (not immediate); no back pain, no hematuria, no hemodynamic compromise.Notify the provider; probably acetaminophen and restart.Leukocyte-reduced products; acetaminophen beforehand if they've reacted before.
Mild allergic (the most common type)Itching, flushing, urticaria (hives). Sensitization to donor WBCs.Stop, notify; diphenhydramine or a steroid; usually restart slowly.Antihistamines and steroids beforehand; washed RBCs.
Severe allergic (anaphylactic)Anxiety, urticaria, bronchospasm, respiratory and cardiovascular compromise, low BP, possible cardiac arrest.Treat like a code: CPR, oxygen, epinephrine, antihistamines, steroids, maybe a beta-2 agonist.Extensively washed RBCs; autologous components if possible.
Bacterial sepsisRapid chills and high fever, vomiting, diarrhea, marked hypotension.Stop and return the blood; cultures; notify; antibiotics, IV fluids, vasopressors (likely ICU).Infuse within 4 hours; aseptic technique.
TACO (transfusion-associated circulatory overload)Often an older patient with heart failure, blood given too fast: lung congestion, crackles at the bases, dyspnea, increased respirations, headache, distended neck veins.The only one where you stop or slow the infusion; call the provider (furosemide, oxygen, maybe morphine); chest X-ray.Know who is at risk; stretch the unit over about 3.5 to 4 hours.

Leukocyte-reduced products

  • Leukocytes collected with whole blood can cause febrile non-hemolytic and allergic reactions.
  • Pre-storage leuko-reduction lowers that risk; most blood at UK is leukocyte-reduced.

Autologous donation vs autotransfusion

  • Autologous donation: donating your own blood before a planned surgery. Less common now (expensive, often wasted).
  • Autotransfusion: collecting blood lost during surgery (for example, open-heart) and giving it back. No reaction risk; it's their blood.
