Types of Fractures

TypeWhat It Is
TransverseStraight across.
SpiralThe bone gets twisted.
GreenstickSplinters like a chicken bone bent in half; often in young people whose bones aren't fully developed.
ComminutedThe bone is shattered into fragments ("not good").
ObliqueAn angled break.
PathologicHappens when it normally wouldn't, because of an underlying pathology such as bone cancer or osteoporosis (a patient with severe osteoporosis fractured ribs from coughing with pneumonia).
StressFrom repetitive stress, for example a foot fracture in an avid runner.

Where the break is determines the treatment. A pelvic fracture can't be casted (a pelvic wrap until surgery, then an internal device); a clavicle fracture is just immobilized to heal.

A wired-shut jaw (mandibular fracture): airway trumps a healing mandible. If the patient vomits they could aspirate, so they are often sent home with a pair of pliers (wire cutters) just in case. They can use their gut (sucking through a straw).

Emergency Care and the Three Goals

Emergency care

  • Airway, breathing, circulation; assess for a head injury.
  • Splint the fracture as quickly as possible, in normal body alignment, and immobilize it.
  • Elevate the body part if possible (venous return, less swelling).
  • Cold pack: vasoconstriction decreases the (often severe) swelling.

Reduce, immobilize, restore function

  • Reduce: put the bone fragments back in their anatomic position.
  • Closed reduction (preferred): non-surgical, manual realignment without an incision, often in the ED, possibly with local anesthesia.
  • Open reduction: through an incision, often coupled with internal fixation (nails, screws, plates): ORIF.
  • Immobilize afterward so the approximated bones can heal (not a quick process), with external or internal devices.

Peripheral Neurovascular Assessment

For anyone with a bone break (casted or not) and anyone with an internal device, assess distal to the break. "Very vigilant."

The 5 P's: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis. Pain is the first sign: increasing pain despite pain medication (anything trending up is significant). By the time there's no pulse, pallor or paralysis, "you've missed the boat."

External Devices

Casts

What they are

  • Circumferential (all the way around the limb), temporary immobilization; common after a closed reduction. Arm, leg, body (body spica) casts.
  • Fiberglass today, but standardized exams still ask about plaster of Paris: it's heated, handled with wet palms (no finger imprints; it takes a while to set), and the edges are petaled (turned outward so they don't dig into the skin).
  • Until set: don't cover it (ventilation), and reposition every 1 to 2 hours.

After it goes on

  • Neurovascular checks distal to the cast every hour for 24 hours: swelling can increase and the hard cast won't expand.
  • Rule of thumb: a finger or two should fit inside.
  • Ice for the first 24 to 36 hours (vasoconstriction, less swelling).
  • Complications: infection, circulation problems, peripheral nerve damage, complications of immobility, and pressure areas over bony prominences (medial malleolus, olecranon process, tibial tuberosity): listen to complaints of pain and discomfort.

Splints, Immobilizers and Traction

Splints and immobilizers (a Thomas leg splint, braces; some can dial in the allowed flexion) are removable, unlike a cast. Traction applies a pulling force on the fractured extremity, and it is continuous unless the provider orders otherwise.

FeatureSkin TractionSkeletal Traction
HowWrapped onto the skinA pin transects the bone; the traction hooks to it
WeightOnly 5 to 10 lb (the skin doesn't tolerate more)More weight (for example 35 lb for a femur)

Buck's traction

  • The simplest form: a straight pull on the affected extremity: running traction (one direction), so the patient constantly slides down to the foot of the bed.
  • Temporary: for muscle spasms, or to immobilize before surgery (buys time for the swelling to go down before an ORIF the next day).

Russell's traction

  • Still skin traction, but it pulls both ways: counter traction.
  • Lets the patient move a little in bed and permits knee flexion; helps relieve muscle spasms and back pain. Also temporary.

Balanced suspension traction (Russell's or skeletal) is preferred: counter-traction pulls in opposite directions, so the patient doesn't slide down; moving the bed or patient doesn't change the pull; more mobility and autonomy. A trapeze bar lets patients lift themselves and pull up in bed.

Caring for a patient in traction: maintain constant traction with no interruption in the weights; the weights always hang free, off the floor; and never let anything interrupt the line of pull.

External Fixators and Pin Care

  • An external fixator ("ex-fix") is used with an open reduction but without suspended skeletal traction. It looks horrific, but the patient is not confined to bed (can get in a wheelchair, though not necessarily bearing weight). Examples: arm, jaw, halo, hand, leg.
  • Ex-fix and skeletal traction both break the skin, so infection is the concern; infection in the bone is a real problem.
  • Assess pin sites: the pin shouldn't wiggle; clear serous drainage is OK; purulent drainage likely means infection.
  • Pin care: typically half-strength peroxide with a sterile swab. No ointments or anything gooey (attracts bacteria). Teach the patient not to touch it.

Internal Devices and Nursing Care After a Fracture

Pins, screws and plates mean the patient had surgery: an open reduction with internal fixation (ORIF). The neurovascular assessment applies again.

Neurovascular dysfunction and pain

  • Elevate the extremity above the heart for the first day or two if not contraindicated; ice compresses.
  • Notify the provider immediately if pain increases and is unrelieved by medication; teach the signs of neurovascular dysfunction.
  • Fracture pain is ongoing and predictable, so give around-the-clock dosing: be proactive, talk about the pain regimen at the start of the shift (many patients don't ask). Non-drug measures supplement, not replace, medication for severe pain.

Infection, skin and mobility

  • Watch pin sites; strict aseptic technique; culture anything suspicious; antibiotics per order; watch temperature and WBC.
  • Petal plaster edges. Nothing inside the cast (no coat hangers to scratch, even when healing itches). Report warmth, worsening pain or odor; keep ventilation; turn if allowed (friction, shearing).
  • Pain control before range of motion; active ROM preferred, passive if they can't, or passive-assistive; PT.
  • Know whether the patient is weight bearing or non-weight bearing (check, double check, triple check the mobility orders); teach assistive devices.

Compartment Syndrome and Fasciotomy

There are about 26 compartments in a leg. Swelling inside a compartment has nowhere to go because the surrounding fascia (the body's internal "cling wrap") won't budge, and it can cause vascular compromise very quickly: miss it on the morning shift and the patient could lose the limb by night.

Compartment syndrome is an emergency. The classic cue is severe pain not fixed with opioids. The procedure that relieves it is a fasciotomy (opening the fascia); once the swelling goes down, it is closed.

Fat Embolism Syndrome

  • Long bone fractures (the femur, the humerus) put patients at risk for clots, including a fat embolism: a fat globule released from the fracture.
  • Prompt surgical stabilization of the fracture reduces its occurrence.
  • Red flags: rising respiratory rate, cyanosis, anxiety, tachycardia, falling O2 sat, chest pain.
  • Neurologic features often precede the pulmonary ones by 6 to 12 hours: LOC changes, confusion, apprehension.
  • A petechial rash (chest, eyes); fluffy shadows on the chest x-ray.
  • Treatment is mainly supportive: oxygen, fluids.

Amputations

Terms and candidates

  • Most often the lower extremity: BKA (below the knee), AKA (above the knee), disarticulation (right at the joint).
  • A residual limb matters for a prosthetic: a BKA keeps the knee joint, a big plus over an AKA.
  • Candidates: arterial insufficiency / peripheral vascular disease (often years of uncontrolled diabetes, older patients); peripheral neuropathy (an unfelt foot sore becomes infected); trauma; thermal injuries; osteomyelitis; tumors; congenital limb disorders.

Types

  • Open (guillotine): the wound bed is left open, for an infected area that must become aseptic before the edges are approximated. Closed is the alternative.
  • Elective (planned, time for anticipatory grieving) vs traumatic (life-saving; the patient may wake up without a limb). Both are very difficult to process.

Complications and Nursing Care

ConcernWhat the Nurse Does
Hemorrhage (the first concern)Monitor vital signs closely: a rising heart rate compensates for lost stroke volume (CO = HR x SV). Check the dressing, and slide a gloved hand under the limb: blood pools underneath by gravity where you can't see it.
InfectionPrevent it as usual.
Phantom limb painNot everyone has it, but when they do it is very real: pain in a limb that's no longer there. Treated with neuropathic analgesics and mirror therapy; usually self-limiting, lasting about a year.
Hip flexion contracture (especially AKA)The residual limb tends to float up in the air, flexing the hip; a contracture keeps the patient from standing. Good alignment, stretching and extension; often prone 30 minutes, 4 times a day (forces hip extension). Elevating the residual limb on a pillow flexes the hip: some providers don't allow it, especially early (provider preference).
PainAround-the-clock dosing for ongoing predictable pain, plus treating phantom pain.
Shaping the limbA compression bandage shapes and molds the limb for eventual prosthetic fitting and promotes shrinkage and maturation: worn at all times except PT and bathing; a figure-eight wrap prevents restricting blood flow; remove and reapply as often as needed to keep the compression.
PsychosocialWhatever the story, it can be devastating. A certified prosthetist or orthotist helps with the prosthesis. "Residual limb" sounds more professional and less blunt than "stump" (you will still hear stump in the hospital).
