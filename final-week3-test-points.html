Intro & Sinus Dysrhythmias

Know for the exam

  • ★ Yes, you need to know it: each tiny box is 0.04 seconds; five boxes are 0.20.
  • ★ A test rhythm strip is always 6 seconds: count the QRSs and multiply by 10.
  • ★ The key-features slide is "very important": signs of decreased cardiac output (hypotension, low urine output, delayed capillary refill) come back with every rhythm.
  • PR interval 0.12 to 0.20; QRS skinny (0.10 or less); SA node fires 60 to 100.
  • Something changes on the monitor: go check the patient first (it may be artifact or a lead).
  • Symptomatic bradycardia: atropine 1 mg IV every 3 to 5 minutes (3 mg max), then transcutaneous pacing. Asymptomatic: monitor, don't treat.

Not Tested

  • How to read a 12-lead EKG.
  • The particulars of S3 and S4 gallops (just normal vs extra sounds).

PAC, A-Fib, A-Flutter & PSVT

Know for the exam

  • ★ In A-fib and A-flutter, rate control is the priority, then the rhythm, then preventing clots.
  • New or increasing PACs: check magnesium, calcium and potassium and tell the provider (they can lead to A-fib).
  • A-fib: no P waves, irregularly irregular, the most common dysrhythmia; the danger is a clot to the brain (stroke). A-flutter: sawtooth F waves.
  • A-fib over 48 hours: anticoagulate 4 to 6 weeks and do a TEE before cardioversion.
  • Cardioversion needs an R wave and the sync on; clear the bed.
  • PSVT: vagal maneuvers, then adenosine: fast push, fast flush, warn about the pause. Adenosine slows; atropine speeds up.

PVC, V-Tach & V-Fib

Know for the exam

  • PVCs are early, wide and ugly; increasing PVCs are reported (V-tach risk). Three in a row is V-tach.
  • Pulse or no pulse decides everything. V-fib has zero cardiac output.
  • V-fib and pulseless V-tach: call a code, CPR, defibrillate (200 up to 360 joules) with the sync off. Cardioversion is 50 to 200 with the sync on.
  • "You can live with A-fib. You can't live with V-fib."

HF, PAD, AAA & Raynaud's

Know for the exam

  • ★ NCLEX and HESI tease out left vs right heart failure: crackles and PND are left-sided; JVD, ascites and edema are right-sided.
  • ★ Digoxin: take an apical pulse for a full minute and hold it below 60 ("commit to memory"); Digibind is the antidote; watch potassium.
  • BNP is to heart failure as troponin is to MI; the higher the BNP, the worse the failure.
  • Acute decompensated HF: high Fowler's with the legs dangling, daily weights, diuretics.
  • ★ The PAD vs venous disease comparison slides hold "lots of potential test questions." PAD: weak or absent pulses, no hair, pale when raised.
  • ★ "You do need to know how to do" the ABI: normal 0.9 to 1.3; below 0.9 points to arterial disease.
  • After a PAD procedure: report loss of pulse, more pain or pallor; no knee flexion.
  • AAA under 4 cm: watchful waiting. After repair: BP adequate but not too high, hourly urine output, distal perfusion.
  • Raynaud's: warmth, avoid triggers, calcium channel blocker.

Not Tested

  • The specific EKG changes in heart failure.
  • BNP normal values.
  • Digoxin therapeutic levels (0.5 to 2).

CVI & VTE

Know for the exam

  • CVI: edema, brown leathery skin, painful ulcers above the medial malleolus; elevate and compress (elevate for veins, not arteries).
  • VTE: venous stasis, endothelial damage and hypercoagulability; unilateral swelling, warmth, tenderness.
  • Prevent it: mobilize early and use the skeletal muscle pump; no SCDs once a VTE is present.
  • Anticoagulation is very important but means bleeding risk. The most serious complication is a pulmonary embolism.
