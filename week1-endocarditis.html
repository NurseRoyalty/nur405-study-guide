Infective Endocarditis

With pericarditis think inflammation; with endocarditis think infection. "Infective endocarditis" is the longer name, but it is usually just called endocarditis.

What It Is

The endocardium and the valves

  • The endocardium is the inside of the heart, which is a cue to where the problem is.
  • The valves are a very movable part of the heart, and a lot of the manifestations of endocarditis relate to the valves.
  • Endocarditis occurs when blood flow within the heart lets the organism infect previously damaged valves or another endothelial surface.

Subacute vs Acute

Subacute

  • Affects those with pre-existing heart problems.
  • Typically milder in presentation.
  • Can be more chronic.

Acute

  • Affects healthy valves.
  • Manifests itself quicker.
  • Therapy needs to be prompt and vigorous.

Who Gets It & What Causes It

Two things have to happen

  • A history of damaged epithelium, such as a valve replacement or a defect.
  • A port of entry for infection to reach the endocardium: a sore, a dental procedure, IV drug use.

Causative organisms

  • Most common: Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus viridans (bacterial).
  • Other possible pathogens: fungi and viruses, so not all endocarditis is bacterial.

Vegetations & Emboli

Why vegetations matter

  • Infection on the moving valves develops vegetations, for example mitral valve vegetation between the left atrium and left ventricle. Bacterial endocarditis of the mitral valve covers it with large, irregular vegetations.
  • The valves are constantly opening and closing, so vegetations can slough off and become emboli, dispersed into the vascular system.

Left-sided vegetation

  • Leaves the heart into the organs.
  • Can cause a limb infarction or an infarction in any organ of the body.

Right-sided vegetation

  • Blood from the right side goes to the lungs.
  • Like a pulmonary embolus.

Manifestations

Very non-specific at first

  • Timing: the transcript says it typically occurs "within 10 two weeks" of the infectious organism (unclear wording in the transcript, so no firm number is given here).
  • Low-grade fever, chills, malaise, fatigue, anorexia, joint pain, muscle pain. If you didn't know better, you'd think it was the flu.
  • Clubbing of the fingers is the odd one out, and it comes later in the disease process, not early.

Clinical Manifestations

ManifestationWhat the Lecture Says
New-onset murmurA murmur is about the opening and closing of the valve. You should hear S1 and S2 crisply; damaged valves produce a murmur, an adventitious heart sound graded from 1 to 6.
Heart failureThe most common complication of infective endocarditis. Damaged valves leak blood back instead of letting it go one direction, the blood backs up, and the patient develops left- and right-sided heart failure.

EchocardiogramEvidence of endocardial enlargement.

Prophylactic Antibiotics

Who gets prophylaxis

  • A mechanical heart valve.
  • A history of endocarditis.
  • A history of congenital heart disease.
  • A heart transplant.

Before which procedures

  • Any dental visit.
  • A biopsy of any kind.
  • Tonsil and adenoid removal.
  • A wound infection or urinary tract infection.

Treatment & Nursing Care

Treatment

  • Typically hospitalized initially; medications usually given IV.
  • Complete eradication may take weeks upon weeks; relapses are common.
  • Home care may include going home with a PICC line for IV antibiotics.

Nursing care and teaching

  • Identify and educate high-risk individuals so they can avoid infections.
  • Good rest; good oral hygiene (the mouth is where bacteria can enter).
  • Let the provider know before any dental procedure.
  • Refer IV drug users to drug rehab.
  • Monitor for the non-specific signs and symptoms that indicate a recurrence.
