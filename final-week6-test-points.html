Neuro Labs & Dx

Know for the exam

  • CT contrast: allergies, IV, kidney status. MRI: no metal. PET shows physiology (metabolism) rather than anatomy.
  • Carotid duplex: increased velocity means stenosis (stroke risk).
  • EEG: hold stimulants 8 hours; painless. The epilepsy monitoring unit stops seizure meds on purpose to record a seizure.
  • EMG for ALS and MG; repetitive nerve stimulation and single fiber EMG for MG; evoked potentials for MS.
  • Lumbar puncture: consent, empty the bladder, fetal position; follow orders on lying flat; a spinal headache is treated with bed rest and analgesics, a blood patch, IV caffeine.

Parkinson's, MG & MS

Know for the exam

  • Parkinson's: not enough dopamine; rest (pill-rolling) tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia (most disabling), later postural instability.
  • Levodopa is the drug of choice; the on-off phenomenon: shorter intervals, controlled release, avoid high-protein meals.
  • PD care: extra time, meds on time, prevent aspiration and falls; freezing tips (step over an imaginary line, rock, lift the toes).
  • ★ MG takeaway: grave muscle weakness in the head area (ptosis, diplopia, chewing and swallowing). You may not hear about it again until the HESI or NCLEX.
  • ★ Pyridostigmine bromide is "the only one that I would commit to memory"; time it to peak at meals.
  • Myasthenic crisis (too little medication) gets better with Tensilon; cholinergic crisis (too much) gets worse.
  • ★ Lhermitte's sign is a specific cue of MS (a student reported it on the med-surg HESI).
  • MS: intention tremor, spasticity, visual changes, heat intolerance; avoid heat, rest, regular aerobic exercise.

Not Tested

  • MS drug therapy (not tested in 405).

ALS, Seizures & GBS

Know for the exam

  • ALS: asymmetric limb weakness, fasciculations, cognition intact; riluzole extends survival; NPO once aspiration risk begins; still assess pain.
  • Seizure first aid: airway and suction, nothing in the mouth, don't restrain, stay, turn the head if possible; let the postictal patient rest.
  • Status epilepticus: over 5 minutes or repeated over 30; airway first; lorazepam or diazepam.
  • ★ NCLEX and HESI "love to talk about phenytoin": narrow therapeutic index (drug levels), and the side effect gingival hyperplasia.
  • Never stop antiepileptics abruptly; non-adherence drives many ER seizure visits.
  • GBS: ascending, symmetric weakness, absent DTRs, dysautonomia; 15 to 30% need a ventilator: watch the gag reflex and breathing.
  • GBS treatment: plasmapheresis or IVIG (equal efficacy) within 4 weeks of onset; 95% survive.

Not Tested

  • State driving laws after seizures.

Stroke & Neuro IR

Know for the exam

  • 87% of strokes are ischemic: thrombotic (most common, atherosclerosis) or embolic (heart, A-fib). High BP is the most common cause of hemorrhagic stroke.
  • A TIA is to a stroke as angina is to an MI: a warning; one third progress to stroke.
  • tPA within 3 hours (up to 4.5 under criteria); get the time of onset; blood on the CT means no tPA.
  • Deficits are on the side opposite the stroke; receptive vs expressive aphasia; hemianopsia (approach from the seeing side).
  • NPO until the speech therapist's swallow evaluation; no pulling on the affected arm; no more than 30 minutes on the affected side; speak slowly, don't shout.
  • Watch for worsening in the first 24 to 48 hours; avoid catheters (bladder retraining).
  • ★ The NIH Stroke Scale is "the most important" of the scales: know what it is; nurses often catch worsening through it. Check the access site after NIR.
  • Thunderclap "worst headache of my life" means subarachnoid hemorrhage (often an aneurysm).
  • Thrombectomy (stent retriever) for large vessels, up to 24 hours; full-dose tPA doesn't rule it out.

Not Tested

  • Which side of the brain causes which particular deficits.
  • NIH Stroke Scale scoring details.
  • Intracranial pressure (taught in 427).
