Cancer, Liver, Gallbladder & Pancreas

Cancer: Prevention, Diagnosis & Treatment

Prevention and early detection

  • Avoid carcinogens: excess alcohol, poor diet, tobacco, too much sun.
  • Diet, exercise, rest; regular exams and screenings; teach self-exams (breast, testicular).

Diagnosis

  • History; cytology (Pap, sputum); tissue biopsy; labs; chest X-ray; mammogram.
  • Tumor markers (PSA, CEA): made by tumor cells; used for diagnosis or progression.
  • Bone marrow exam for cancers that start in the marrow (leukemia, lymphoma).
CAUTION, the seven warning signs: Change in bowel or bladder habits · A sore that does not heal · Unusual bleeding or discharge · Thickening or lump in the breast or elsewhere · Indigestion or difficulty swallowing · Obvious change in a wart or mole · Nagging cough or hoarseness.

Goals: cure; if not, control; if not, palliative care. Surgery (about half of patients): prophylactic (double mastectomy for a high-risk gene), biopsy, cure, control, debulking (remove part of a tumor for comfort or to help chemo and radiation; not curative), palliation, second look, reconstruction.

Chemotherapy

Giving chemo

  • The mainstay of cancer therapy: solid tumors and blood cancers. Systemic.
  • Must be chemo certified; very toxic; protect patient and nurse; two-RN double check.
  • Central venous catheters preferred (not required).
  • Premedicate for nausea and vomiting (and anticipatory nausea and vomiting); anti-inflammatory and anti-allergy meds.
  • Check WBC, platelets, H&H first: hold chemo if WBCs or platelets are too low.

Nadir

  • The point after chemo when counts are at their lowest; they then regenerate.
  • Chemo every two weeks: about day 7, 8, 9 the patient feels worst; counts should recover by the next visit, or treatment is delayed.
  • Teach: you won't feel well then; limit exposure to other people.

Central venous catheters and ports covered again here — review in Week 2: Hematology if needed

Extravasation (a vesicant infiltrating tissue) can cause necrosis and limb loss. Prevent: watch the site closely, a patent, free-flowing IV with good blood return. Pain is the cardinal symptom, but not always. Blood return lost? Stop the chemo. If it happens: stop the infusion immediately, attach a syringe and aspirate gently, remove the cannula, elevate the arm, give the antidote per protocol (pharmacy helps).

Radiation

About 60% of patients with cancer get radiation. Local, precisely targeted (chemo is systemic); alone or with chemo or surgery.

Sealed implant precautions

  • Time, distance, shielding. Wear your own dosimeter (tracks exposure; no protection; don't share).
  • Private room, sign on the door.
  • No pregnant women, no children under 16. Visitors 30 minutes a day, 6 feet from the source.
  • Dislodged source: forceps into the lead container in the room, never hands.
  • Save dressings and linens until the source is removed.

Skin teaching (radiated area)

  • Wash daily with mild soap and water, using the hand, not a washcloth; rinse well.
  • Don't wash off skin markings.
  • Pat dry. No powders, lotions or creams unless the radiologist prescribes them.
  • Soft, loose clothing; no direct sun or temperature extremes.
  • Dry vs wet desquamation (moist, incredibly tender).

Managing Cancer Treatment Side Effects

Chemo kills rapidly dividing cells: bone marrow, GI tract, hair. Radiation effects depend on where it's aimed (bowel: diarrhea; neck: swallowing and taste).

Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC)Meaning
Greater than 2,500Where we like it.
Greater than 1,000Low but pretty safe.
Less than 1,000Neutropenic.
Less than 500Protective precautions.

In cancer care, neutrophils are an actual count (segs plus bands), not a differential percentage. Neutrophils answer acute bacterial infection, so neutropenia = infection risk; filgrastim (a WBC growth factor) may be given.

Neutropenic precautions

  • Isolation technique, screen visitors, no fresh flowers or plants, monitor for infection.
  • Diet: avoid raw fruits and vegetables that can't be peeled (berries); peelable ones are OK (oranges, bananas, apples, pineapple); wrapped food and flatware; no raw peanuts or seeds.

Platelets and anemia

  • Platelets normal 150,000 to 400,000. Serious bleeding may not show until below 50,000; transfuse usually below 20,000.
  • Anemia: fatigue. Monitor H&H; energy conservation but stay active; iron-rich diet. Epoetin if hemoglobin is less than 11; PRBCs if severe.

GI effects

  • Anorexia, taste changes (focus on liked foods), nausea and vomiting.
  • Weigh twice weekly; albumin and prealbumin; small, frequent, high-protein, high-calorie meals; supplements.
  • Diarrhea is often from localized radiation.

Stomatitis

  • Inflamed, ulcerated mouth (mucositis is the general term); extremely painful.
  • Thorough oral assessment; meticulous, gentle brushing and flossing; saline gargles; numbing swish-and-spit mixtures.
  • No alcohol mouthwash. Artificial saliva; soft, non-irritating foods.
  • Other effects: fatigue, hair loss (unpredictable; may come back thicker and curly), lung and heart damage, infertility in young people.
  • Support the right to complementary treatments while encouraging the prescribed plan; a calm, present, healing environment.
  • Psychosocial: hair or body-part loss, fear of recurrence ("body vigilant"), secondary cancers, disability and death. Hope is positive reappraisal; spirituality can help or hurt; social support is one of the most important things.

Hepatitis

Often no symptoms: 30% of hepatitis B and as much as 80% of hepatitis C. Acute phase lasts 1 to 4 months: vague malaise, flu-like symptoms (myalgia, arthralgia), nausea and vomiting, RUQ discomfort, hepatomegaly, jaundice (icteric), itching (bile salts), dark "coke-colored" urine. Then a convalescent phase. Non-viral causes include too much acetaminophen.

Jaundice

  • Yellow sclera, skin or hard palate; always too much bilirubin; usually present when bilirubin is greater than 2.5. A symptom, not a disease.
  • Hemolytic: RBC breakdown (wrong blood; check urine for hematuria).
  • Obstructive: intrahepatic or extrahepatic (a gallstone in the common bile duct). Bilirubin can't reach the gut: clay-colored stools and dark urine.

Care and teaching

  • No specific treatment: rest, managed at home.
  • No special diet: well balanced, adequate calories; low fat if fat isn't tolerated; B complex and vitamin K.
  • Itching: diphenhydramine.
  • Stop alcohol; avoid hepatotoxic drugs: acetaminophen and isoniazid.
  • Hygiene; treated or bottled water abroad; don't share linens, utensils, glasses, needles, razors, nail clippers, toothbrushes; condoms; cover cuts. Hep C: never donate blood, organs or tissue. Notify contacts.

Cirrhosis

Early (insidious): weight loss, weakness, GI upset, bowel changes, nausea and vomiting. Late: jaundice, low albumin, prolonged PT, and everything driven by portal hypertension.

Late FindingWhy
AscitesPortal hypertension plus low albumin (lost "pulling power"): fluid third-spaces into the peritoneum.
Bleeding riskLack of prothrombin: longer bleeding times.
SplenomegalyCongestion from portal hypertension (spleen is LUQ; liver mostly RUQ).
Spider angiomas, caput medusaeEngorged vessels from portal hypertension.

Hepatic encephalopathyToo much serum ammonia (worth memorizing): not converted to urea; crosses the blood-brain barrier; sedative. Reversible. Confusion, slowed responses, personality change, ataxia, up to hepatic coma.
Asterixis ("liver flap")Wrists flexed, hands flap involuntarily: elevated ammonia.

Managing ascites and fluid

  • Rest first.
  • Fluid and sodium restriction (water follows salt).
  • Diuretics: spironolactone (potassium sparing) and furosemide (potassium losing), often both.
  • Albumin to raise oncotic pressure.
  • Abdominal girth with skin markings, but daily weight is more accurate.

Paracentesis and TIPS

  • Paracentesis when ascites impairs breathing. Consent, baseline vitals, have the patient void (avoid puncturing a full bladder), supine or high Fowler's. Often repeated in cirrhosis.
  • TIPS (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt): a non-surgical stent that lowers portal hypertension, controlling ascites and variceal bleeding.

Nutrition

  • High calorie, high carbohydrate, moderate to low fat.
  • Restrict protein only with severe encephalopathy; otherwise protein's benefits outweigh the ammonia risk.
  • Low sodium with fluid excess and ascites.

Bleeding Precautions

Assess

  • Coags; oral cavity (gums bleed first); ecchymosis, purpura, petechiae.
  • Fall prevention: a fall risks head injury and subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Avoid

  • Aspirin; alcohol, spicy and bulky foods; injections.
  • Vigorous nose blowing and straining at stool (raises pressure and can rupture varices): stool softeners.
  • Hard toothbrushes, rectal temps, enemas. Hold pressure longer after an IM shot.
Bleeding esophageal varices are an emergency. Endoscopic sclerotherapy (injected sclerosing agent) or variceal banding (rubber bands around the base). For an active bleed: balloon tamponade (Blakemore tube) applies direct pressure; the patient goes to the ICU.

Hepatic Encephalopathy Care

  • Goal: get the ammonia down.
  • Control GI bleeding: blood in the gut is nitrogen-rich and raises ammonia.
  • Avoid constipation. Lactulose titrated to about 2 to 4 stools a day to remove ammonia. Teach why, even if they've already had a BM today.
  • Monitor level of consciousness and GCS (EMV); safety precautions for confusion.

Cholelithiasis & Cholecystitis

Cholelithiasis = gallstones in the gallbladder; cholecystitis = inflamed gallbladder (acute or chronic). Risks: middle-aged female, fair skin, overweight, high-fat diet, oral contraceptives.

Manifestations

  • Episodic, vague upper abdominal pain radiating to the right shoulder, triggered by high-fat or large meals.
  • Nausea, vomiting, indigestion, belching, gas; fever with inflammation.
  • Jaundice, clay-colored stools, dark urine, steatorrhea: most common with chronic cholecystitis (a stone blocking the common bile duct).

Treatment

  • Non-surgical: avoid fatty foods; NPO during a flare; opioids, antiemetics, antispasmodics for biliary pain.
  • Rarely lithotripsy; uncommonly ERCP with sphincterotomy.
  • Most common: laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

After a lap chole

  • Remove the puncture-site bandages the day after surgery; may shower.
  • Call for redness, swelling, purulent or bile-colored drainage, increasing pain, nausea, vomiting, fever, chills.
  • Gradually increase activity; back to work in about a week.
  • Low-fat diet tolerated better for the first few weeks; then a regular diet.

Open cholecystectomy and T-tube

  • Subcostal incision healing by primary intention.
  • A T-tube keeps the common bile duct patent while inflammation goes down.
  • Rarely goes home with it: teach emptying the bile bag.

Pancreatitis

Acute: premature activation of pancreatic enzymes that autodigest the pancreas. Gallstones and alcohol are the two most common causes. CT is the best imaging for pancreatitis and its complications.

Complications

  • Pseudocyst: cavity of necrotic products outside the pancreas; often resolves; worst case it perforates into the peritoneum (severe peritonitis).
  • Abscess: within the pancreas; often needs surgical drainage.

Acute care

  • Pain relief first (can cause hemodynamic compromise): IV opioids (morphine); knees bent / fetal position.
  • Labile vitals: monitor often; fluid balance; aggressive IV hydration.
  • NPO with NG to low wall suction during a flare to rest the pancreas.
  • Monitor stools for steatorrhea and blood sugar for hyperglycemia (endocrine function).
  • Quit alcohol. Diet after NPO: carbohydrates encouraged (less stimulating), fat restricted.

Chronic Pancreatitis

  • Progressive, destructive inflammation and fibrosis with remissions and flares. Intense pain (less tenderness than acute); a mass is likely a pseudocyst or abscess; ascites with possible respiratory compromise; steatorrhea (oily, floating, greasy, foul); dark urine.
  • Opioids given more cautiously (some can increase pain through spasms).
  • Weight loss can be significant: 4,000 to 6,000 calories a day PO, so TPN may be considered.
  • Prevent flares: avoid alcohol, caffeine, nicotine, spices; bland, low-fat, high-protein, high-carbohydrate, small high-calorie meals; rest after eating.

Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT): pancrelipase

  • Standard of care to prevent malnutrition, malabsorption and weight loss. Contains amylase, lipase and protease.
  • Take with the first bite of every meal or snack. Sometimes ordered with an antacid or H2 blocker (pH).
  • Swallow without chewing; wipe the lips. Powder in applesauce or juice; not mixed with protein foods. Don't crush enteric-coated forms.
  • Record the number and consistency of stools: goal is fewer, less fatty stools.
