Respiratory Labs & Diagnostics

The NUR 334 instructor: every system starts with a labs-and-diagnostics voiceover, you are held accountable for the related nursing care (pre- and post-procedure) of each one, and you should be able to explain each test to a patient. ABGs are saved for block two.

Pulse Oximetry

O2 sat

  • The amount of hemoglobin that is carrying oxygen.
  • Normal: 95% or greater (voiceover); 95 to 100 (live lecture). Less than 90% can indicate hypoxemia (live lecture).
  • A low sat could mean hypoventilation, atelectasis, a pneumothorax, or other lung issues.
  • Not invasive; intermittent (with vital signs, Q4) or continuous (fresh post-op). Commonly used to titrate oxygen.
  • Best on a finger without nail polish (or fungus); the earlobe or toe is sometimes used, but a finger probe belongs on a finger.
  • On oxygen, we don't love 100%: wean it (for example from 4 L to 2 L nasal cannula). The exception is hyperoxygenating before trach suctioning. 100% on room air is fine.
The bus analogy: the red blood cell is the bus, hemoglobin is the seats (iron is what the seats are made of), and oxygen is the passengers. The O2 sat is how full the buses are; the hematocrit is how much of the highway is made up of buses.

Sputum Studies

The instructor says to commit these three to memory.

Sputum TestWhat It Looks For
Culture and sensitivity (C&S)Bacteria. The culture identifies the pathogen; the sensitivity shows which antibiotic works. Results can take 48 to 72 hours.
CytologyCancer cells (malignancy or abnormal changes); ordered when lung cancer is suspected.
Acid-fast bacillus (AFB)Active tuberculosis; the definitive diagnosis of TB.

Collecting sputum

  • From deep in the lungs, not saliva: have the patient cough (expectorate), don't let them just spit in the jar.
  • Sterile container; to the lab as soon as possible (not sitting on the bedside table all day).
  • The morning specimen is best: secretions have settled overnight and the patient hasn't eaten or drunk.
  • Suction the patient if they can't cough productively.

TB Testing

Skin test (Mantoux / PPD) and IGRA

  • The TB skin test is intradermal (needle at a 10 to 15 degree angle) and should raise a bleb. Read in 48 to 72 hours.
  • The IGRA (interferon gamma release assay; QuantiFERON-TB Gold) is a blood test; no return visit to read it. Neither test is better than the other.
  • Both are screening tests: they do not tell latent from active TB. Positive screen, then chest X-ray; positive chest X-ray, then sputum for AFB.

Bronchoscopy

What it does

  • Scoping (directly visualizing) the bronchi and airways.
  • Obtain a biopsy; remove secretions the patient can't expectorate (lavage); remove a foreign body; diagnose infection, tumors, bleeding.

Nursing care

  • Signed consent (invasive). Oropharynx anesthetized; sedation (conscious sedation or general).
  • NPO before: 8 to 12 hours.
  • NPO until the gag reflex returns.
  • Blood-tinged mucus is expected: document and continue to monitor; no need to call the provider.
  • After a biopsy, watch for bleeding (an unexplained rising heart rate) or a pneumothorax (abrupt shortness of air).

Thoracentesis

Removing pleural fluid

  • The provider puts a large-bore needle into the pleural space for a pleural effusion (fluid where it doesn't belong; third spacing that reduces surface area for oxygen). "-centesis" removes fluid or gas (paracentesis is the abdominal version).
  • Can be diagnostic (analyze the fluid), therapeutic (relieve dyspnea), or both.
  • Before: consent; sit upright with elbows on the overbed table (opens the intercostal spaces); stay very still and don't talk.
  • After: a chest X-ray to rule out a pneumothorax; assess for hypoxia. Risks: pneumothorax, bleeding, infection.

Pulmonary Function Tests & Peak Flow

PFTs

  • Measure lung function with respect to time (seconds); usually done by respiratory therapy, nose pinched, mouth sealed on the device.
  • Total lung capacity: inhale as deep as you can and exhale as deep as you can.
  • FEV1 (forced expiratory volume in 1 second): a deep breath in, then push it out as fast as you can. It is lower in obstructive pulmonary disease, where getting air out is the problem.

Peak flow meter

  • More of an asthma device (peds content), carried by the patient to check their own FEV1.
  • They track their personal best; a drop from it may signal an early asthma attack.

Upper Respiratory Problems

Epistaxis (Nosebleed)

Causes

  • Irritation (dry air), trauma, infection, a foreign body (kids), tumors.
  • Hypertension; blood dyscrasias; anticoagulants; chemotherapy.
  • With a spontaneous nosebleed, ask about anticoagulants: they affect the cause and how fast it stops. Antidotes: protamine sulfate for IV heparin, vitamin K for warfarin.

Anterior (most common)

  • Upright, leaning forward (leaning back sends blood down the throat: aspiration risk). Keep calm.
  • Pinch the soft tissue just below the bony prominence; pinching the bone won't stop it. Breathe through the mouth.
  • Lateral pressure with ice to vasoconstrict; nasal tampon or packing if needed.
  • Discourage nose blowing.

Posterior (less common, more serious)

  • An emergency; usually needs hospitalization (and often a specialist). Hard to stop because you can't pinch it.
  • Balloon catheter (tamponade) or posterior packing; can impede breathing.
  • Humidification, oxygenation, bed rest, pain control, oral care.
  • Teaching: saline spray, humidification, avoid aspirin and NSAIDs, avoid strenuous activity.
Live-lecture priority: for a patient with nasal packing, the priority assessment is the oxygen saturation: packing can obstruct the airway. Pain and dressing drainage matter, but think ABCs first.

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)

What and who

  • Repetitive collapse of the upper airway during sleep (the mandible drops back and blocks it). Apnea = breathing stops; hypopnea = it slows considerably.
  • Primary risk factors: increased age, male, obesity (large neck circumference), and nasopharyngeal structural abnormalities. Smoking is a less established risk factor.
  • Signs: waking tired, loud snoring and noises (often reported by the partner), morning headaches, daytime sleepiness; witnessed apnea on the hospital monitor.
  • Complications: motor vehicle crashes, neuropsychiatric dysfunction (lost REM sleep), pulmonary pressure, hypertension, heart failure, metabolic syndrome.
  • Gold standard diagnosis: polysomnography (a sleep study).

Treatment

  • Weight reduction, exercise; avoid alcohol and smoking; sleep on the side, not the back; sleep hygiene (routine, off the phone and TV).
  • Oral appliances (keep the mandible pulled up) are preferred over CPAP for mild to moderate cases.
  • Surgery: tissue removal, jaw repositioning, or (in serious cases) a trach.
  • The big problem is adherence: an estimated 20 to 40% don't use their CPAP. If a CPAP patient isn't rested, ask whether they are actually using it.

Trach Review

Parts

  • Flange (faceplate on the outside), outer cannula, inner cannula (inside the outer cannula; removed for cleaning), and the obturator (only for reinserting a trach that came out).
  • Shiley (white plastic): has a cuff; disposable inner cannula you throw away at trach care; every fresh post-op trach starts as a Shiley.
  • Jackson (metal): no cuff; the inner cannula is cleaned with a brush; used for long-term or permanent trachs.
  • The cuff and pilot balloon go together: inflated pilot balloon = inflated cuff. The cuff is inflated when the patient is on a ventilator (so all air goes from the vent to the patient) and typically deflated off the vent. A speaking valve requires a deflated cuff.
  • An oral ET tube is used only about a week to 10 days before a trach is placed (FYI). Trachs can be temporary (downsized, then removed) or permanent.
  • Trach suctioning and care are fair game on the exam.
The trach comes out: the obturator is always taped at the bedside, with a spare trach and a bag-valve device with a trach adapter. Take out the inner cannula, put the obturator in the outer cannula, slightly extend the neck, guide it into the stoma (back and down), then remove the obturator immediately (left in, it obstructs the airway) and reinsert the inner cannula. Confirm with lung sounds, call for help, check sat, suction as needed, and secure it.

Preventing thick secretions (live-lecture question)

  • The question asks about preventing thickening: changing the inner cannula treats it but doesn't prevent it; low Fowler's is too low for the airway; vital signs don't prevent it. Read what the question is actually asking before you look at the options.

Head & Neck Cancer

Who and why it is devastating

  • Tobacco causes 85%; more men; usually over 50. Under 50, often linked to HPV.
  • Symptoms (hoarseness, dysphagia, a neck mass) come late; very few early symptoms means high mortality. Most have locally advanced disease at diagnosis.
  • Surgery is first-line (then radiation and chemo): vocal cord stripping, laryngectomy (removal of the voice box), tracheostomy, lymph node removal, neck dissection.
  • Huge impact on body image (and it's the airway).

After a laryngectomy

  • A permanent tracheostomy; the natural voice is lost.
  • Air no longer enters through the nose or mouth, only the stoma; sputum comes out the stoma.
  • The patient can eventually eat and drink (the esophagus is intact), but has tube feeding at first (no swallowing for about 10 days to 2 weeks while swelling settles).
Code on a laryngectomy patient: do not bag the mouth and nose. Bag the stoma, using a pediatric face mask that fits over it.
Speech OptionKey Points
Artificial larynx (electrolarynx)Probably the most common; held against the throat to vibrate. Usable right after surgery, easy, well understood (sounds a bit robotic).
Tracheoesophageal voice restorationA valve placed in the area lets the patient talk.
Esophageal speechThe oldest; hands-free with no device, but hard to learn: air is sucked into the esophagus and "burped" out to form words.

Post-op care (including radical neck dissection)

  • Airway is number one; trach collar with O2 and humidification.
  • Cough regularly; suction (Yankauer and deep trach suctioning). A mucus plug can cause a code.
  • Blood-tinged sputum can be expected for the first couple of days.
  • Pulmonary toilet, stoma care, pain control (enough for good pulmonary toilet); Jackson-Pratt drain.
  • Often malnourished before surgery: tube feedings early. Physical and speech therapy; psychosocial support.

Pneumonia

An acute infection of the lung parenchyma (the functional unit, where the alveoli exchange O2 and CO2). Can be bacterial, viral, or fungal.

Risk factors

  • Older age; altered level of consciousness (weak cough, aspiration risk); can't expectorate secretions.
  • Immobility or bed rest; debilitating illness; malnutrition; smoking.
  • Tracheal intubation (on a ventilator in the ICU).
  • Picture it: an older man from a nursing home after a stroke, low albumin, a smoker.

Classifications

  • CAP (community-acquired): not hospitalized or living in long-term care within 14 days of symptoms.
  • HAP (hospital-acquired): begins 48 hours or more after admission, not present on admission; non-intubated patients.
  • VAP (ventilator-associated): on a ventilator in the ICU.
TypeKey Points
ViralThe most common; may be mild and self-limiting (usually resolves in 3 to 4 days) but can be life-threatening. Cough is scanty or non-productive.
BacterialUsually more serious; sometimes hospitalized. Cough is typically productive, possibly purulent.
AspirationMaterial from the mouth or stomach enters the trachea and lungs. Risk: decreased LOC, dysphagia (thin liquids), NG and feeding tubes (Dobhoff tubes are placed post-pyloric). Can be silent (no vomiting or coughing).
OpportunisticImmunocompromised patients.

Manifestations and diagnosis

  • Often follows a simple respiratory infection (a "post-influenza pneumonia"), then fever, chills, cough, malaise, painful deep breaths and coughing, dyspnea, fatigue, myalgia; crackles.
  • In the elderly, a new onset of confusion can be one of the first signs of any infection.
  • Severe cases: respiratory distress and failure (patients with comorbidities tolerate it worst).
  • Chest X-ray: infiltrates (haziness where it should be clear).
  • CBC with diff: leukocytosis (high WBC). An acute bacterial infection may show a shift to the left: more neutrophils, including immature neutrophils released early.
  • Sputum C&S identifies the bacteria and the antibiotic; a viral infection grows no bacteria (and gets no antibiotics).
Don't mix these up (the instructor says exam options will test it): leukocytosis = increased WBCs; leukopenia = decreased WBCs.

Prevention

  • Pneumococcal vaccine for those at risk or of age; flu vaccine.
  • Don't smoke; balanced diet.
  • In the hospital: know who is at risk; incentive spirometer, turn-cough-deep breathe, ambulate.
  • Standard precautions; hand hygiene.

Care

  • Trend vital signs and pulse ox; listen to lungs at the start of the shift for a baseline.
  • Oxygen as needed to keep the sat above 95%.
  • Pulmonary toilet; the incentive spirometer (make sure they use it and document it). Chest physiotherapy.
  • Increase fluids to thin secretions (unless contraindicated, such as severe heart failure).
  • Ambulation balanced with energy conservation (cluster care).
  • Antibiotics if bacterial; analgesics; antipyretics.

Live-lecture question: what to do for a pneumonia patient

  • Appropriate: acetaminophen for fever (a higher temperature raises the cells' oxygen demand), notify the provider, check urine output (hydration), and encourage fluids.
  • Not appropriate: complete bed rest (it worsens pneumonia).

COPD

Obstructive pulmonary disease is an umbrella term for asthma, chronic bronchitis, and emphysema. Asthma is taught in peds, not med surg. COPD usually means emphysema and/or chronic bronchitis. It is preventable and treatable.

Emphysema

  • Alveolar damage: alveoli overinflated, stretched, destroyed.
  • Impaired gas exchange at the parenchyma.
  • Barrel chest, dyspnea, but not the daily sputum of bronchitis.

Chronic bronchitis

  • Inflammation with excessive mucus in the bronchi.
  • Coughing something up every single day.

Findings

  • The problem is getting air out, not in. Diagnosis: history, symptoms, spirometry.
  • Barrel chest: AP diameter closer to 1:1 instead of the normal 2:1 (on X-ray: widened AP diameter, flattened diaphragm).
  • Decreased or diminished breath sounds; wheezes, rhonchi, crackles (especially in an exacerbation); prolonged expiration.
  • Advanced disease: pursed-lip breathing (creates positive pressure to push air out; most patients already do it), neck vein distension and peripheral edema (from pulmonary hypertension backing things up), and cachexia (they burn so many calories breathing).
  • Nursing problems: activity intolerance (sat may drop talking or eating), ineffective breathing pattern (usually too fast), ineffective airway clearance, impaired gas exchange, anxiety, poor nutrition.

Nursing implications

  • Smoking cessation: the single most effective and cost-effective intervention (Kentucky has many smokers).
  • Flu and other vaccines; catch respiratory infections early.
  • Inhaler therapy is the mainstay of self-management; adherence is critical (non-adherent patients end up with exacerbations). Long-acting beta agonists and long-acting muscarinic antagonists bronchodilate; inhaled steroids reduce inflammation.
  • Home or hospital O2 with a lower sat target than the average patient.
  • Tripod position and pursed-lip breathing (they usually already do both); huff coughing; energy conservation and relaxation; high calorie needs; psychosocial support.

COPD Exacerbation

When they come into the hospital

  • A flare-up: worsening dyspnea, cough, or sputum beyond their usual day-to-day variation, needing a change in treatment. The most common cause is a respiratory infection (often after an upper respiratory infection).
  • Mainstay: oxygen for hypoxemia. Sat goal: 88 to 92%. 99 to 100% is too high.
  • Non-invasive positive pressure (BiPAP) when hypercapnic (rising CO2).
  • Bronchodilators (MDI at home; nebulizer in the hospital); chest physiotherapy; antibiotics if bacterial.
CO2 narcosis, and never withhold oxygen. A long-term CO2 retainer's drive to breathe is low oxygen (ours is high CO2). Too much oxygen (for example 6 L with a sat of 99%) can take that drive away. Only an ABG proves someone is a CO2 retainer. Even so, oxygen should never be withheld from a hypoxemic patient; just don't push them to high sats.

Tuberculosis

Caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Resistant to isoniazid (INH) and rifampin = multi-drug resistant TB (MDR-TB). Risk: poor, underserved, and minority populations; IV drug users; poor sanitation; crowded living; immunosuppression.

ClassificationWhat HappensContagious?
PrimaryInhaled; the immune response encapsulates the organism. Usually asymptomatic.No

Reading the TB Skin Test

Measure induration (the raised area), not redness. Redness that isn't raised is not positive. The instructor wants you familiar with these cut-offs.

Positive AtFor Whom
15 mm or moreHealthy people with no risk factors.
10 mm or moreImmigrants, drug users, nursing home residents, young children.
5 mm or moreThe immunosuppressed: HIV, recent contact with TB, organ transplant.

After a positive screen

  • Positive skin test or IGRA, then a chest X-ray. Clear chest X-ray (no Ghon nodule) = latent TB. Nodule present = more testing (sputum for AFB) for active TB.

TB Treatment & Care

Drug therapy

  • The mainstay; months (6 or more) to years. Strict adherence is essential. Learn it as "isoniazid," not just INH.
  • Directly observed therapy (DOT): supervised dosing to increase adherence and decrease MDR-TB, and to assess adverse effects.
DrugNotable Side Effect
Isoniazid (INH)Hepatotoxicity (with acetaminophen, one of the two hepatotoxic drugs to know in med surg); peripheral neuropathy.
RifampinRed-orange secretions (bright red urine that isn't hematuria).
EthambutolVisual acuity changes; trouble telling colors apart.

In the hospital

  • Airborne precautions in a negative pressure room (air pushed outside, not to other rooms).
  • A fitted HEPA mask (fit testing).

At home

  • Preventive drugs for high-risk contacts (a spouse, for example); test contacts.
  • Cover mouth and nose when coughing; mask in crowds.
  • Sputum for AFB every couple of weeks: three negative cultures typically means no longer infectious.

Lung Cancer

Presentation and diagnosis

  • One of the most prevalent cancers in Kentucky. About 10% are found incidentally on an X-ray for something else.
  • Few early symptoms; often masked by a smoker's chronic cough. Most common symptom: a persistent cough (productive); also hemoptysis, weight loss, chest pain.
  • Chest X-ray: a mass (consolidation) or infiltrate (haziness). CT or MRI for metastasis.
  • Sputum cytology: only 20 to 30 of 100 patients with lung cancer test positive.
  • The definitive diagnosis is a biopsy: fine needle aspirate, bronchoscopy, or VATS (provider preference).

Care

  • Problems: airway clearance, ineffective breathing, anxiety, malnutrition, fatigue (space activities, allow rest), knowledge deficit.
  • Smoking cessation; anxiety reduction and rapport.
  • Surgery: pneumonectomy (whole lung), lobectomy, or wedge resection (surgeon preference); plus radiation and/or chemo.
  • After a pneumonectomy, no breath sounds on that side (sounds near midline may be referred).
