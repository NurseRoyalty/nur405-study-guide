Respiratory Labs & Diagnostics

The NUR 334 instructor: every system starts with a labs-and-diagnostics voiceover, you are held accountable for the related nursing care (pre- and post-procedure) of each one, and you should be able to explain each test to a patient. ABGs are saved for block two.

Pulse Oximetry

O2 sat

  • The amount of hemoglobin that is carrying oxygen.
  • Normal: 95% or greater (voiceover); 95 to 100 (live lecture). Less than 90% can indicate hypoxemia (live lecture).
  • A low sat could mean hypoventilation, atelectasis, a pneumothorax, or other lung issues.
  • Not invasive; intermittent (with vital signs, Q4) or continuous (fresh post-op). Commonly used to titrate oxygen.
  • Best on a finger without nail polish (or fungus); the earlobe or toe is sometimes used, but a finger probe belongs on a finger.
  • On oxygen, we don't love 100%: wean it (for example from 4 L to 2 L nasal cannula). The exception is hyperoxygenating before trach suctioning. 100% on room air is fine.
The bus analogy: the red blood cell is the bus, hemoglobin is the seats (iron is what the seats are made of), and oxygen is the passengers. The O2 sat is how full the buses are; the hematocrit is how much of the highway is made up of buses.

  • From deep in the lungs, not saliva: have the patient cough (expectorate), don't let them just spit in the jar.

TB Testing

Skin test (Mantoux / PPD) and IGRA

  • The TB skin test is intradermal (needle at a 10 to 15 degree angle) and should raise a bleb. Read in 48 to 72 hours.
  • The IGRA (interferon gamma release assay; QuantiFERON-TB Gold) is a blood test; no return visit to read it. Neither test is better than the other.
  • Both are screening tests: they do not tell latent from active TB. Positive screen, then chest X-ray; positive chest X-ray, then sputum for AFB.

Bronchoscopy

What it does

  • Scoping (directly visualizing) the bronchi and airways.
  • Obtain a biopsy; remove secretions the patient can't expectorate (lavage); remove a foreign body; diagnose infection, tumors, bleeding.

Nursing care

  • Signed consent (invasive). Oropharynx anesthetized; sedation (conscious sedation or general).
  • NPO before: 8 to 12 hours.
  • NPO until the gag reflex returns.
  • Blood-tinged mucus is expected: document and continue to monitor; no need to call the provider.
  • After a biopsy, watch for bleeding (an unexplained rising heart rate) or a pneumothorax (abrupt shortness of air).

Thoracentesis

Removing pleural fluid

  • The provider puts a large-bore needle into the pleural space for a pleural effusion (fluid where it doesn't belong; third spacing that reduces surface area for oxygen). "-centesis" removes fluid or gas (paracentesis is the abdominal version).
  • Can be diagnostic (analyze the fluid), therapeutic (relieve dyspnea), or both.
  • Before: consent; sit upright with elbows on the overbed table (opens the intercostal spaces); stay very still and don't talk.
  • After: a chest X-ray to rule out a pneumothorax; assess for hypoxia. Risks: pneumothorax, bleeding, infection.

Pulmonary Function Tests & Peak Flow

PFTs

  • Measure lung function with respect to time (seconds); usually done by respiratory therapy, nose pinched, mouth sealed on the device.
  • Total lung capacity: inhale as deep as you can and exhale as deep as you can.
  • FEV1 (forced expiratory volume in 1 second): a deep breath in, then push it out as fast as you can. It is lower in obstructive pulmonary disease, where getting air out is the problem.

Peak flow meter

  • More of an asthma device (peds content), carried by the patient to check their own FEV1.
  • They track their personal best; a drop from it may signal an early asthma attack.

Upper Respiratory Problems

Epistaxis (Nosebleed)

Causes

  • Irritation (dry air), trauma, infection, a foreign body (kids), tumors.
  • Hypertension; blood dyscrasias; anticoagulants; chemotherapy.
  • With a spontaneous nosebleed, ask about anticoagulants: they affect the cause and how fast it stops. Antidotes: protamine sulfate for IV heparin, vitamin K for warfarin.

Anterior (most common)

  • Upright, leaning forward (leaning back sends blood down the throat: aspiration risk). Keep calm.
  • Pinch the soft tissue just below the bony prominence; pinching the bone won't stop it. Breathe through the mouth.
  • Lateral pressure with ice to vasoconstrict; nasal tampon or packing if needed.
  • Discourage nose blowing.

Posterior (less common, more serious)

  • An emergency; usually needs hospitalization (and often a specialist). Hard to stop because you can't pinch it.
  • Balloon catheter (tamponade) or posterior packing; can impede breathing.
  • Humidification, oxygenation, bed rest, pain control, oral care.
  • Teaching: saline spray, humidification, avoid aspirin and NSAIDs, avoid strenuous activity.
Live-lecture priority: for a patient with nasal packing, the priority assessment is the oxygen saturation: packing can obstruct the airway. Pain and dressing drainage matter, but think ABCs first.

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)

What and who

  • Repetitive collapse of the upper airway during sleep (the mandible drops back and blocks it). Apnea = breathing stops; hypopnea = it slows considerably.
  • Primary risk factors: increased age, male, obesity (large neck circumference), and nasopharyngeal structural abnormalities. Smoking is a less established risk factor.
  • Signs: waking tired, loud snoring and noises (often reported by the partner), morning headaches, daytime sleepiness; witnessed apnea on the hospital monitor.
  • Complications: motor vehicle crashes, neuropsychiatric dysfunction (lost REM sleep), pulmonary pressure, hypertension, heart failure, metabolic syndrome.
  • Gold standard diagnosis: polysomnography (a sleep study).

Treatment

  • Weight reduction, exercise; avoid alcohol and smoking; sleep on the side, not the back; sleep hygiene (routine, off the phone and TV).
  • Oral appliances (keep the mandible pulled up) are preferred over CPAP for mild to moderate cases.
  • Surgery: tissue removal, jaw repositioning, or (in serious cases) a trach.
  • The big problem is adherence: an estimated 20 to 40% don't use their CPAP. If a CPAP patient isn't rested, ask whether they are actually using it.

Trach Review

Parts

  • Flange (faceplate on the outside), outer cannula, inner cannula (inside the outer cannula; removed for cleaning), and the obturator (only for reinserting a trach that came out).
  • Shiley (white plastic): has a cuff; disposable inner cannula you throw away at trach care; every fresh post-op trach starts as a Shiley.
  • Jackson (metal): no cuff; the inner cannula is cleaned with a brush; used for long-term or permanent trachs.
  • The cuff and pilot balloon go together: inflated pilot balloon = inflated cuff. The cuff is inflated when the patient is on a ventilator (so all air goes from the vent to the patient) and typically deflated off the vent. A speaking valve requires a deflated cuff.
  • An oral ET tube is used only about a week to 10 days before a trach is placed (FYI). Trachs can be temporary (downsized, then removed) or permanent.
  • Trach suctioning and care are fair game on the exam.
The trach comes out: the obturator is always taped at the bedside, with a spare trach and a bag-valve device with a trach adapter. Take out the inner cannula, put the obturator in the outer cannula, slightly extend the neck, guide it into the stoma (back and down), then remove the obturator immediately (left in, it obstructs the airway) and reinsert the inner cannula. Confirm with lung sounds, call for help, check sat, suction as needed, and secure it.

Preventing thick secretions (live-lecture question)

  • The question asks about preventing thickening: changing the inner cannula treats it but doesn't prevent it; low Fowler's is too low for the airway; vital signs don't prevent it. Read what the question is actually asking before you look at the options.

Head & Neck Cancer

Who and why it is devastating

  • Tobacco causes 85%; more men; usually over 50. Under 50, often linked to HPV.
  • Symptoms (hoarseness, dysphagia, a neck mass) come late; very few early symptoms means high mortality. Most have locally advanced disease at diagnosis.
  • Surgery is first-line (then radiation and chemo): vocal cord stripping, laryngectomy (removal of the voice box), tracheostomy, lymph node removal, neck dissection.
  • Huge impact on body image (and it's the airway).

After a laryngectomy

  • A permanent tracheostomy; the natural voice is lost.
  • Air no longer enters through the nose or mouth, only the stoma; sputum comes out the stoma.
  • The patient can eventually eat and drink (the esophagus is intact), but has tube feeding at first (no swallowing for about 10 days to 2 weeks while swelling settles).
Code on a laryngectomy patient: do not bag the mouth and nose. Bag the stoma, using a pediatric face mask that fits over it.
Speech OptionKey Points
Artificial larynx (electrolarynx)Probably the most common; held against the throat to vibrate. Usable right after surgery, easy, well understood (sounds a bit robotic).
Tracheoesophageal voice restorationA valve placed in the area lets the patient talk.
Esophageal speechThe oldest; hands-free with no device, but hard to learn: air is sucked into the esophagus and "burped" out to form words.

Post-op care (including radical neck dissection)

  • Airway is number one; trach collar with O2 and humidification.
  • Cough regularly; suction (Yankauer and deep trach suctioning). A mucus plug can cause a code.
  • Blood-tinged sputum can be expected for the first couple of days.
  • Pulmonary toilet, stoma care, pain control (enough for good pulmonary toilet); Jackson-Pratt drain.
  • Often malnourished before surgery: tube feedings early. Physical and speech therapy; psychosocial support.

Pneumonia

An acute infection of the lung parenchyma (the functional unit, where the alveoli exchange O2 and CO2). Can be bacterial, viral, or fungal.

Risk factors

  • Older age; altered level of consciousness (weak cough, aspiration risk); can't expectorate secretions.
  • Immobility or bed rest; debilitating illness; malnutrition; smoking.
  • Tracheal intubation (on a ventilator in the ICU).
  • Picture it: an older man from a nursing home after a stroke, low albumin, a smoker.

Classifications

  • CAP (community-acquired): not hospitalized or living in long-term care within 14 days of symptoms.
  • HAP (hospital-acquired): begins 48 hours or more after admission, not present on admission; non-intubated patients.
  • VAP (ventilator-associated): on a ventilator in the ICU.
TypeKey Points
ViralThe most common; may be mild and self-limiting (usually resolves in 3 to 4 days) but can be life-threatening. Cough is scanty or non-productive.
BacterialUsually more serious; sometimes hospitalized. Cough is typically productive, possibly purulent.
AspirationMaterial from the mouth or stomach enters the trachea and lungs. Risk: decreased LOC, dysphagia (thin liquids), NG and feeding tubes (Dobhoff tubes are placed post-pyloric). Can be silent (no vomiting or coughing).
OpportunisticImmunocompromised patients.

Manifestations and diagnosis

  • Often follows a simple respiratory infection (a "post-influenza pneumonia"), then fever, chills, cough, malaise, painful deep breaths and coughing, dyspnea, fatigue, myalgia; crackles.
  • In the elderly, a new onset of confusion can be one of the first signs of any infection.
  • Severe cases: respiratory distress and failure (patients with comorbidities tolerate it worst).
  • Chest X-ray: infiltrates (haziness where it should be clear).
  • CBC with diff: leukocytosis (high WBC). An acute bacterial infection may show a shift to the left: more neutrophils, including immature neutrophils released early.
  • Sputum C&S identifies the bacteria and the antibiotic; a viral infection grows no bacteria (and gets no antibiotics).
Don't mix these up (the instructor says exam options will test it): leukocytosis = increased WBCs; leukopenia = decreased WBCs.

Prevention

  • Pneumococcal vaccine for those at risk or of age; flu vaccine.
  • Don't smoke; balanced diet.
  • In the hospital: know who is at risk; incentive spirometer, turn-cough-deep breathe, ambulate.
  • Standard precautions; hand hygiene.

Care

  • Trend vital signs and pulse ox; listen to lungs at the start of the shift for a baseline.
  • Oxygen as needed to keep the sat above 95%.
  • Pulmonary toilet; the incentive spirometer (make sure they use it and document it). Chest physiotherapy.
  • Increase fluids to thin secretions (unless contraindicated, such as severe heart failure).
  • Ambulation balanced with energy conservation (cluster care).
  • Antibiotics if bacterial; analgesics; antipyretics.

Live-lecture question: what to do for a pneumonia patient

  • Appropriate: acetaminophen for fever (a higher temperature raises the cells' oxygen demand), notify the provider, check urine output (hydration), and encourage fluids.
  • Not appropriate: complete bed rest (it worsens pneumonia).

COPD

Obstructive pulmonary disease is an umbrella term for asthma, chronic bronchitis, and emphysema. Asthma is taught in peds, not med surg. COPD usually means emphysema and/or chronic bronchitis. It is preventable and treatable.

Emphysema

  • Alveolar damage: alveoli overinflated, stretched, destroyed.
  • Impaired gas exchange at the parenchyma.
  • Barrel chest, dyspnea, but not the daily sputum of bronchitis.

Chronic bronchitis

  • Inflammation with excessive mucus in the bronchi.
  • Coughing something up every single day.

Findings

  • The problem is getting air out, not in. Diagnosis: history, symptoms, spirometry.
  • Barrel chest: AP diameter closer to 1:1 instead of the normal 2:1 (on X-ray: widened AP diameter, flattened diaphragm).
  • Decreased or diminished breath sounds; wheezes, rhonchi, crackles (especially in an exacerbation); prolonged expiration.
  • Advanced disease: pursed-lip breathing (creates positive pressure to push air out; most patients already do it), neck vein distension and peripheral edema (from pulmonary hypertension backing things up), and cachexia (they burn so many calories breathing).
  • Nursing problems: activity intolerance (sat may drop talking or eating), ineffective breathing pattern (usually too fast), ineffective airway clearance, impaired gas exchange, anxiety, poor nutrition.

Nursing implications

  • Smoking cessation: the single most effective and cost-effective intervention (Kentucky has many smokers).
  • Flu and other vaccines; catch respiratory infections early.
  • Inhaler therapy is the mainstay of self-management; adherence is critical (non-adherent patients end up with exacerbations). Long-acting beta agonists and long-acting muscarinic antagonists bronchodilate; inhaled steroids reduce inflammation.
  • Home or hospital O2 with a lower sat target than the average patient.
  • Tripod position and pursed-lip breathing (they usually already do both); huff coughing; energy conservation and relaxation; high calorie needs; psychosocial support.

COPD Exacerbation

When they come into the hospital

  • A flare-up: worsening dyspnea, cough, or sputum beyond their usual day-to-day variation, needing a change in treatment. The most common cause is a respiratory infection (often after an upper respiratory infection).
  • Mainstay: oxygen for hypoxemia. Sat goal: 88 to 92%. 99 to 100% is too high.
  • Non-invasive positive pressure (BiPAP) when hypercapnic (rising CO2).
  • Bronchodilators (MDI at home; nebulizer in the hospital); chest physiotherapy; antibiotics if bacterial.
CO2 narcosis, and never withhold oxygen. A long-term CO2 retainer's drive to breathe is low oxygen (ours is high CO2). Too much oxygen (for example 6 L with a sat of 99%) can take that drive away. Only an ABG proves someone is a CO2 retainer. Even so, oxygen should never be withheld from a hypoxemic patient; just don't push them to high sats.

Tuberculosis

Caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Resistant to isoniazid (INH) and rifampin = multi-drug resistant TB (MDR-TB). Risk: poor, underserved, and minority populations; IV drug users; poor sanitation; crowded living; immunosuppression.

TB Treatment & Care

Drug therapy

  • The mainstay; months (6 or more) to years. Strict adherence is essential. Learn it as "isoniazid," not just INH.
  • Directly observed therapy (DOT): supervised dosing to increase adherence and decrease MDR-TB, and to assess adverse effects.
DrugNotable Side Effect
Isoniazid (INH)Hepatotoxicity (with acetaminophen, one of the two hepatotoxic drugs to know in med surg); peripheral neuropathy.

In the hospital

  • Airborne precautions in a negative pressure room (air pushed outside, not to other rooms).
  • A fitted HEPA mask (fit testing).

At home

  • Preventive drugs for high-risk contacts (a spouse, for example); test contacts.
  • Cover mouth and nose when coughing; mask in crowds.
  • Sputum for AFB every couple of weeks: three negative cultures typically means no longer infectious.

Lung Cancer

Presentation and diagnosis

  • One of the most prevalent cancers in Kentucky. About 10% are found incidentally on an X-ray for something else.
  • Few early symptoms; often masked by a smoker's chronic cough. Most common symptom: a persistent cough (productive); also hemoptysis, weight loss, chest pain.
  • Chest X-ray: a mass (consolidation) or infiltrate (haziness). CT or MRI for metastasis.
  • Sputum cytology: only 20 to 30 of 100 patients with lung cancer test positive.
  • The definitive diagnosis is a biopsy: fine needle aspirate, bronchoscopy, or VATS (provider preference).

Care

  • Problems: airway clearance, ineffective breathing, anxiety, malnutrition, fatigue (space activities, allow rest), knowledge deficit.
  • Smoking cessation; anxiety reduction and rapport.
  • Surgery: pneumonectomy (whole lung), lobectomy, or wedge resection (surgeon preference); plus radiation and/or chemo.
  • After a pneumonectomy, no breath sounds on that side (sounds near midline may be referred).

Hemoglobin & Hematocrit

Commit these to memory "forever" (the instructor's words): Hemoglobin women 12 to 16, men 14 to 18. Hematocrit women 37 to 47%, men 42 to 52%.

Definitions

  • Hemoglobin: the iron-containing pigment of the RBC.
  • Hematocrit: the percentage of total blood volume made up of RBCs. The hematocrit is roughly three times the hemoglobin (a hemoglobin around 12 goes with a crit of 36).
  • A low crit is anemia (anemia doesn't cause it); the cause might be bleeding, bone marrow failure, dietary deficiency, renal disease. Too many RBCs = polycythemia.
  • Trend, don't read stand-alone values: a post-op H&H of 9.2 and 28.1 that was lower yesterday is trending up; keep an eye on it.

False lows and false highs

  • Fluid retention (heart failure, kidney failure): extra plasma lowers the percentage = a false low hematocrit, not true anemia.
  • Dehydration: less plasma raises the percentage = a false high hematocrit, not true polycythemia.
The bus analogy again: RBC = bus, hemoglobin = seats, iron = what the seats are made of, oxygen = passengers. O2 sat = how full the buses are (we want 95% or higher). Hematocrit = how much of the highway is buses.

  • Melena: dark, tarry, very malodorous, sticky stool from digested blood. Significant.

Anemia

Blood cell terms

  • Leukopenia = low WBCs; neutropenia = low neutrophils (big trouble: the first responders are gone); leukocytosis = too many WBCs (infection).
  • Thrombocytopenia = low platelets (thrombocytes). Erythrocytes = RBCs. "-penia" = low or deficient.
  • Pancytopenia = low RBCs, WBCs, and platelets (aplastic anemia).

Nursing Care of the Anemic Patient

Nursing problems

  • Activity intolerance (probably the most common): fine at rest, but heart rate up, palpitations, anxiety, and short of breath with activity. Fatigue is being tired even lying in bed.
  • Nutrition (not taking in or not absorbing nutrients).
  • Aplastic anemia: risk for bleeding (thrombocytopenia), infection (neutropenia), and fatigue (anemia), all in one patient.

Ongoing care

  • Trend labs; watch for hypoxemia; vital signs and O2 sat; neuro checks (B12).
  • Oxygen for the very anemic patient (fewer buses need all the help they can get).
  • Rest periods.
  • Keep them warm, but no heating pads (paresthesia risk).
  • Possible PRBC transfusion.
  • Treat the cause: nutrition, stop alcohol, supplements, find and stop the bleeding.

Polycythemia

An abnormally high H&H and RBC count. Three classifications.

TypeCause
RelativeDecreased plasma volume or dehydration: the false high hematocrit; not a true increase in RBCs.

Manifestations

  • Thicker, more blood: hypertension, headache, trouble concentrating.
  • Ruddy (dusky red) complexion; possible cyanosis.
  • Clots or hemorrhage: angina, MI, TIA, stroke. The most common serious acute complication is a stroke.
  • Hypermetabolic: night sweats, weight loss.
  • Pruritus (itching) worsened by a hot bath is classic; pain in the fingers and toes.

Collaborative care

  • Phlebotomy is the mainstay for chronic polycythemia: 300 to 500 mL every other day at first, eventually every few months.
  • Goal: hematocrit below 42 in women, below 45 in men.
  • Hydration (the blood is so viscous).
  • Anticoagulants (clot risk); mobility matters but clot risk is high.
  • Antihistamines (diphenhydramine) for serious itching.
  • Ongoing evaluation and support.

Blood Types

Compatibility

  • Two antigen sets: ABO (A, B, both, or neither) and Rh (have it or don't).
  • You can only receive blood with antigens you already have; a foreign antigen causes a hemolytic reaction.
  • O negative = universal donor (no A, B, or Rh). AB positive = universal recipient.
  • Examples: B positive can receive O negative, O positive, B negative, B positive. A negative can receive only O negative and A negative.
  • You don't have to memorize the chart, but you must understand it well enough to answer, for example, a select-all-that-apply on which types an AB negative patient can receive. (Most common types, O positive and A positive, are not on the test.)

Perioperative Overview

PhaseWhat Happens
Pre-opThe anesthesiologist assesses and introduces; care is planned with the patient. The surgical site is marked (preventing wrong-site surgery). Very anxiety-producing for the patient.
Intra-opFrom being wheeled into the OR until transfer to the PACU. Nursing roles: scrub nurse, circulating nurse, RN first assist.
Post-opThe PACU: airway first, recovery from general anesthesia, pain management, close vital signs (patients can be hemodynamically unstable and change quickly). Then back to the ICU, step-down, or med surg unit.

Purposes and settings

  • Purposes: diagnosis, cure, palliation (treating symptoms), prevention, exploration, cosmetic improvement.
  • Ambulatory: home the same day. Same-day admit: surgery in the morning, admitted overnight (usually one night). Inpatient: in the hospital before and after.

Timing

  • Elective: not urgent; improves quality of life (cataracts). Low risk, but not no risk.
  • Semi-elective: more time-sensitive, but not needed within 24 hours (gallstones, fibroid bleeding).
  • Urgent: within 24 hours of diagnosis (hip fracture, an appendix about to rupture).
  • Emergency: can't be delayed, ideally within 2 hours (unstable GI bleed, subdural hematoma).

Pre-op

The Pre-op History

Steps in the interview

  • Review what is documented before the interview and just double-check it (asking the same questions repeatedly is annoying). Can happen in advance (a phone call) or on the day.
  • Confirm understanding of the procedure (teach-back: "tell me about the procedure you're going to have").
  • Previous surgeries and anesthetics; family history of anesthesia problems (malignant hyperthermia is familial).
  • All medications: prescription, over-the-counter, herbals.
  • Allergies and intolerances, including latex (see below).
  • Illicit drug use: not optional to ask, even if sensitive.
  • Tobacco: quitting 6 weeks before surgery is worth it (smoking compromises oxygen binding); quitting any time before is better than not at all.
  • Possibility of pregnancy.
  • End with: "Is there anything else you'd like to share with me?"
Latex allergy clues: allergy to kiwis, bananas, avocados, chestnuts, or a poinsettia plant; daily latex exposure; past reactions to balloons or condoms; a history of allergies and asthma. Latex allergy can be anaphylactic.

Informed Consent

The surgeon's job

  • Full disclosure: diagnosis, purpose, risks (even common non-serious and serious uncommon ones), alternatives, the risk of no treatment, who performs it, short- and long-term costs, hospital stay, pain, recovery, loss of function, scarring.
  • The patient must show comprehension; signing is voluntary.

The nurse's job

  • Witness the signature and act as the patient advocate.
  • You can answer follow-up questions, but if the patient expresses uncertainty about the procedure, explore it, and the next best action is to let the provider know they are having second thoughts.
  • Minors, unconscious, or mentally incompetent patients: a legal representative; an emancipated minor can consent. A true medical emergency can override consent when life is at stake.

Day of Surgery & the Pre-op Checklist

Fasting and checklist

  • No more blanket "NPO after midnight." Typically liquids are held a few hours and food 8 or more hours, but follow the surgeon's orders (don't memorize the table; gut surgery may mean longer NPO).

Malignant Hyperthermia

Malignant hyperthermia: an inherited muscle disorder triggered by certain volatile anesthetics; rare, but high mortality if untreated ("all hands on deck"). The most common initial sign is an unexpected rise in end-tidal CO2 (hypercarbia), not a fever. Also tachycardia, masseter (jaw) muscle spasm or generalized rigidity, muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis), and metabolic acidosis. Hyperthermia comes later. Antidote: dantrolene IV (worth memorizing).

Not predictable

  • Familial tendency, but some patients have no family history.
  • Over 50% had uneventful general anesthetics before, so past uneventful surgeries don't rule it out.

Post-op

PACU basics

  • All orders must be rewritten after surgery; pre-op orders don't carry over. Read every new order.
  • PACU handoff: patient history, intra-op management, vital signs, lab results, estimated blood loss.
  • Initial PACU assessment: airway, breathing, circulation, pain, level of consciousness, voiding, bowel sounds, safety needs.
  • Possible problems: neuro/psych, GI (delayed gastric emptying, paralytic ileus), urinary (trouble voiding, infection with a Foley), respiratory (especially with lung history or smoking), cardiovascular, integument (dehiscence, hematoma, infection), fluids and electrolytes.

Respiratory

Problems

  • Hypoxia from shallow breathing, anesthesia, obesity, respiratory depression (especially first-time narcotics), laryngeal spasm.
  • Atelectasis and pneumonia.

Prevention

  • Pulmonary toilet; head of bed elevated; vital signs and O2 sat; suction if needed.
  • Effective coughing with splinting for abdominal or thoracic incisions (a pillow; CABG patients get a red heart pillow). Coughing after these surgeries is mandatory.
  • Early mobilization.
  • Around-the-clock pain dosing so they can cough and move.

Cardiovascular

Decreased cardiac output and VTE

  • Monitor vital signs (BP is the indirect measure of cardiac output), skin temperature, and color (peripheral perfusion). Trend and compare to baseline.
  • DVT (most often elderly, obese, immobilized) can become a PE; together called VTE. Prevent with leg and ankle exercises (dorsiflex, plantar flex, circles: they mimic walking's muscle pump), SCDs, early ambulation, phlebitis assessment.
  • TED hose must fit correctly (an ill-fitting pair caused a stage 2 pressure ulcer); SCDs are used more now.

Neurologic & Psychological

Emergence delirium and more

  • A restless patient always means think hypoxia first (do they need more oxygen?); also pain, anesthetic agents, the ET tube.
  • Post-op delirium is more common in the elderly. Post-op depression exists (lack of sleep, lack of home support, body image, pain): assess mood and allow time to talk.
  • Assess LOC, orientation, memory, following commands, grips, moving extremities, pupils; always compare to baseline (was that weak left grip there before?).

Pain & Temperature

Pain

  • Post-op pain is continuous and predictable: around-the-clock dosing, not waiting for the patient to ask.
  • Pain increases as anesthesia wears off. Opioids and non-opioids first for severe pain, supplemented by non-drug measures.
  • Watch every patient on opioids for respiratory depression. Document outcomes.

Temperature

  • Often cold after surgery: warm blankets; check the temperature.
  • First 48 hours: a low-grade temp is usually expected, the inflammatory response to surgery; too soon for infection. Above 100.4, think lung congestion or dehydration: pulmonary toilet and fluids.
  • After 48 hours: an elevated temp raises concern for infection (wound, lungs); check the WBC and neutrophils.

GI

Paralytic ileus and more

  • Abdominal discomfort is common; mobilization often helps most.
  • Paralytic ileus is most likely after bowel manipulation. After colon surgery, large intestine motility may take 2 to 7 days (small intestine sooner). Signs: distended, tender belly; absent or high-pitched bowel sounds.
  • No bowel sounds at 24 to 48 hours isn't necessarily an ileus; peristalsis may not have kicked in yet. Watch for bowel sounds and passing gas.
  • Ileus treatment: NG tube to low wall suction, NPO ("don't feed a paralytic ileus").
  • Treat nausea (antiemetic); advance the diet as tolerated (NPO, clear liquids, full liquids); document intake; ambulate; hydrate; monitor bowel sounds every shift; privacy.
  • Opioids cause constipation, and patients never develop tolerance to it: be proactive about stool softeners (ask the provider if none is ordered).

Urinary

Voiding and output

  • Get the Foley out as soon as possible; watch for UTI (in older patients, the first cue is often confusion).
  • Urine output greater than 30 mL/hour (rule of thumb). Urine output reflects renal perfusion: falling BP and cardiac output lower it.
  • Assess the urge to void, palpate the bladder, use the bladder scanner, normal voiding position, running water.
  • No void within 6 to 8 hours after surgery: get an order to catheterize (usually in-and-out).

Integument, Tubes & Drains

Wounds

  • Assess drainage (amount, color, odor, consistency) and erythema; purulent drainage is not expected.
  • Dehiscence: the wound opens. Evisceration: the insides come out. Full care in Week 5: Complications of Wounds
  • At risk: large or obese, older, coughing a lot.
  • Dressings clean, dry, intact; meticulous asepsis; nutrition is paramount (no wound heals without it).

Tubes, drains, catheters to know

  • Indwelling and suprapubic catheters; ostomies; G-tube (gastrostomy) to drainage; Tenckhoff (peritoneal dialysis) catheter; rectal tube/fecal management system; NG to low wall suction.
  • Penrose drain; closed suction drains (Hemovac, Jackson-Pratt); a T-tube; chest tube after pleural/lung surgery.
  • Know what each is for and monitor its output.

Fluid Volume Imbalances

Review from 325

  • Oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure: albumin, the primary colloid, has "pulling power" that keeps fluid in the intravascular space. Low albumin: fluid leaks into the interstitium and can third-space.
  • Hydrostatic pressure: "pushing power." In heart failure, backed-up fluid raises it and pushes fluid into the interstitium.
  • Third spacing: fluid collecting where it serves no purpose (pleural effusion, peritoneum, pericardium). Pulmonary edema = fluid in the lungs; peripheral edema = in the ankles.

The UA

  • First morning urine (most concentrated); analyze within the hour.
  • Should have no bilirubin, glucose, ketones, protein, RBCs, or WBCs. Glucose: think diabetes (glucosuria, DKA). WBCs: suspect UTI. RBCs: hematuria.

  • Red: hematuria, or certain medications.
  • Brown, "Coca-Cola" urine: obstructive jaundice (bilirubin excreted in urine instead of the GI tract).

Urinary Tract Infection

Terms

  • Upper UTI: the kidney parenchyma, pelvis, ureters; more serious, systemic (fever, chills, flank pain / CVA tenderness).
  • Lower UTI: the bladder (cystitis); no systemic signs.
  • Pyelonephritis: inflammation, usually infection, of the renal parenchyma and collecting system.
  • Urosepsis: the infection in the blood; can be life-threatening.
  • CAUTI (catheter-associated UTI): almost a third of healthcare-associated infections. "Catheters are bad": if not truly needed, get them out; the nurse is often the one to ask.

Risk factors

  • Urinary stasis (neurogenic bladder, an obstructing stone, urethral stricture, tumors).
  • Females (shorter urethra); pregnancy.
  • Compromised immunity (aging, diabetes); severe constipation (impedes outflow).
  • Poor hygiene; habitually delaying urination ("nurse's bladder").

Lower UTI symptoms

  • Emptying: hesitancy, intermittency, post-void dribbling, retention, incomplete emptying, dysuria.
  • Storage: frequency, urgency, incontinence, nocturia, nocturnal enuresis.
  • The most common bacterial infection in women; usually gram-negative E. coli ascending the urethra.
  • Older adults often present with confusion, sometimes as the first sign.

Diagnosis

  • Bacteria and pyuria (WBCs) in the urine. A point-of-care dipstick: positive nitrites and leukocyte esterase (an enzyme in WBCs). Confirm with a urinalysis.
  • Clean catch or Foley sample for complicated, hospital-acquired, frequent, or non-responding UTIs.

Prevention teaching

  • Empty the bladder regularly and completely; don't rush.
  • Wipe front to back; drink lots of fluid (water is best).
  • Cranberry: no definitive evidence it improves outcomes (fine if it means they drink more).
  • Sexually active women: urinate before and especially after intercourse.
  • Avoid bladder irritants.

Hospital-acquired UTI prevention and care

  • Remove catheters ASAP or avoid them; aseptic technique.
  • Catheter in the vagina by mistake: leave it there, get a new sterile kit, and catheterize the urethra.
  • Hand washing, gloves, nail care; clean up incontinence quickly; offer regular toileting.
  • Fluids unless contraindicated (heart failure, kidney failure, advanced liver disease).
  • Comfort: warm bath or shower (no bubble baths), local heat.
  • Take the full antibiotic course; should feel better within a couple of days, otherwise follow up (culture and sensitivity).

Pyelonephritis

Presentation and diagnosis

  • An upper UTI involving the parenchyma; more systemic than cystitis.
  • From mild fatigue to a sudden onset of chills, fever, vomiting, and CVA tenderness (flank pain).
  • UTI diagnostics plus a CBC with diff (shift to the left: neutrophils and bands), imaging, blood cultures if it may be in the blood.

Treatment

  • Mild: outpatient; fluids; NSAIDs or antipyretics (acetaminophen); broad-spectrum antibiotic, then narrowed to the culture. Cultures repeated after 14 to 21 days; may be on antibiotics for weeks.
  • Sicker: hospitalized with IV hydration and IV antibiotics, switched to oral once nausea, vomiting, and dehydration improve.

CVA tenderness

  • Costovertebral angle (not cerebrovascular accident): just below the last rib on each side of the back, where the kidneys sit (kidneys aren't palpable).
  • The provider lays one hand there and strikes it with the other fist. With pyelonephritis it is exceedingly tender; the patient will jump. An unexpected finding that points to an upper UTI.

Kidney Stones (Nephrolithiasis)

Diagnosis and types

  • History and physical; non-contrast spiral CT (gold standard), ultrasound, KUB, or IVP.
  • Urinalysis for blood, crystals, pH; analyze any stone retrieved.
  • Five types: calcium oxalate (most common), calcium phosphate, uric acid, cystine, struvite.

Pain

  • One of the strongest pain sensations known: severe visceral pain, "renal colic" (waxes and wanes, spasms); nausea and vomiting that don't improve after vomiting.
  • It can cause hemodynamic compromise, so treating the pain may be the priority (unlike typical post-op pain).
  • Opioids are first-line (sometimes a PCA), plus non-drug measures. Some patients are admitted just for uncontrolled pain.

Treatment and teaching

  • Small stones: pass spontaneously at home, straining all urine to catch the stone. Larger stones: a stent, or removal. Antibiotics if infected; watch for obstruction.
  • Lithotripsy (outpatient): shock waves pulverize the stone. Internal (cystoscope, percutaneous, laser) or external (non-invasive). Afterward hematuria is expected; a stent placed to pass fragments comes out in 1 to 2 weeks; decreased urine output is not expected (obstruction).
  • Daily hydration (water). Forcing fluids once a stone is present hasn't been shown to push it out.
  • Diet by stone type: calcium oxalate = low-oxalate diet (dark roughage, spinach, cocoa, nuts), not a low-calcium diet; uric acid = low-purine diet (gout patients can form uric acid stones).
  • Immobile or sedentary patients are at risk: mobilize, turn, fluids; sit (women) or stand (men) to void regularly for full emptying.

Nephrostomy tube

  • Surgically placed directly into the renal pelvis through the flank when a ureter is totally obstructed; drains to an external bag, bypassing the ureter, bladder, and urethra. Not long-term.
  • Pain or drainage around the site: suspect a blockage. Irrigation requires an order; meticulous aseptic technique (high infection risk); only a tiny amount of sterile saline (it's the renal pelvis, not the bladder).

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

What it is

  • A sudden, rapidly progressive decline in kidney function (hours to days), with or without less urine. A syndrome, from slight deterioration to severe impairment.
  • Decreased GFR (less than 90), decreased urine output, BUN above 20, creatinine elevated.
  • Potentially reversible (CKD is not), but high mortality, especially in the ICU; usually in patients with other comorbidities.
  • Often follows prolonged hypotension, hypovolemia, or a nephrotoxic agent.

Urine output thresholds

  • Less than 30 mL/hour (the instructor's preferred "magic number"), or less than 400 mL in 24 hours: loose definitions of oliguria.
  • Example: 50 mL in 2 hours = notify the provider (after ruling out something you can fix, like a kinked Foley).
  • Waiting 24 hours may let the AKI worsen.

Conservative therapy (not dialysis)

  • Control BP and glucose.
  • Contrast dye: avoid it, or protect the kidneys with acetylcysteine beforehand (also the acetaminophen-overdose antidote) and forced fluids.
  • Avoid nephrotoxic drugs: NSAIDs and aminoglycosides. (Acetaminophen is hard on the liver.)
  • Treat fluid overload (less PO intake, diuretics if the kidneys work), hyperkalemia (anticipate orders near 6), acidosis, mineral and bone disorders, hypertension, anemia, dyslipidemia, malnutrition.

  • Protein restriction before dialysis (BUN and creatinine are protein end products), even though the patient is malnourished: a conundrum. Once on dialysis, protein can increase and restrictions loosen. (You won't be asked to choose between the two.)

How it works

Hemodialysis Access

AV fistula

  • A surgeon anastomoses an artery to a vein, most often in the forearm; permanent.
  • Needs about 6 weeks to mature before use (a temporary access is used meanwhile). If it survives the first 6 months, it can last up to 20 years.
  • Assess every shift: palpate a thrill (a vibration like a cat purring) and auscultate a bruit ("whoosh"); both are expected and confirm patency. A lost bruit = notify the provider.
  • Sign over the bed: no BPs or IV sticks in that arm.
  • Check distal perfusion (cap refill, numbness, tingling, radial and ulnar pulses, motor function, pain), especially right after surgery.

AV graft and temporary catheters

  • AV graft: synthetic loop under the skin; usable sooner; but a foreign body, so infection risk. For small, weak veins or when access is needed within 6 weeks.
  • Tunneled, cuffed IJ catheter (provider-placed; about 1 to 3 weeks), or a femoral catheter (bed rest, minimal leg movement; removed ASAP).
  • A temporary dialysis catheter (the transcript calls it a "u-doll" catheter; often for AKI in the ICU; about 2 weeks): looks like a central line but is not; never run medications or fluids through it.

HIV Labs & Diagnostics

Screening

  • Routine testing for adults and pregnant women: at least once for ages 13 to 75 without risk factors.
  • High risk: test yearly. Men who have sex with men, IV drug users, people who exchange sex for money or drugs, sex partners of people with HIV, and people whose partner's HIV status is unknown.
  • Consent is voluntary. Opt out: a patient can decline, and we document them as opted out.
  • Confidentiality; counseling and referral to care if positive.

The test to remember

  • Fourth generation (HIV antibody AND antigen) is the preferred test. The antigen comes before antibodies, so it can turn positive within 2 to 3 weeks (older antibody-only tests: up to a month or longer).
  • Identifies early infection in up to 80% of patients; can be a rapid test.
  • ELISA tests antibodies only; Western blot is sometimes the second test.
  • HIV RNA (viral load): qualitative (positive or negative) and quantitative (progression, response to treatment).

AIDS = either an opportunistic infection develops or the CD4 drops below 200 (usually around the same time). Opportunistic infections are ones normal people don't get; they appear with immunosuppression.

1. Early / acute infectionRapid replication; not detected on lab tests; no symptoms, but infectious. The patient then seroconverts: antibodies detectable, "the worst flu ever," highly infectious, and most people don't think to get tested.
2. LatencyBody fights effectively; asymptomatic or mild symptoms; virus still producing; steady drop in CD4.
3. AIDSViral load increases, CD4 low; opportunistic diseases around a CD4 of 200.

Lecture conflict, kept as stated: the HIV labs voiceover gives the latency period as 10 to 20 years; the HIV voiceover gives 3 to 12 years.

Assessment

  • AIDS dementia complex: cognitive changes with advanced HIV.
  • Oral manifestations are common as the CD4 drops: do an oral assessment.
  • Infections can be fungal, viral, bacterial or cancerous.
  • Transmission: sexual, parenteral, mother to baby.

Collaborative goals

  • Monitor progression and immune function.
  • Start and monitor antiretroviral therapy (ART).
  • Prevent, detect and treat opportunistic infections.
  • Manage symptoms and ART complications; prevent transmission.
  • Be comfortable with sensitive subjects: often the nurse is the one who brings them up.

Occupational Exposure & PEP

Post-exposure prophylaxis: if drug therapy is indicated, start it within 1 to 2 hours, and definitely within 3 days. Follow-up HIV testing at 6 and 12 weeks, then 6 months.
  • Blood is the highest risk at work; other body fluids can carry HIV. Universal precautions.
  • Needlestick from a person with HIV: a very small risk (heard as "02 percent" in the transcript; the exact figure is garbled).
  • Mucous membranes and non-intact skin also count as exposure. The regimen depends on how severe the exposure was and the source's status; it may be no drug therapy or combination therapy.

ART Adherence & Teaching

Promoting adherence

  • Non-adherence is a real danger: a patient who doesn't feel sick is less likely to take a daily regimen.
  • Patient is motivated before getting the prescription.
  • Social support is a primary priority for a positive outcome.
  • Negotiate the plan (autonomy); a simple plan; anticipate side effects; establish trust.

Teaching the patient with AIDS

  • They are immunosuppressed: avoid crowds and places with poor sanitation, and foods that can carry bacteria.
  • Avoid cleaning pet litter boxes; keep the home clean; no sick visitors.
  • Continue ART; CD4 and viral load every 3 to 4 months.
  • Express empathy; be judgment free.

PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis)

  • Candidates: anal or vaginal sex in the last 6 months and a partner with HIV (especially an unknown or detectable viral load), inconsistent condom use, or an STI diagnosed in the last 6 months; people who inject drugs with an injection partner who has HIV.
  • Daily medication. Used as prescribed it prevents HIV from sex by 99%, and 74% for IV drug users.
  • Does not protect against other STIs: still use condoms. Insurance and Medicaid cover it.

The most diagnosed cancer, partly because skin is visible (early detection). Classified as non-melanoma or melanoma.

Skin Infections, Reactions & Benign Disorders

Bacterial: impetigo and cellulitis

  • Risks: excess moisture, obesity, dermatitis, systemic steroid or antibiotic use, chronic disease such as diabetes. Usually staph and strep.
  • If exudate (drainage) is present, the drainage is infectious. Good hygiene and infection control.
  • Cellulitis: inflammation of subcutaneous tissue; hot, tender, red, diffuse borders; chills, malaise, fever.
  • Treat with moist heat, immobilize, elevate; IV antibiotics if admitted. Untreated: gangrene, possible limb loss.

Viral: harder to treat

  • Herpes simplex, shingles, HPV, warts.
  • Shingles (herpes zoster): reactivated varicella zoster; vesicles; burning pain and neuralgia along a dermatome; incidence rises with age; contagious to people who haven't had the virus.
  • Shingles vaccine: advised for anyone older than 60, one time.
  • HPV: warts anywhere; usually preventable with a vaccine.

Fungal: easy to diagnose

  • Candidiasis: mouth, vagina, skin.
  • Tinea is named by location: corporis = ringworm, cruris = jock itch, pedis = athlete's foot.
  • Skin, hair and nails most susceptible; antifungal cream or solution.

Infestations and bites

  • Pediculosis (lice), bedbugs, ticks, scabies. Allergy to the venom usually causes the problem.
  • Prevent: avoid them, repellents, meticulous hygiene (hair, skin, clothing, bed, sexual partners), routine skin checks; tick check after the woods.
Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN): rare but life-threatening. Usually a severe adverse reaction to a medication (or to infection). Acute destruction of skin epithelium and mucous membranes (peeling lips); fever, cough, headache, anorexia, muscle weakness. Can be fatal.

Contact dermatitis is the benign end of the dermatitis spectrum (example: a reaction to rubber boots).

Psoriasis

  • Chronic autoimmune disease; usually develops at 15 to 35; runs in families.
  • Plaque psoriasis is most common: distinct red, scaly lesions on knees, elbows, hands, lower back; painful and itchy.
  • 4 out of 10 develop psoriatic arthritis. Not life-threatening; body image disturbance.

Care for Skin Disorders

Interprofessional therapy

  • Phototherapy; radiation for squamous or basal cell; laser.
  • Antibiotics if bacterial. Steroids decrease inflammation but use judiciously (side effects, including skin atrophy).
  • Antihistamines for urticaria or pruritus; immunomodulators suppress an overactive immune system.
  • Punch biopsy or scraping (for example, malignant melanoma).

Nursing tips

  • Compresses: room-temperature tap water; assess for maceration (white, puffy epidermis from a wet dressing).
  • Tepid baths: too hot a bath adds to itching.
  • Apply lotion while skin is still damp (seals in moisture).
  • Pruritus: cool environment, hydration, antipruritic creams, systemic antihistamines (diphenhydramine), avoid scratching.

Surgical Wound Management

What slows healing

  • Age and loss of skin turgor; fragile skin, worst with long-term systemic steroids.
  • Decreased circulation and oxygenation (peripheral vascular disease).
  • Slower regeneration, less nutrient absorption and collagen, impaired immunity, dehydration.
  • Leukopenia; existing infection; chemo, anti-inflammatories, steroids.
  • Low hemoglobin, obesity, smoking, chronic disease, malnutrition.

Assessment

  • Three parts of wound management: assess, clean, protect.
  • Color: red/pink = healthy regeneration; yellow = maybe purulent; black = eschar (hinders healing).
  • Length x width x depth (cm); sinus tracts or tunnels; redness or swelling.
  • Primary intention: edges well approximated (butted together; staples, sutures, adhesive). Pulling apart = not approximated.
  • Drains and tubing; pain at the site.

Measuring & Charting

  • First measurement head to toe, second side to side, third depth.
  • Tunneling: a tunnel under the skin, probed with a cotton-tipped applicator. Undermining: slips under the lip around the wound.
  • Charted by the clock, 12 o'clock at the patient's head. Example: "full thickness, red wound bed, 7 x 5 x 3 cm, 3 cm tunneling at 7 o'clock, 2 cm undermining at 3 to 5 o'clock."

Note amount (scant, moderate, large, copious; if weighed, 1 gram = 1 mL), odor, consistency, color, and the skin around the wound and drain.

Complications of Wounds

Adhesions, contractions, hemorrhage, dehiscence, evisceration, fistula, infection, excessive granulation tissue, keloids. Starred in lecture: hemorrhage, dehiscence and evisceration, infection.

Hemorrhage

  • Greatest risk up to 48 hours after injury or surgery.
  • Clot coming loose, slipped sutures, vessel damage.
  • Internal bleeding: swelling and distension, sanguineous drainage, subtle vital sign changes; first: increased heart rate (compensating for decreased stroke volume).
  • Hematoma: local blood collection, blue or red bruise.
  • Apply direct pressure, notify the provider, monitor vitals closely.

Infection

  • Infection does not happen within 24 to 48 hours: a low-grade temp then isn't infection yet.
  • Shows 2 to 11 days after surgery or injury: pain, erythema, swelling, purulent drainage, fever, chills, foul odor, raised vitals, leukocytosis.
  • Risks: impaired circulation, malnutrition, chronic disease, poor wound care.
  • Aseptic technique, nutrition, rest; antibiotics after cultures are drawn (broad spectrum, then tailored to C&S).

Dehiscence and evisceration

  • Dehiscence: separation of the wound, usually a few underlying layers. Evisceration (the worst): viscera visible; can follow abdominal incisions; needs immediate surgery.
  • Signs: a sudden large increase in sanguineous or serosanguineous drainage after straining; the patient felt something "pop" or "give way."
  • Precipitated by coughing, sneezing, vomiting, hiccuping, straining for a BM.
  • Risks: older age, obesity, abdominal cancer, vomiting, infection, poor suturing, dehydration, malnutrition, increased pressure.
Evisceration or severe dehiscence: notify the provider immediately and stay with the patient. Cover the wound and any organs with sterile dressings soaked in sterile normal saline. Do not reinsert organs. Position with hips and knees bent. Keep calm and NPO (likely emergency surgery).

  • 1. Airway. 2. Fluids.

Acute & Rehab Phases

Acute phase

  • Begins with mobilization of extracellular fluid and diuresis; ends when partial-thickness wounds heal and full-thickness wounds are grafted. Weeks to months.
  • Partial thickness: eschar removed, re-epithelialization. Full thickness: surgical debridement and skin grafts.
  • Split-thickness graft harvested with a dermatome (often the thigh), may be meshed; donor site covered with a hydrophilic foam dressing.
  • Keep monitoring labs: fluid and electrolytes still in flux. PT, OT.

Rehab phase

  • Goal: return to a functional role in society; reconstructive surgery.
  • Prevent contractures and hypertrophic scarring: PT, OT, scheduled range of motion, pressure garments (keep scars flat).
  • Emotional needs: anger, anxiety, depression, fear, guilt, helplessness.

GERD & Hiatal Hernia

GERD: backward flow of acidic stomach contents into the esophagus through a loose lower esophageal sphincter (LES). Heartburn is the most common symptom; regurgitation; respiratory symptoms (night coughing spells).

Complications and diagnosis

  • Esophagitis; Barrett's esophagus; cough and bronchospasm.
  • Barium swallow or EGD, but often just a high-dose PPI for about two weeks: if symptoms respond, that's the diagnosis and invasive tests aren't needed.

Lifestyle teaching

  • Identify and avoid triggers. Quit smoking: LES strength improves immediately.
  • Avoid fatty foods; small meals, no fluids with meals (drink between); no late-night snacking.
  • Sit up 2 to 3 hours after meals; raise the head of the bed on 4 to 6 inch blocks ("gravity is not reflux's friend").
  • Weight loss if large (eases intra-abdominal pressure); medications.
Refractory GERD ProcedureHow It Works
LINX deviceEndoscopic. A ring of rare-earth magnets around the LES: increases closure but lets food pass.
Nissen fundoplicationSurgical (laparoscopic or open). The fundus is wrapped and sutured into a collar around the LES ("a big bear hug"). Laparoscopic preferred over a big incision when there's a choice.

Hiatal hernia

  • Part of the stomach herniates up through the diaphragm (diaphragmatic or esophageal hernia). Not reflux: a herniated area above the LES.
  • Often structural: increased abdominal pressure (pregnancy, obesity).
  • May be asymptomatic; symptoms and treatment similar to GERD.

Barrett's esophagus

  • Long-term esophagitis that changes cells (metaplasia). Can follow GERD or hiatal hernia; not everyone gets it.
  • Rare; confirmed by biopsy.
  • 30 to 40 fold increased risk of esophageal cancer.

Peptic Ulcer Disease

Erosion of GI mucosa from hydrochloric acid and pepsin. Causes: H. pylori and drugs, number one being NSAIDs. NSAIDs plus H. pylori = "double trouble." Also alcohol, smoking, family history. Stress aggravates an existing ulcer but hasn't been shown to cause one.

Commit to memory: NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) = GI upset and kidney problems. Acetaminophen (not an NSAID) = hepatotoxicity.

Types

  • Gastric: usually the antrum. Duodenal: just past the pyloric sphincter; 80% of ulcers are duodenal.
  • Most ulcers have H. pylori (the percentage was garbled in the transcript as "90 to 90%").
  • Acute: fewer layers, shorter, resolves. Chronic: 4 times more common, more severe, long-term.

Diagnosis

  • EGD.
  • Urea breath test and stool test: non-invasive H. pylori tests. Biopsy is the gold standard (invasive).
  • CBC (H&H for bleeding) and stool for occult blood (may be dark and tarry or invisible).

TPN

  • Know whether it's temporary or permanent before you go in.
  • Assess color, swelling, bleeding.
  • Mild soap and warm water; skin barrier, then a snug pouch cut to the exact stoma size so no skin touches the drainage.

  • A colostomy near the rectum (solid stool) may be irrigated like an enema through the stoma, every day or two.
  • Old stool and mucus may still pass from the anus.
  • Teaching may go slowly: a patient not yet willing to look at the stoma at discharge isn't uncommon.

Colorectal Cancer

Risks and signs

  • Red or processed meat, overweight, inactivity, alcohol, smoking, few fruits and vegetables, IBD history, genetics.
  • Insidious: often found advanced. Anemia (low H&H), rectal bleeding, abdominal pain, change in bowel habits, obstruction or perforation.
  • Rectal: discomfort, blood in stool. Descending: bowel change, bright red stool. Ascending: subtler (anemia).

Screening

  • Colonoscopy is the gold standard: average risk starts at 45, every 10 years; earlier and more often with strong family history or IBD. Remove precancerous polyps.
  • Fecal occult blood or stool DNA test: less favorable but acceptable, yearly.

Diverticulosis & Diverticulitis

Diverticulosis (the condition)

  • Outpouchings of the intestinal mucosa. Usually asymptomatic; maybe pain, bloating, gas, bowel changes.
  • Prevent: high fiber, low fat, low red meat, activity. With it: keep eating high fiber.
  • No need to avoid nuts and seeds (no evidence) unless they cause a flare.
  • Weight reduction; avoid raised intra-abdominal pressure (constipation).

Diverticulitis (the flare)

  • Left lower quadrant pain, palpable mass, signs of infection (an older adult may only be confused).
  • NPO to rest the gut; if severe, admit for IV fluids and antibiotics.
  • Watch for abscess, bleeding, peritonitis (a ruptured diverticulum spills stool into the peritoneum).
  • Complications may need resection and a temporary ostomy.

Intestinal Obstruction

Mechanical (most obstructions)

  • Mostly small intestine: adhesions, hernias, strictures.
  • Surgical adhesions are the number one cause, days to years post-op.

Non-mechanical: paralytic ileus

  • About 1 in 10. Peristalsis stops, most often after the bowel has been manipulated.
  • NG to low wall suction and wait for peristalsis, or the patient keeps vomiting.

Care: decompress (NG to low wall suction), correct fluids and electrolytes, pain control, remove the obstruction if needed (bowel resection with end-to-end anastomosis: no stoma).

Cancer: Prevention, Diagnosis & Treatment

Prevention and early detection

  • Avoid carcinogens: excess alcohol, poor diet, tobacco, too much sun.
  • Diet, exercise, rest; regular exams and screenings; teach self-exams (breast, testicular).

Diagnosis

  • History; cytology (Pap, sputum); tissue biopsy; labs; chest X-ray; mammogram.
  • Tumor markers (PSA, CEA): made by tumor cells; used for diagnosis or progression.
  • Bone marrow exam for cancers that start in the marrow (leukemia, lymphoma).
CAUTION, the seven warning signs: Change in bowel or bladder habits · A sore that does not heal · Unusual bleeding or discharge · Thickening or lump in the breast or elsewhere · Indigestion or difficulty swallowing · Obvious change in a wart or mole · Nagging cough or hoarseness.

Goals: cure; if not, control; if not, palliative care. Surgery (about half of patients): prophylactic (double mastectomy for a high-risk gene), biopsy, cure, control, debulking (remove part of a tumor for comfort or to help chemo and radiation; not curative), palliation, second look, reconstruction.

Chemotherapy

Giving chemo

  • The mainstay of cancer therapy: solid tumors and blood cancers. Systemic.
  • Must be chemo certified; very toxic; protect patient and nurse; two-RN double check.
  • Central venous catheters preferred (not required).
  • Premedicate for nausea and vomiting (and anticipatory nausea and vomiting); anti-inflammatory and anti-allergy meds.
  • Check WBC, platelets, H&H first: hold chemo if WBCs or platelets are too low.

Nadir

  • The point after chemo when counts are at their lowest; they then regenerate.
  • Chemo every two weeks: about day 7, 8, 9 the patient feels worst; counts should recover by the next visit, or treatment is delayed.
  • Teach: you won't feel well then; limit exposure to other people.
Extravasation (a vesicant infiltrating tissue) can cause necrosis and limb loss. Prevent: watch the site closely, a patent, free-flowing IV with good blood return. Pain is the cardinal symptom, but not always. Blood return lost? Stop the chemo. If it happens: stop the infusion immediately, attach a syringe and aspirate gently, remove the cannula, elevate the arm, give the antidote per protocol (pharmacy helps).

Radiation

About 60% of patients with cancer get radiation. Local, precisely targeted (chemo is systemic); alone or with chemo or surgery.

  • Wash daily with mild soap and water, using the hand, not a washcloth; rinse well.

  • Pat dry. No powders, lotions or creams unless the radiologist prescribes them.
  • Soft, loose clothing; no direct sun or temperature extremes.
  • Dry vs wet desquamation (moist, incredibly tender).

In cancer care, neutrophils are an actual count (segs plus bands), not a differential percentage. Neutrophils answer acute bacterial infection, so neutropenia = infection risk; filgrastim (a WBC growth factor) may be given.

Neutropenic precautions

  • Isolation technique, screen visitors, no fresh flowers or plants, monitor for infection.
  • Diet: avoid raw fruits and vegetables that can't be peeled (berries); peelable ones are OK (oranges, bananas, apples, pineapple); wrapped food and flatware; no raw peanuts or seeds.

Platelets and anemia

  • Platelets normal 150,000 to 400,000. Serious bleeding may not show until below 50,000; transfuse usually below 20,000.
  • Anemia: fatigue. Monitor H&H; energy conservation but stay active; iron-rich diet. Epoetin if hemoglobin is less than 11; PRBCs if severe.

GI effects

  • Anorexia, taste changes (focus on liked foods), nausea and vomiting.
  • Weigh twice weekly; albumin and prealbumin; small, frequent, high-protein, high-calorie meals; supplements.
  • Diarrhea is often from localized radiation.

Stomatitis

  • Inflamed, ulcerated mouth (mucositis is the general term); extremely painful.
  • Thorough oral assessment; meticulous, gentle brushing and flossing; saline gargles; numbing swish-and-spit mixtures.
  • No alcohol mouthwash. Artificial saliva; soft, non-irritating foods.
  • Other effects: fatigue, hair loss (unpredictable; may come back thicker and curly), lung and heart damage, infertility in young people.
  • Support the right to complementary treatments while encouraging the prescribed plan; a calm, present, healing environment.
  • Psychosocial: hair or body-part loss, fear of recurrence ("body vigilant"), secondary cancers, disability and death. Hope is positive reappraisal; spirituality can help or hurt; social support is one of the most important things.

Jaundice

  • Yellow sclera, skin or hard palate; always too much bilirubin; usually present when bilirubin is greater than 2.5. A symptom, not a disease.
  • Hemolytic: RBC breakdown (wrong blood; check urine for hematuria).
  • Obstructive: intrahepatic or extrahepatic (a gallstone in the common bile duct). Bilirubin can't reach the gut: clay-colored stools and dark urine.

Care and teaching

  • No specific treatment: rest, managed at home.
  • No special diet: well balanced, adequate calories; low fat if fat isn't tolerated; B complex and vitamin K.
  • Itching: diphenhydramine.
  • Stop alcohol; avoid hepatotoxic drugs: acetaminophen and isoniazid.
  • Hygiene; treated or bottled water abroad; don't share linens, utensils, glasses, needles, razors, nail clippers, toothbrushes; condoms; cover cuts. Hep C: never donate blood, organs or tissue. Notify contacts.

Managing ascites and fluid

  • Rest first.
  • Fluid and sodium restriction (water follows salt).
  • Diuretics: spironolactone (potassium sparing) and furosemide (potassium losing), often both.
  • Albumin to raise oncotic pressure.
  • Abdominal girth with skin markings, but daily weight is more accurate.

Paracentesis and TIPS

  • Paracentesis when ascites impairs breathing. Consent, baseline vitals, have the patient void (avoid puncturing a full bladder), supine or high Fowler's. Often repeated in cirrhosis.
  • TIPS (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt): a non-surgical stent that lowers portal hypertension, controlling ascites and variceal bleeding.

Nutrition

  • High calorie, high carbohydrate, moderate to low fat.
  • Restrict protein only with severe encephalopathy; otherwise protein's benefits outweigh the ammonia risk.
  • Low sodium with fluid excess and ascites.

Bleeding Precautions

Assess

  • Coags; oral cavity (gums bleed first); ecchymosis, purpura, petechiae.
  • Fall prevention: a fall risks head injury and subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Avoid

  • Aspirin; alcohol, spicy and bulky foods; injections.
  • Vigorous nose blowing and straining at stool (raises pressure and can rupture varices): stool softeners.
  • Hard toothbrushes, rectal temps, enemas. Hold pressure longer after an IM shot.
Bleeding esophageal varices are an emergency. Endoscopic sclerotherapy (injected sclerosing agent) or variceal banding (rubber bands around the base). For an active bleed: balloon tamponade (Blakemore tube) applies direct pressure; the patient goes to the ICU.

Hepatic Encephalopathy Care

  • Goal: get the ammonia down.
  • Control GI bleeding: blood in the gut is nitrogen-rich and raises ammonia.
  • Avoid constipation. Lactulose titrated to about 2 to 4 stools a day to remove ammonia. Teach why, even if they've already had a BM today.
  • Monitor level of consciousness and GCS (EMV); safety precautions for confusion.

Cholelithiasis & Cholecystitis

Cholelithiasis = gallstones in the gallbladder; cholecystitis = inflamed gallbladder (acute or chronic). Risks: middle-aged female, fair skin, overweight, high-fat diet, oral contraceptives.

Manifestations

  • Episodic, vague upper abdominal pain radiating to the right shoulder, triggered by high-fat or large meals.
  • Nausea, vomiting, indigestion, belching, gas; fever with inflammation.
  • Jaundice, clay-colored stools, dark urine, steatorrhea: most common with chronic cholecystitis (a stone blocking the common bile duct).

Treatment

  • Non-surgical: avoid fatty foods; NPO during a flare; opioids, antiemetics, antispasmodics for biliary pain.
  • Rarely lithotripsy; uncommonly ERCP with sphincterotomy.
  • Most common: laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

After a lap chole

  • Remove the puncture-site bandages the day after surgery; may shower.
  • Call for redness, swelling, purulent or bile-colored drainage, increasing pain, nausea, vomiting, fever, chills.
  • Gradually increase activity; back to work in about a week.
  • Low-fat diet tolerated better for the first few weeks; then a regular diet.

Open cholecystectomy and T-tube

  • Subcostal incision healing by primary intention.
  • A T-tube keeps the common bile duct patent while inflammation goes down.
  • Rarely goes home with it: teach emptying the bile bag.

Complications

  • Pseudocyst: cavity of necrotic products outside the pancreas; often resolves; worst case it perforates into the peritoneum (severe peritonitis).

Acute care

  • Pain relief first (can cause hemodynamic compromise): IV opioids (morphine); knees bent / fetal position.
  • Labile vitals: monitor often; fluid balance; aggressive IV hydration.

  • Monitor stools for steatorrhea and blood sugar for hyperglycemia (endocrine function).
  • Quit alcohol. Diet after NPO: carbohydrates encouraged (less stimulating), fat restricted.

Chronic Pancreatitis

  • Progressive, destructive inflammation and fibrosis with remissions and flares. Intense pain (less tenderness than acute); a mass is likely a pseudocyst or abscess; ascites with possible respiratory compromise; steatorrhea (oily, floating, greasy, foul); dark urine.
  • Opioids given more cautiously (some can increase pain through spasms).
  • Weight loss can be significant: 4,000 to 6,000 calories a day PO, so TPN may be considered.
  • Prevent flares: avoid alcohol, caffeine, nicotine, spices; bland, low-fat, high-protein, high-carbohydrate, small high-calorie meals; rest after eating.

Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT): pancrelipase

  • Standard of care to prevent malnutrition, malabsorption and weight loss. Contains amylase, lipase and protease.
  • Take with the first bite of every meal or snack. Sometimes ordered with an antacid or H2 blocker (pH).
  • Swallow without chewing; wipe the lips. Powder in applesauce or juice; not mixed with protein foods. Don't crush enteric-coated forms.
  • Record the number and consistency of stools: goal is fewer, less fatty stools.
