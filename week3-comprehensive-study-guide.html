Part 1 — Intro to Dysrhythmias, Key Features of Dysrhythmias & Sinus Dysrhythmias

From Intro to Dysrhythmias, Key Features of Dysrhythmias & Sinus Dysrhythmias. Built from the first three Week 3 voiceovers: intro to dysrhythmias (conduction, the EKG waveform, telemetry), key features of dysrhythmias, and sinus dysrhythmias. Reading a rhythm strip step by step is saved for clinical conference. Heart blocks and junctional rhythms are not covered in 405.

Conduction Review

The chambers

  • Atria on top, ventricles on the bottom. Right side pumps to the lungs; left side pumps to the body.
  • The left ventricle is the powerhouse: it's where cardiac output (stroke volume) happens.
  • We can live with quivering atria (atrial fibrillation). We cannot live with quivering ventricles (V-fib).
  • Electrical conduction drives the mechanical pumping; without it, the heart does not pump.

The pathway

  • SA (sinoatrial) node: the initiator, in the right atrium. Intrinsic rate 60 to 100 per minute. Below 60 = bradycardia; above 100 = tachycardia.
  • It fires, a tiny delay, then the atria contract.
  • The impulse reaches the AV (atrioventricular) node, the gateway to the ventricles; a tiny pause.
  • Then the bundle of His and Purkinje fibers, and the ventricles contract.

Dysrhythmias are abnormal cardiac rhythms (the name changed from "arrhythmia," which implies no rhythm at all). They require a prompt assessment, and the nurse decides whether to do something about it.

The EKG Waveform

An EKG (ECG, electrocardiogram) is the graphic tracing of the heart's electrical impulses; the waveforms represent charged ions moving across the myocardial cell membranes. "Very fundamental and necessary to know" for any nurse caring for a patient on telemetry.

Depolarization = contraction. Repolarization = relaxation. Atrial relaxation happens but is buried in the QRS, so you can't see it on the strip.

Reading the Strip: Boxes and Six Seconds

The boxes

  • Each tiny box = 0.04 seconds (you need to know this).
  • Five tiny boxes (one big box) = 0.20 seconds.
  • Used to measure the PR interval and QRS width.

Heart rate from a strip

  • Every test strip is a 6-second strip; the marks at the top are 3 seconds apart, so two of them make 6 seconds.
  • Count the QRSs and multiply by 10: 9 QRSs = a heart rate of 90.

Telemetry

Electrode placement (5-lead)

  • White on the right: right below the right clavicle.
  • Black ("smoke"): just below the clavicle on the MCL.
  • Red ("fire"): smoke over fire.
  • Green ("grass"): lower chest, just to the right of the belly button. Snow (white) over grass.
  • Brown ("chocolate close to my heart"): 4th ICS, right sternal border.
  • Telemetry is 3-lead or 5-lead. Students can put electrodes on and replace ones that come off.

Leads and monitoring

  • Changing the lead at the monitor (lead I, II, III) just changes the "camera angle"; it does nothing to the patient. Lead I reads left to right; lead II (right shoulder to groin) is the usual choice.
  • A strip that looks strange may just be in lead III: change the lead to get a better look.
  • Central monitoring (as at UK) and alarms are not fail-proof.
Something changes on the monitor? Go check on your patient. It might be a lead that fell off, but something else could be going on. Artifact (brushing teeth, chest physiotherapy, moving a lot in bed, a low battery) looks scary but isn't the patient's heart: check the patient, the connections and the electrodes, and replace them if needed.

Classifying Dysrhythmias

Two ways: by early, slow or fast (premature complexes, bradydysrhythmias below 60, tachydysrhythmias above 100), or by the site of origin in the heart (sinus, atrial, ventricular).

Bradydysrhythmia (HR below 60)

  • ☺ Reduces myocardial oxygen demand (why beta blockers slow the rate on purpose).
  • ☺ Longer diastole = more time to perfuse the coronary arteries (they perfuse during diastole).
  • ☹ If too slow, coronary perfusion falls (angina).
  • May be tolerated if the BP is OK (an athlete with a resting HR of 54). If BP isn't adequate: myocardial ischemia or infarct, dysrhythmias, hypotension, heart failure.

Tachydysrhythmia (HR above 100)

  • ☹ Shortens diastole, so less coronary perfusion time (angina).
  • ☺ At first may raise cardiac output and BP (compensation).
  • ☹ If sustained: less ventricular filling, so stroke volume, cardiac output and BP fall. About 104 is usually tolerable; at 120 to 130 this starts happening.
  • ☹ More cardiac workload = more myocardial oxygen demand. Can lead to MI, unstable angina, dysrhythmias, low BP, heart failure.

Sinus Rhythms

All four start in the SA node. Normal sinus rhythm and sinus arrhythmia are not dysrhythmias at all; nurses often just say the patient is "in sinus."

RhythmRateR to RKey Points

Sinus arrhythmiaNormal (the example strip: 8 QRSs = 80)Not quite regularFrom changes in intrathoracic pressure with breathing (inspiration and expiration). Normal physiology, seen in young healthy people. Skinny QRSs, a P before every QRS. Not treated: just an assessment finding.

Sinus Bradycardia

Causes

  • Vagal (parasympathetic) stimulation: carotid sinus massage (a provider-only procedure), vomiting or gagging, the Valsalva maneuver (bearing down), applying eyeball pressure.
  • Parasympathomimetic drugs (given on purpose to slow the rate).
  • Digoxin toxicity (narrow therapeutic index; can be life threatening).
  • Hyperkalemia (slows depolarization).
  • Myocardial infarction.

What the nurse does

  • HR of 52? Go check on the patient and take a full set of vital signs (BP is the indirect measure of cardiac output).
  • Asymptomatic ("I'm feeling fine") with OK vitals: monitor, don't treat.
  • Symptomatic (lightheaded, nauseous, "I feel like I'm going to pass out," low BP): give atropine.
Symptomatic bradycardia: atropine 1 mg IV every 3 to 5 minutes, 3 mg max (a vagolytic: it blocks the vagus nerve). Still symptomatic? Transcutaneous pacing right here, right now: pads on, push the button, and you essentially create the SA node through the skin. Persistent bradycardia that doesn't respond may eventually need a permanent pacemaker (under the skin below the clavicle; fires when the SA node isn't cooperating, every beat or only some).

Sinus Tachycardia

Causes

  • Exertion (running up the stairs), anxiety, pain, stress, fever.
  • Anemia (not enough "buses," so the heart speeds up to circulate the ones you have); hypoxia.
  • Compensation for decreased BP: cardiac output = heart rate x stroke volume, so low stroke volume raises the HR (for example MI, heart failure).

Treatment: treat the cause

  • Not one size fits all, unlike atropine for bradycardia.
  • Low fluid volume: replace fluid. Pain: analgesic. Fever: antipyretic. Panic attack: anxiolytic (a benzodiazepine).
  • Beta blockers reduce HR and myocardial oxygen demand, but try to treat the cause first.
  • Symptoms if not tolerated: the key-features table (palpitations are the tachy-specific one).

Part 2 — Atrial Dysrhythmias: PAC, A-Fib, A-Flutter & PSVT

"Pretty meaty content, so pretty significant." For the symptoms of any too-fast rhythm, the lecture keeps pointing back to one slide:

Premature Atrial Contraction (PAC)

  • A one-time early discharge of an ectopic beat outside the SA node: an isolated premature atrial beat. A PAC is to the atria what a PVC is to the ventricles.
  • On the strip: equal R to R, then one early beat, then back to equal R to R (normal sinus rhythm with one PAC).
  • Causes: usually benign; stress, stimulants; electrolyte imbalances; pathology.
  • Benign: do nothing. Pathologic: intervene, with the same medications as for A-fib.
New-onset or increasing PACs in a hospital patient: check the electrolytes (especially magnesium, calcium and potassium) and let the provider know. Pathologic PACs can be a precursor to A-fib, so time can be of the essence: medication may prevent the conversion.

Atrial Fibrillation vs Atrial Flutter

FeatureAtrial Fibrillation (A-Fib)Atrial Flutter (A-Flutter)
What happensDisorganized atrial activity: the atria quiver. The SA node is no longer in charge; many ectopic foci fire at once.One single ectopic focus in the atria tries to overtake the SA node; the atria flutter (not fibrillate).
WavesNo P waves: tiny irregular waves (quivers). The atrial kick is lost.Sawtooth flutter waves ("shark bites"), called F waves, not P waves. No consistent P wave either.

The AV node is the gatekeeper. It keeps 400 atrial impulses from all reaching the ventricles. The ventricular rate is what matters: an A-fib patient with a controlled ventricular rate of 90 can live with it. "You can live with A-fib. You can't live with V-fib."
The danger of A-fib: clots. The atria aren't contracting, so blood pools and clots form. A clot that breaks loose from the left atrium becomes an embolus and can cause a stroke.

Treating A-Fib and A-Flutter

Number one: control the ventricular rate. Then control the rhythm, and prevent an embolic stroke. Rate is the priority.

GoalDrugs (Usually IV at First)
Control the rate (priority)Beta blockers (metoprolol) and calcium channel blockers (diltiazem, verapamil); digoxin or amiodarone are also options in the stable patient.
Control the rhythmAmiodarone, dofetilide, initially IV.
Prevent clotsWarfarin or another anticoagulant.

Stable but symptomatic

  • Hemodynamically stable (vital signs OK) but symptomatic: slow the ventricular rate with an IV calcium channel blocker, beta blocker, digoxin or amiodarone.
  • Usually a bolus, then a drip: the bolus raises the drug level immediately; a drip alone would take too long. The bolus wears off as the drip takes over.

Unstable

  • Hemodynamically unstable (vital signs unstable, not tolerating it): synchronized cardioversion.
  • It's the choice of therapy for hemodynamically unstable supraventricular tachydysrhythmias (tachy rhythms that start above the ventricle).

Synchronized Cardioversion

  • Done with the LifePak (the "life pack"). It delivers a countershock on the R wave of the QRS. "Cardioversion" always means synchronized, even when the word is dropped.
  • You must have an R wave to cardiovert. A-fib and A-flutter have one, so these patients are cardioverted, not defibrillated.
  • Turn the sync switch ON (the sync button sits under the shock button). Unsynchronized, the shock fires whenever you push the button, and if it lands on the wrong part of the QRS it can trigger a life-threatening dysrhythmia.
  • Non-emergency: sedate first. Start low, usually 50 to 100 joules, and increase if necessary (the joules go up if the first try doesn't work).
  • Make sure all personnel are clear and no one is touching the bed.
  • If the patient becomes pulseless, the QRS is lost: turn the sync off and defibrillate.
  • Nursing care: patent airway (sedated), supplemental oxygen, vital signs (hemodynamic stability), level of consciousness, monitor for dysrhythmias, emotional support, document the results.
A-fib longer than 48 hours: anticoagulation is recommended for 4 to 6 weeks before cardioversion, and a TEE is done first to make sure there are no clots in the atria. Otherwise a successful cardioversion restores a strong atrial kick that can throw the clot to the brain (stroke). An emergent cardioversion in a very unstable patient: likely a low molecular weight heparin or a heparin drip.

Long-Term Anticoagulation

Warfarin

  • If treatment doesn't work, long-term anticoagulation: A-fib for life means warfarin for life.
  • Disadvantage: the INR must be monitored regularly ("therapeutically thin, but not too thin").
  • Antidote: vitamin K, or FFP (not a nursing decision).

The newer anticoagulants

  • Some don't need routine lab testing (an advantage).
  • The instructor doesn't think they have an antidote (a disadvantage).
  • They cost more and are contraindicated with impaired renal function: check the creatinine before giving them.
  • Which one: the provider's decision.

Non-Drug Treatments: Catheter Ablation and the Maze Procedure

Drug therapy for dysrhythmias, while frequently effective, is limited by high failure rates and potential drug toxicity.

Catheter ablation

  • Invasive, in the cath lab. After electrophysiology studies and mapping locate the irritable focus, the provider destroys (ablates) it.

Maze procedure

  • A surgical procedure: intentional incisions in the atrium create a one-way path from the SA node to the AV node.

PSVT (Paroxysmal Supraventricular Tachycardia)

  • Supraventricular = the impulse starts above the ventricle (above the bifurcation of the bundle of His): the SA node or anywhere in the atria. A generic catch-all term.
  • A run of repeated premature beats that usually starts with a PAC.
  • Paroxysmal = abrupt onset and abrupt termination. Sustained SVT starts abruptly but doesn't stop on its own, so something must be done.
  • Causes: in a normal heart, overexertion, stress, stimulants (not terribly uncommon). More serious: digoxin toxicity and various heart diseases.
  • Presentation depends on how long it lasts and how fast the ventricular rate is: too fast means poor ventricular filling, lower stroke volume and cardiac output (the tachydysrhythmia key features).
StepTreating PSVT (Patient on the Monitor)
1. Vagal maneuversThe most effective: bear down like having a bowel movement. Hold the breath 10 to 15 seconds, hard enough that the neck veins distend, then resume breathing. If that fails, intentional coughing (on a ventilator, suctioning to make them cough).
Provider onlyCarotid sinus massage ("MD only": not a nursing action).
Rarely used nowThe diving reflex: submerging the face in cold water.
2. AdenosineIf vagal maneuvers don't work.

Two A drugs, opposite jobs: Adenosine slows the heart rate (PSVT). Atropine speeds it up (symptomatic bradycardia). Don't mix them up.
Giving adenosine: an incredibly short half-life, so push it very fast and flush with saline immediately (one or two saline syringes through a stopcock, pushed at the same time). Onset 10 to 40 seconds, duration 1 to 2 minutes. The strip may show a brief pause (asystole) and the patient may feel it: warn the patient so it doesn't scare them.

Part 3 — Ventricular Dysrhythmias: PVC, V-Tach & V-Fib

From Ventricular Dysrhythmias: PVC, V-Tach & V-Fib. Built from the Week 3 ventricular dysrhythmias voiceover. These "can be scary": they are very life-threatening. ACLS is a course of its own; for the 405 exam the instructor will ask only what is on these slides.

Premature Ventricular Contraction (PVC)

  • A premature beat from an ectopic focus in the ventricles. It comes earlier than the QRS should and doesn't follow the normal rhythm.
  • "Premature and it's wide and it's ugly": a wide, distorted QRS that doesn't look like the others. It typically does not perfuse.
  • Causes: stimulants, electrolytes (always check them with dysrhythmias), hypoxia, fever, exercise, emotional stress, cardiovascular disease.
  • Treatment: find and fix the cause. Drugs for a tendency toward PVCs: beta blockers, lidocaine, amiodarone.
PatternWhat It Means
BigeminyA PVC every other beat. Since PVCs don't usually perfuse, only every other beat is perfusing.
TrigeminyA PVC every third beat.
QuadrigeminyA PVC every fourth beat.
Unifocal PVCsAll from the same irritable focus (they look alike).
Multifocal PVCsFrom more than one irritable focus (one goes up, another goes down). "Makes me raise my eyebrows even further."
PVCs increasing in frequency, or new-onset: watch closely and let the provider know. An isolated PVC can be benign (some patients always have a few), but a patient with a PVC every other beat may be about to convert to V-tach, a crisis.

Part 4 — Heart Failure, Peripheral Arterial Disease, Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm & Raynaud's

From Heart Failure, Peripheral Arterial Disease, Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm & Raynaud's. Built from three Week 3 voiceovers: heart failure, PAD, and AAA with Raynaud's. PAD, AAA and Raynaud's are the three arterial disorders; the two venous ones are on the next page.

Heart Failure: The Basics

Know the circulation well, or left- and right-sided failure get mixed up: right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonic valve, pulmonary artery, lungs (oxygenated), left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve, aorta, body. Right-sided failure backs blood up on the right side; left-sided failure backs it up on the left. The manifestations depend on where the backup happens.

CauseWhat It Means
PreloadThe blood sitting in the heart during diastole. More blood volume = more preload.
AfterloadThe resistance the left ventricle must overcome to get blood out. High blood pressure raises afterload and, untreated, causes heart failure.
Myocardial contractilityThe muscle itself can't contract, regardless of fluid volume (for example a myocardium damaged by an MI).
Heart rateToo fast or too slow for a long period of time.

Ejection fraction

  • Stroke volume divided by the end-diastolic volume (the blood in the left ventricle before ejection).
  • Normal 55 to 70%. 40% or lower: heart failure. Very, very low is incompatible with life: an LVAD or heart transplant candidate.

HFrEF vs HFpEF (you'll see these in clinical)

  • HFrEF (reduced EF): a defect in systolic function: the LV isn't contracting effectively.
  • HFpEF (preserved EF): a defect in diastolic function (filling): the muscle contracts normally but is stiff and doesn't relax; EF normal or near normal.

Left-Sided vs Right-Sided Heart Failure

NCLEX and HESI questions tease out the difference, but it's a closed circuit: left failure eventually causes right failure, and vice versa, so patients often have both.

FeatureLeft-SidedRight-Sided
Where blood backs upLeft atrium and pulmonary veins, so the cues are pulmonaryRight atrium and the venous circulation, everywhere but the lungs
CuesPulmonary congestion; crackles, rhonchi, dyspnea (on exertion or at rest); orthopnea (tripod position); paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND); blood-tinged sputum; restless, confused; tachycardia; fatigue; possibly cyanoticJVD (the first place it goes, right outside the right atrium); hepatomegaly (tender RUQ) and splenic engorgement; ascites (third spacing); nausea and no appetite (engorged GI tract); peripheral edema (settles with gravity in the ankles; can be in the hands); scrotal edema in men (can be very painful); fatigue

Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND)

  • Paroxysmal = abrupt onset without warning; nocturnal = night; dyspnea = short of breath.
  • Asleep, then suddenly wakes and has to sit up to catch their breath: a sign of advanced left-sided failure.

Cor pulmonale is different

  • Enlargement of the right heart from pulmonary hypertension (for example in COPD): a type of right heart failure.
  • Right-sided failure that results from left-sided failure is not cor pulmonale.

Chronic HF vs Acute Decompensated HF

Chronic heart failure

  • Typically diagnosed outpatient; periods of acutely or slowly worsening function.
  • Often the consequence of damage from other cardiac events (CAD, hypertension). Can be insidious ("I've been feeling fatigued").

Acute decompensated (ADHF)

  • Diagnosed inpatient; no time to compensate. Can be life-threatening and need urgent therapy.
  • They may have 4+ edema and lots of ascites, but what brings them in is that they can't breathe.

Managing Chronic Heart Failure

Nursing measures

  • Target the underlying cause (hypertension: antihypertensives).
  • Oxygen 2 to 6 L (home, temporary or PRN) for dyspnea and fatigue.
  • Energy conservation and minimizing oxygen demand, but not sedentary: maintain activity.
  • Daily weights: same time of day, same scale, about the same clothing. Weight is the most consistent measure of fluid volume status.
  • Low-sodium diet ("salt sucks"): less water retention.

Drug therapy

  • ACE inhibitors and ARBs.
  • Beta blockers (carvedilol is usually the one).
  • Diuretics to get the fluid off.
  • Nitrates (coronary vasodilators such as nitroglycerin).
  • Digoxin (a cardiac glycoside): second line because of dysrhythmia risk, but HESI and NCLEX love it.
  • Spironolactone: a potassium-sparing diuretic, used here for suppressing sodium and water retention to offload the left ventricle; linked to fewer hospitalizations and cardiac deaths. It retains potassium and can worsen renal failure (loop and thiazide diuretics are potassium-losing).

Digoxin

What it does

  • Negative chronotrope: slows the heart rate (chrono = time, rate).
  • Positive inotrope: increases contractility.
  • Together: enhances cardiac output.
  • Narrow therapeutic index. Level 0.5 to 2 (won't be asked).

Digitoxicity

  • Digitalis + hypokalemia = digitoxicity. Monitor potassium, especially on a potassium-losing diuretic.
  • Signs: serious dysrhythmias and bradycardias, halos around lights, nausea and vomiting.
  • Antidote: Digibind.
Before every digoxin dose: take an apical heart rate for a full minute and hold it if the rate is below 60 ("commit to memory"). Teach the patient to take and record their own pulse daily.

LVAD (left ventricular assist device; not tested)

  • A bridge to a heart transplant, or destination therapy (prolonging life with no transplant planned).
  • Continuous flow: BP can't be read by machine, so take it manually with a Doppler. No S1 and S2, just a humming sound.
  • Unresponsive? Don't start CPR until you're sure the pump is completely off. Lots of patient and family teaching.

Acute decompensated HF care

  • Treat the cause; close vital signs and urine output (poor kidney perfusion lowers it); continuous telemetry and pulse oximetry; ABGs.
  • High Fowler's with the feet horizontal or dangling: decreases venous return and preload, easing the heart's workload.
  • BiPAP, face mask or non-rebreather; daily weights.
  • Meds: diuretics, vasodilators, morphine (decreases preload and afterload), positive inotropes such as digoxin.
  • Very sick: ICU and hemodynamic monitoring (not covered in med surg).

Arteries vs Veins

Arteries

  • A high-pressure system carrying oxygenated blood away from the heart.
  • Where the peripheral pulses come from. A needle in an artery gushes; we only stick arteries for ABGs or arterial lines.

Veins

  • A low-pressure system carrying deoxygenated blood back to the heart (often uphill from the legs).
  • Veins have valves to help venous return. IVs go in veins.

Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)

Blockages in the arteries of the periphery: the same idea as coronary artery disease. Risk factors: atherosclerosis plus smoking, diabetes, a really bad diet, elevated CRP, uncontrolled hypertension, family history. Put three or four together and PAD is no surprise.

Intermittent claudication

  • The classic PAD symptom: legs hurt when I walk, pain stops when I stop.
  • Why: perfusion is already poor, and the skeletal muscle pump of walking squeezes off some of the little arterial flow there is.
  • The pain's location matches where the occlusion is. Severe PAD: rest pain, a constant ache in bed.

Diagnosis

  • Doppler ultrasound: degree of blood flow.
  • Ankle brachial index (ABI): a non-invasive screening tool, office or bedside. "Yes, you do need to know how to do it."

Calculating the Ankle Brachial Index

Take a systolic pressure in all four extremities with a handheld Doppler. For one leg: that ankle's pressure divided by the higher of the two arm pressures.

PAD Collaborative Care

Medical and nursing care

  • Risk factor modification for the modifiable ones.
  • Statins, antihypertensives, possibly antiplatelets.
  • Pletal: specific for intermittent claudication; inhibits platelet aggregation and increases vasodilation.
  • Walk to the point of pain, stop and rest, then resume: builds collateral circulation.
  • Meticulous foot care: inspect the feet regularly (a nail stepped on may go unnoticed).

Procedures

  • Catheter-based (cath lab, usually the femoral artery; an alternative to open surgery): angioplasty (balloon), stent, atherectomy (plaque removal), angioplasty plus cold therapy. Like PCI for the heart.
  • Peripheral arterial bypass (like a CABG), named for what's bypassed: a fem-pop (femoral popliteal) bypass with a synthetic graft.
  • Amputation: the last resort.
After a PAD procedure: a meticulous peripheral vascular assessment, trended. Notify the provider of a dramatic increase in pain, a loss of pulse, extreme pallor, or any change. Avoid the knee-flexed position (impedes arterial flow). Early ambulation; meticulous foot care.

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)

An aneurysm is a permanent outpouching of the vessel wall. The aorta is a likely spot because of the incredibly high pressure it withstands. AAAs are often in the descending aorta.

Manifestations and risk

  • Usually no symptoms; often found on a routine exam: a mass, a bruit heard with the bell over the abdomen (you should not hear a bruit), or back pain.
  • The concern is rupture: life-threatening in a high-pressure system.
  • Familial tendency: monitor early in life if a family member had one.

Management

  • Small (under 4 cm): watchful waiting: decrease risk factors, keep the BP down, monitor the size annually.
  • Larger: surgery, ideally elective, because after a rupture mortality is very high (usually no time to reach the OR).

Raynaud's Phenomenon

What it is

  • An autoimmune vasospastic disorder of the small cutaneous arteries, usually the fingers (sometimes the toes).
  • May or may not occur with other diseases (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis). A young woman's disease.
  • Color changes from vasospasm: white, blue, sometimes red.
  • Triggers: cold, emotional stress, tobacco, caffeine.
  • Lasts a minute to hours: cold and numb, then throbbing, aching, tingling as perfusion returns.

Nursing management

  • Layered clothing, gloves; avoid extreme temperatures.
  • Immerse the hands in warm water to ease the spasms.
  • Avoid the triggers.
  • Severe: a slow-release calcium channel blocker, the first-line therapy, for the vessel spasms, not to lower BP.

Part 5 — Chronic Venous Insufficiency & Venous Thromboembolism

From Chronic Venous Insufficiency & Venous Thromboembolism. Built from the last Week 3 voiceover: the two venous disorders. Veins are a low-pressure system with valves whose job is to get blood back to the heart; when they can't, you get venous congestion, pooling and peripheral edema.

Chronic Venous Insufficiency (CVI)

What it looks like

  • Varicose veins (tortuous vessels): a venous finding.
  • Lipodermatosclerosis: thick, leathery skin of the lower leg with a characteristic brownish ("brawny") discoloration.
  • Pitting edema.
  • Eczema with itching.
  • Patients can have both arterial and venous disease, with a mixture of cues.

Venous ulcers

  • Typically above the medial malleolus; they look and behave differently from arterial ulcers.
  • Can be quite painful, worse when dependent (arterial ulcers often aren't, from lost nerve endings).
  • More common than arterial ulcers.
  • Untreated: deep, wide ulcers at increased risk for infection.

CVI Collaborative Care

Nursing measures

  • Avoid standing or sitting for long periods (impedes venous return).
  • Elevate the legs above the level of the heart: promotes venous return and decreases swelling.
  • A daily walking program (keeps both venous and arterial circulation moving).
  • Proper foot and leg care.
  • High-calorie, high-protein diet for skin healing.

Compression therapy

  • Two types, static and dynamic; all of them promote venous return by squeezing blood back to the heart. Static = graded compression from distal to proximal.
  • Prescription stockings may need a vascular specialist to fit them. A stocking donner helps put them on correctly.
  • White TED hose are seen less: put on wrong, they can impede blood flow.
  • SCDs (intermittent compression devices) are used for venous insufficiency, but are not recommended with an actual VTE (they can move the clot).
Elevate for veins, not for arteries. Raising the legs above the heart helps venous return, but in arterial disease it turns the legs pale.

Venous Thromboembolism (VTE)

What used to be called a DVT and a PE is now lumped together as a VTE. We can anticipate them, so the goal is prevention: target the modifiable risk factors.

Clinical manifestations

  • Unilateral leg edema: one leg larger than the other is concerning. Bilateral edema is unlikely to be a clot (more likely venous insufficiency).
  • Can be painful or tender to palpation (not always).
  • Dilated superficial veins; a sense of fullness or heaviness in the thigh or calf.
  • Numbness; warm, reddened skin and a low-grade temp (inflammation).
  • Usually the lower extremity.

Nursing care

  • Early and aggressive mobilization (patients with clots used to be kept in bed; not necessarily anymore). If not, turn every 2 hours.
  • Flex and extend the feet, hips and knees every 2 to 4 hours while awake: mimics the skeletal muscle pump.
  • Anticoagulation is very important, but it puts them at risk for bleeding.
  • Teach how to minimize the modifiable risk factors.
The most serious complication of a thrombus is a pulmonary embolism: the clot leaves where it started and ends up where it can be fatal.

Recurrent VTEs: a vena cava (Greenfield) filter can be placed in the inferior vena cava: a little cage that catches clots the patient still throws (the lecture says the clot "never gets to the brain"). Less common now; provider preference.
