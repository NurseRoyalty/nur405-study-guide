Premature Atrial Contraction (PAC)

  • A one-time early discharge of an ectopic beat outside the SA node: an isolated premature atrial beat. A PAC is to the atria what a PVC is to the ventricles.
  • On the strip: equal R to R, then one early beat, then back to equal R to R (normal sinus rhythm with one PAC).
  • Causes: usually benign; stress, stimulants; electrolyte imbalances; pathology.
  • Benign: do nothing. Pathologic: intervene, with the same medications as for A-fib.
New-onset or increasing PACs in a hospital patient: check the electrolytes (especially magnesium, calcium and potassium) and let the provider know. Pathologic PACs can be a precursor to A-fib, so time can be of the essence: medication may prevent the conversion.

Atrial Fibrillation vs Atrial Flutter

FeatureAtrial Fibrillation (A-Fib)Atrial Flutter (A-Flutter)
What happensDisorganized atrial activity: the atria quiver. The SA node is no longer in charge; many ectopic foci fire at once.One single ectopic focus in the atria tries to overtake the SA node; the atria flutter (not fibrillate).
WavesNo P waves: tiny irregular waves (quivers). The atrial kick is lost.Sawtooth flutter waves ("shark bites"), called F waves, not P waves. No consistent P wave either.
Atrial rateAbout 400About 250
Ventricular rateAnywhere from 100 to 175Slower than the atrial rate (not every impulse gets through)
R to RIrregularly irregular: no rhyme or reasonMore often regular (can still be irregular), since one focus gives it some rhythm
How commonThe most common dysrhythmia; prevalence increases with ageLess common than A-fib; rarely occurs in a healthy heart
CausesAlmost any underlying heart disease, electrolyte imbalances, hypoxia; often after cardiac surgeryUsually an underlying heart condition; check the electrolytes
PresentationDepends on the ventricular rate, how long the rhythm has been present, and the cardiovascular status. Onset is usually fast and abrupt, so the signs are those of a tachydysrhythmia. Both carry a risk of emboli.
The AV node is the gatekeeper. It keeps 400 atrial impulses from all reaching the ventricles. The ventricular rate is what matters: an A-fib patient with a controlled ventricular rate of 90 can live with it. "You can live with A-fib. You can't live with V-fib."
The danger of A-fib: clots. The atria aren't contracting, so blood pools and clots form. A clot that breaks loose from the left atrium becomes an embolus and can cause a stroke.

Treating A-Fib and A-Flutter

Number one: control the ventricular rate. Then control the rhythm, and prevent an embolic stroke. Rate is the priority.

GoalDrugs (Usually IV at First)
Control the rate (priority)Beta blockers (metoprolol) and calcium channel blockers (diltiazem, verapamil); digoxin or amiodarone are also options in the stable patient.
Control the rhythmAmiodarone, dofetilide, initially IV.
Prevent clotsWarfarin or another anticoagulant.

Stable but symptomatic

  • Hemodynamically stable (vital signs OK) but symptomatic: slow the ventricular rate with an IV calcium channel blocker, beta blocker, digoxin or amiodarone.
  • Usually a bolus, then a drip: the bolus raises the drug level immediately; a drip alone would take too long. The bolus wears off as the drip takes over.

Unstable

  • Hemodynamically unstable (vital signs unstable, not tolerating it): synchronized cardioversion.
  • It's the choice of therapy for hemodynamically unstable supraventricular tachydysrhythmias (tachy rhythms that start above the ventricle).

Synchronized Cardioversion

  • Done with the LifePak (the "life pack"). It delivers a countershock on the R wave of the QRS. "Cardioversion" always means synchronized, even when the word is dropped.
  • You must have an R wave to cardiovert. A-fib and A-flutter have one, so these patients are cardioverted, not defibrillated.
  • Turn the sync switch ON (the sync button sits under the shock button). Unsynchronized, the shock fires whenever you push the button, and if it lands on the wrong part of the QRS it can trigger a life-threatening dysrhythmia.
  • Non-emergency: sedate first. Start low, usually 50 to 100 joules, and increase if necessary (the joules go up if the first try doesn't work).
  • Make sure all personnel are clear and no one is touching the bed.
  • If the patient becomes pulseless, the QRS is lost: turn the sync off and defibrillate.
  • Nursing care: patent airway (sedated), supplemental oxygen, vital signs (hemodynamic stability), level of consciousness, monitor for dysrhythmias, emotional support, document the results.
A-fib longer than 48 hours: anticoagulation is recommended for 4 to 6 weeks before cardioversion, and a TEE is done first to make sure there are no clots in the atria. Otherwise a successful cardioversion restores a strong atrial kick that can throw the clot to the brain (stroke). An emergent cardioversion in a very unstable patient: likely a low molecular weight heparin or a heparin drip.

Long-Term Anticoagulation

Warfarin

  • If treatment doesn't work, long-term anticoagulation: A-fib for life means warfarin for life.
  • Disadvantage: the INR must be monitored regularly ("therapeutically thin, but not too thin").
  • Antidote: vitamin K, or FFP (not a nursing decision).

The newer anticoagulants

  • Some don't need routine lab testing (an advantage).
  • The instructor doesn't think they have an antidote (a disadvantage).
  • They cost more and are contraindicated with impaired renal function: check the creatinine before giving them.
  • Which one: the provider's decision.

Non-Drug Treatments: Catheter Ablation and the Maze Procedure

Drug therapy for dysrhythmias, while frequently effective, is limited by high failure rates and potential drug toxicity.

Catheter ablation

  • Invasive, in the cath lab. After electrophysiology studies and mapping locate the irritable focus, the provider destroys (ablates) it.

Maze procedure

  • A surgical procedure: intentional incisions in the atrium create a one-way path from the SA node to the AV node.

PSVT (Paroxysmal Supraventricular Tachycardia)

  • Supraventricular = the impulse starts above the ventricle (above the bifurcation of the bundle of His): the SA node or anywhere in the atria. A generic catch-all term.
  • A run of repeated premature beats that usually starts with a PAC.
  • Paroxysmal = abrupt onset and abrupt termination. Sustained SVT starts abruptly but doesn't stop on its own, so something must be done.
  • Causes: in a normal heart, overexertion, stress, stimulants (not terribly uncommon). More serious: digoxin toxicity and various heart diseases.
  • Presentation depends on how long it lasts and how fast the ventricular rate is: too fast means poor ventricular filling, lower stroke volume and cardiac output (the tachydysrhythmia key features).
StepTreating PSVT (Patient on the Monitor)
1. Vagal maneuversThe most effective: bear down like having a bowel movement. Hold the breath 10 to 15 seconds, hard enough that the neck veins distend, then resume breathing. If that fails, intentional coughing (on a ventilator, suctioning to make them cough).
Provider onlyCarotid sinus massage ("MD only": not a nursing action).
Rarely used nowThe diving reflex: submerging the face in cold water.
2. AdenosineIf vagal maneuvers don't work.
3. Synchronized cardioversionIf adenosine doesn't work and the patient becomes hemodynamically unstable. It resets the impulse.
Two A drugs, opposite jobs: Adenosine slows the heart rate (PSVT). Atropine speeds it up (symptomatic bradycardia). Don't mix them up.
Giving adenosine: an incredibly short half-life, so push it very fast and flush with saline immediately (one or two saline syringes through a stopcock, pushed at the same time). Onset 10 to 40 seconds, duration 1 to 2 minutes. The strip may show a brief pause (asystole) and the patient may feel it: warn the patient so it doesn't scare them.
