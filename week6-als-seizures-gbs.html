Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis, Seizure Disorders & Guillain-Barré Syndrome

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

Lou Gehrig's disease: a relentlessly progressive, presently incurable neurodegenerative disorder causing muscle weakness, disability and eventually death. Median survival is 3 to 5 years; 10% live 10 years or longer. A devastating trajectory: the patient remains cognitively intact while physically wasting away.

Cues

  • Asymmetric limb weakness is the most common presentation (one side, not the other).
  • Upper extremity: often a weak hand or shoulder girdle. Lower extremity: often weak dorsiflexion (foot drop: they stumble).
  • Symptoms spread within the area of onset, then to other regions in a fairly predictable pattern.
  • Fatigue; dysarthria (garbled speech) and dysphagia (difficulty swallowing).
  • Muscle atrophy and spasticity; fasciculations (twitching, as in the face or tongue).
  • Ultimately respiratory muscle weakness (intercostals, diaphragm): life-threatening.

Plan of care

  • Riluzole: the only FDA-approved drug for ALS; not a cure, but it extends survival.
  • PT consult: moderate-intensity endurance exercises for the trunk and limbs to reduce spasticity.
  • Support cognitive and emotional function; facilitate communication with speech therapy.
  • Keep the patient NPO once the risk for aspiration begins.
  • Pain assessment: they may not move well, but they still have sensation and still feel pain.
  • Minimize injury risk; diversional activities; anticipatory grieving for the patient and family.

Seizure Disorders

Seizures are transient, uncontrolled electrical discharges of neurons in the brain that interrupt normal function. Many things cause seizures; if the underlying cause can be removed, it isn't called a seizure disorder or epilepsy.

Types of Seizures

TypeWhat It Looks Like
Generalized: tonic-clonicBoth sides of the brain. Stiffening and jerking; loss of consciousness, falling, possible salivation and incontinence. Then the postictal phase: no memory, nearly unarousable. Keep them safe and let them rest (don't shake them awake).
Generalized: absenceUsually young people; rare beyond adolescence. A brief staring spell that looks like daydreaming; unresponsive during it. Hard to diagnose (a teacher may think the child isn't paying attention).
Focal onset with retained awarenessOne side of the brain. No loss of consciousness; brief; unusual sensations in many forms.
Focal onset without retained awarenessEyes open but loss of consciousness or changed awareness, as if daydreaming; movements may look purposeful but you can't interact with them (they may walk into traffic or take off their clothes). 1 to 2 minutes; afterward confused and very fatigued.

The Four Phases

PhaseExamples
Prodromal (some patients)A sensation or behavior hours to days before: insomnia, euphoria, disorientation, light sensitivity.
AuraA sensory warning that a seizure is about to happen: zigzag lines in the vision, a metallic taste, sudden nausea.
IctalThe seizure itself, from the first symptoms to the end of the seizure activity.
PostictalThe recovery period after the seizure.

Diagnosis

  • An accurate, comprehensive description of the seizure activity; health history.
  • An EEG, ideally as soon as possible after the seizure; it only helps diagnostically if the results are abnormal.
  • Rule out metabolic disorders and other causes.

During and After a Seizure

First aid during the seizure

  • ABCs: a patent airway; suction handy; breathing assistance afterward if needed.
  • Some agencies pad the rails.
  • Don't force anything into the mouth (no tongue blade).
  • Don't restrain unless something places the patient in danger.
  • Stay with the patient until the seizure ends.
  • Turn the head to the side if possible, but don't force it.

Monitor and document

  • Glasgow Coma Scale (eyes, motor, verbal) and pupil size and reactivity.
  • Onset and duration of the seizure.
  • Cyanosis (seen with tonic-clonic; usually self-limiting, but note it).
  • Let the person rest until fully awake; be reassuring and supportive.
Status epilepticus is a medical emergency: a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or repeated (back-to-back) seizures over 30 minutes. The airway is the number one concern; call the rapid response team (ICU if unresolved). The drugs of choice are benzodiazepines: lorazepam or diazepam ("-pam"). Seizures lasting uninterrupted more than 10 minutes can cause death, so the seizure activity must be interrupted.

Long-Term Management

Drugs and safety

  • A patient at risk for seizures in the hospital needs IV access (like a patient at risk for dysrhythmias); a medical alert ID.
  • Antiepileptic drugs: start with one and titrate to control the seizures.
  • Older drugs have a narrow therapeutic index, so drug levels are drawn. The classic example is phenytoin (Dilantin): a favorite of the NCLEX and HESI.
  • Phenytoin side effect to remember: gingival hyperplasia.
  • Teach adherence and never stop abruptly (that can cause a seizure). Half of ER seizure visits in one study were from non-adherence.

Surgery and psychosocial care

  • Surgical options: resecting part of the brain, or a vagal nerve stimulator ("a pacemaker for the brain") under the skin that sends regular mild pulses through the vagus nerve to prevent seizures.
  • Psychosocial ramifications are the most common complication of living with seizures: loss of independence, employment, insurance, the ability to drive, self-esteem.
  • Driving laws vary by state (all require a period without driving after a seizure); not tested.
  • Referrals, counseling, epilepsy support groups.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

An acute, immune-mediated polyneuritis: progressive, usually ascending, fairly symmetric muscle weakness with absent or depressed deep tendon reflexes (DTRs, not mentioned with the other disorders). Very often preceded by a viral infection, immunization, trauma or surgery.

Cues and course

  • Patients present a few days to a week after symptoms start; weakness ranges from mild difficulty walking to nearly complete paralysis.
  • Paresthesia in the hands and feet is very common; pain in the back and extremities.
  • Dysautonomia (the "automatic" nervous system): tachycardia, labile vital signs, ileus, urinary retention.
  • Progresses over about 2 weeks; by 4 weeks, 9 of 10 have reached the nadir (the peak).

Diagnosis

  • History and physical.
  • CSF from a spinal tap shows changes only after 1 week of onset.
  • Nerve conduction tests / EMG: confirm the diagnosis and help with the prognosis.
GBS can deteriorate quickly: 15 to 30% end up on mechanical ventilation. Vigilance: assess the gag and corneal reflexes, take frequent vital signs (autonomic changes), and expect ICU care if very hemodynamically labile.

GBS Care and Treatment

  • Nutrition: careful with any dysphagia; otherwise a nutrient-dense diet.
  • Neuropathic pain management (likely medication).
  • Monitor that the patient is voiding (autonomic disruption); support and encouragement through a variable recovery.
  • Plasmapheresis (removes circulating antibodies, complement and soluble biologic response modifiers): every other day over 10 days.
  • IVIG over 5 days. Both have equal efficacy, and must be given within 4 weeks of onset: time is of the essence.
  • Prognosis is very good: 95% survive, 75% recover completely, 25 to 30% have residual weakness after 3 years, and only 3% have weakness and tingling many years later.
