OnsetAbrupt: often arrives with a really high glucose, very sickCan go undiagnosed for years; often screened for by risk factors, not symptoms

DiagnosisIslet cell autoantibody testing (A1C still tracks treatment)The diagnostic criteria below

Symptoms

  • The three P's: polydipsia (excessive thirst), polyphagia (excessive hunger), polyuria (excessive urination).
  • Fatigue; recurrent infections (lower immune function); slow wound healing.

Type 2 risk factors

  • Non-modifiable: family history; over 45; African American, Hispanic, American Indian and Pacific Islander (higher risk than Caucasians); a history of gestational diabetes.
  • Modifiable (the target): physical inactivity, overweight or high body fat, high blood pressure, high cholesterol.

Diabetes Labs & Diagnosis

LabWhat the Lecture Says
Fasting blood glucoseNo food or drink for 8 hours. Normal is less than 126 (as stated); over that is a warning sign of diabetes.
Casual (random) blood glucoseNormal is less than 200; over 200 may indicate diabetes.
Urine ketonesPositive or high ketones relate to hyperglycemia; greater than 300 is a medical emergency (a clinic would send them to the ED and let the provider know).
Lipid profileLDLs and triglycerides may be elevated; HDLs lower.
Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)Used more for gestational diabetes, not usually type 1 or 2. Fasting level first, drink the glucose, then levels every 30 minutes for 2 hours. Goals: before, under 110; 1 hour, under 180; 2 hours, under 140.
Hemoglobin A1C (glycosylated hemoglobin)"This is huge." The average glucose over the past 120 days (about 3 months). Used to diagnose and to evaluate whether treatment is working (fasting glucose isn't used for that). Normal 4 to 6%; 6.5% or greater: possibly diabetes; the target for routine care is 7%.

Diagnosis (ADA criteria, mg/dL)

  • At least one: A1C 6.5 or greater; fasting glucose over 126; OGTT 200 or greater; or classic hyperglycemia symptoms with a random glucose over 200 or a hyperglycemic crisis.
  • Not from one lab: a fasting glucose over 126 is repeated within the next week; an A1C over 6.5 gets lifestyle changes and a recheck within 3 months.
  • The mmol/L values don't need memorizing.

Prediabetes

  • Impaired glucose tolerance, impaired fasting glucose, or both. High risk for type 2; usually no symptoms, but damage from insulin resistance may already be happening.
  • Nursing is mostly teaching: lifestyle modification, monitor glucose and A1C, follow up with the primary care provider, avoid sugary foods, count and monitor carbohydrates. Insulin resistance can be reversed.

Medications

Oral medications

  • Used more in type 2; started low and increased gradually based on the A1C and fasting (morning) glucose.
  • In the hospital, oral diabetes meds are often stopped and replaced with insulin while acutely ill (sugar is hard to control). Explain this, and that they go back to oral meds at home.
  • Bring forward pathopharm concepts, such as holding metformin before specific procedures and the drug classes (for example, know that an SGLT-2 inhibitor treats diabetes). No specific side-effect questions.

Insulin: mimic the body

  • The body spikes insulin with each meal. A basal-bolus regimen copies this: a basal (long-acting) insulin once a day, usually at bedtime (glargine), plus rapid or short-acting boluses before each meal.
  • Inhaled insulin: be aware of it; not tested.

Insulin is a high-alert medication. Never give insulin without knowing the current blood glucose ("always, always, always"), and know the normal range. Check the diet order and oral intake (no food for 24 hours changes things; scheduled doses may need adjusting with the provider). Know when the insulin peaks, how long it lasts and which type it is. Know the signs of hypoglycemia. NPO with scheduled insulin: follow hospital policy (for example 50% of the scheduled dose, or holding boluses). Ate, got insulin, then vomited: be ready to treat hypoglycemia.
  • Teaching matters most with insulin. Long-time diabetics often know their bodies: if they say "my sugar's about to drop," listen (recheck in 15 minutes).
  • Newly diagnosed: watch them self-administer. Timing is crucial: when it was given, when it will act, and when to expect a hypoglycemic reaction.

Monitoring and Sick Days

Self-monitoring blood glucose

  • A crucial part of management; frequency depends on the glycemic goal, the type, the regimen, supplies and willingness. A new type 2 on metformin: maybe morning and bedtime. On insulin: multiple times a day.
  • CGM (continuous glucose monitor): reads every 1 to 5 minutes to a phone or watch; more common in type 1; better control but expensive.
  • Insulin pumps: a continuous subQ infusion of rapid or regular insulin (a basal rate, plus self-given boluses). Still check fingersticks at least 4 times a day (before meals and bedtime), unless using a CGM.

Insulin pumps in the hospital

  • Acute care: deactivate the pump and switch to a sliding scale for tighter control.
  • Check the sites for infection (high glucose raises infection risk: "bacteria love to eat sugar").
  • Risk of DKA: a malfunctioning pump may go unnoticed while sugars climb.

Sick-Day Management

Illness is a stress that releases hormones that raise blood sugar, and steroids (oral or IV, common when acutely ill) can raise it dramatically, so home insulin may need adjusting. Sick patients are more prone to DKA (or HHS, the type 2 equivalent).

Teach the patient to

  • Let the provider know they're sick.
  • Check blood sugar more often, even every 2 to 4 hours.
  • Keep taking their medications, even insulin as long as the blood sugar tolerates it (oral meds work long-term, so take them even with a stomach virus).
  • Prevent dehydration; meet carbohydrate needs with food or liquids; rest.

Call the provider if

  • Urine is positive for ketones.
  • Blood sugar is greater than 250.
  • A fever not getting better with Tylenol.
  • Confused, disoriented, or rapid breathing.
  • Ongoing nausea, vomiting and diarrhea; can't tolerate liquids; illness lasting longer than 2 days.

This list is patient teaching for home. A hospitalized patient with a blood sugar over 250 on a test question doesn't automatically mean "call the provider."

Hypoglycemia and Hyperglycemia

Hypoglycemia (below 70)

  • Patients can have symptoms above 70 too, especially if they usually run high.
  • Sweating, blurry vision, irritability, shakiness; they can feel like they're going to die. Untreated: seizures and death.
  • Common after rapid-acting insulin when they then don't eat or go for a procedure.
  • An unresponsive patient: after the airway, check the blood sugar.

Hyperglycemia

  • Causes: illness, infection, self-management problems (running out of insulin, which is expensive), extreme stress, corticosteroids.
  • Weak, very tired, blurry vision, headache, upset stomach. Usually gradual and vague, so patients often don't notice until a crisis.
  • Usually means over 250, even 300; check urine ketones. Treat with insulin, fluids to prevent dehydration, and teaching (you may need more insulin when sick).
  • DKA and HHS (sugars in the 600s and 700s) are emergencies covered later: the electrolyte abnormalities can be fatal.
The rule of 15: confirm with a fingerstick (below 70). If awake and able to swallow, give 15 grams of simple carbohydrate: 4 to 6 oz of orange juice or regular soda, 3 glucose tabs, 1 tablespoon of honey, or 5 to 8 Lifesavers (no candy bars: fat delays absorption). This should raise the sugar about 50 in 15 minutes (54 to about 104). Recheck in 15 minutes; still below 70, repeat; once stable, eat a regular meal. Unresponsive or nearly passed out: IM glucagon or IV dextrose (25 to 50 mL); at home, a glucose gel under the tongue.

Chronic Complications

Caused by long-term hyperglycemia damaging the blood vessels (angiopathy), the usual cause of death in diabetes: about 68% cardiovascular and about 16% stroke.

Macrovascular (large vessels)

  • Coronary, cerebral and peripheral vessels. A much higher cardiovascular risk: women 4 to 6 times, men 2 to 3 times.
  • Prevent: stop smoking, control BP, modify high-fat diets, keep the A1C as low as possible, treat all the conditions (together they make metabolic syndrome, which raises the risk of death).

Microvascular (small vessels)

  • Eyes: frequent eye exams.
  • Kidneys: diabetes is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease in the US; treat hypertension too.
  • Nerves: neuropathy in about 60 to 70%, the major contributor to amputation. Only real treatment: early screening and lower blood sugars.

Neuropathy and Foot Care

  • Loss of protective sensation: a cut or a too-tight shoe goes unnoticed, especially on the bottoms of the feet. Ulcers can become neuro-ischemic (lost blood flow), won't heal without nutrients and white cells, and can lead to losing toes. Smoking and PAD compound the risk.
  • Know diabetic foot care (expect a select-all question): for example no commercial remedies for calluses; a mild foot powder or lanolin is OK; well-fitting shoes.
  • Monofilament test (a bit outdated): measures how much sensation is lost on the bottom of the feet.

Nutrition, Exercise and the Hospitalized Patient

Nutrition

  • A balanced, high-fiber, low-fat, low-cholesterol diet (especially type 2).
  • Don't avoid carbs: about 45 to 65% of daily calories, from whole grains, fruits, legumes and milk; limit simple carbs like pasta and bread (never "never eat pasta").
  • Low saturated and trans fat; polyunsaturated fats (fish, nuts) are best.
  • Fiber (beans, vegetables, oats, whole grains) improves carbohydrate metabolism and lowers cholesterol.
  • Protein about 15 to 20%, from lean meats like chicken and fish (red meat has a higher fat content).
  • Alcohol: limit to 1 a day for women, 2 for men; it can raise sugar and then cause a rebound drop.
  • Alternative sweeteners don't raise blood sugar and are acceptable.
  • UK uses consistent carbohydrate diets; type 1 patients often carb count (dosing insulin per 15 g of carbs).

Exercise and the hospital

  • Exercise is incredibly important, but it can lower blood sugar. Don't exercise below 80 or above 250. Best after a meal; a carbohydrate snack first if it's been over an hour since eating or before high-intensity exercise.
  • Medical alert bracelet; well-fitting shoes.
  • Hospitalized: stress and surgery push sugars much higher than at home (reassure them it's normal). Wound healing is impaired and infection risk is higher: watch the WBC count, fever and abnormal exudate.
  • Skin changes from uncontrolled hyperglycemia (reddish-brown spots on the legs, brown-black thickening in skin folds, red patches around blood vessels): not life-threatening, but preventable, and in someone undiagnosed, a reason to check the A1C.
