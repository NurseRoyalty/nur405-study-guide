Chronic Venous Insufficiency & Venous Thromboembolism

Built from the last Week 3 voiceover: the two venous disorders. Veins are a low-pressure system with valves whose job is to get blood back to the heart; when they can't, you get venous congestion, pooling and peripheral edema.

Arterial vs venous disease (pulses, edema, ulcers, skin, color) covered again here — review in HF, PAD, AAA & Raynaud's if needed

Chronic Venous Insufficiency (CVI)

What it looks like

  • Varicose veins (tortuous vessels): a venous finding.
  • Lipodermatosclerosis: thick, leathery skin of the lower leg with a characteristic brownish ("brawny") discoloration.
  • Pitting edema.
  • Eczema with itching.
  • Patients can have both arterial and venous disease, with a mixture of cues.

Venous ulcers

  • Typically above the medial malleolus; they look and behave differently from arterial ulcers.
  • Can be quite painful, worse when dependent (arterial ulcers often aren't, from lost nerve endings).
  • More common than arterial ulcers.
  • Untreated: deep, wide ulcers at increased risk for infection.

CVI Collaborative Care

Nursing measures

  • Avoid standing or sitting for long periods (impedes venous return).
  • Elevate the legs above the level of the heart: promotes venous return and decreases swelling.
  • A daily walking program (keeps both venous and arterial circulation moving).
  • Proper foot and leg care.
  • High-calorie, high-protein diet for skin healing.

Compression therapy

  • Two types, static and dynamic; all of them promote venous return by squeezing blood back to the heart. Static = graded compression from distal to proximal.
  • Prescription stockings may need a vascular specialist to fit them. A stocking donner helps put them on correctly.
  • White TED hose are seen less: put on wrong, they can impede blood flow.
  • SCDs (intermittent compression devices) are used for venous insufficiency, but are not recommended with an actual VTE (they can move the clot).
Elevate for veins, not for arteries. Raising the legs above the heart helps venous return, but in arterial disease it turns the legs pale.

Venous Thromboembolism (VTE)

What used to be called a DVT and a PE is now lumped together as a VTE. We can anticipate them, so the goal is prevention: target the modifiable risk factors.

The Three FactorsRisk Factors From the Lecture
Venous stasisOlder age, bed rest, heart failure, a fractured limb, long trips without adequate exercise, obesity, pregnancy, varicose veins.
Endothelial damageCaustic or hypertonic IV fluids or drugs, fractures of the pelvis, hip or leg, IV drug abuse, trauma.
HypercoagulabilityDehydration or malnutrition (modify by encouraging fluids), high altitude, oral contraceptives, pregnancy, cancer, tobacco use.

Clinical manifestations

  • Unilateral leg edema: one leg larger than the other is concerning. Bilateral edema is unlikely to be a clot (more likely venous insufficiency).
  • Can be painful or tender to palpation (not always).
  • Dilated superficial veins; a sense of fullness or heaviness in the thigh or calf.
  • Numbness; warm, reddened skin and a low-grade temp (inflammation).
  • Usually the lower extremity.

Nursing care

  • Early and aggressive mobilization (patients with clots used to be kept in bed; not necessarily anymore). If not, turn every 2 hours.
  • Flex and extend the feet, hips and knees every 2 to 4 hours while awake: mimics the skeletal muscle pump.
  • Anticoagulation is very important, but it puts them at risk for bleeding.
  • Teach how to minimize the modifiable risk factors.
The most serious complication of a thrombus is a pulmonary embolism: the clot leaves where it started and ends up where it can be fatal.

Recurrent VTEs: a vena cava (Greenfield) filter can be placed in the inferior vena cava: a little cage that catches clots the patient still throws (the lecture says the clot "never gets to the brain"). Less common now; provider preference.
