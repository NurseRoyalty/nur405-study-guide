Heart Failure, Peripheral Arterial Disease, Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm & Raynaud's

Built from three Week 3 voiceovers: heart failure, PAD, and AAA with Raynaud's. PAD, AAA and Raynaud's are the three arterial disorders; the two venous ones are on the next page.

Heart Failure: The Basics

Know the circulation well, or left- and right-sided failure get mixed up: right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonic valve, pulmonary artery, lungs (oxygenated), left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve, aorta, body. Right-sided failure backs blood up on the right side; left-sided failure backs it up on the left. The manifestations depend on where the backup happens.

CauseWhat It Means
PreloadThe blood sitting in the heart during diastole. More blood volume = more preload.
AfterloadThe resistance the left ventricle must overcome to get blood out. High blood pressure raises afterload and, untreated, causes heart failure.
Myocardial contractilityThe muscle itself can't contract, regardless of fluid volume (for example a myocardium damaged by an MI).
Heart rateToo fast or too slow for a long period of time.

Ejection fraction

  • Stroke volume divided by the end-diastolic volume (the blood in the left ventricle before ejection).
  • Normal 55 to 70%. 40% or lower: heart failure. Very, very low is incompatible with life: an LVAD or heart transplant candidate.

HFrEF vs HFpEF (you'll see these in clinical)

  • HFrEF (reduced EF): a defect in systolic function: the LV isn't contracting effectively.
  • HFpEF (preserved EF): a defect in diastolic function (filling): the muscle contracts normally but is stiff and doesn't relax; EF normal or near normal.

Left-Sided vs Right-Sided Heart Failure

NCLEX and HESI questions tease out the difference, but it's a closed circuit: left failure eventually causes right failure, and vice versa, so patients often have both.

FeatureLeft-SidedRight-Sided
Where blood backs upLeft atrium and pulmonary veins, so the cues are pulmonaryRight atrium and the venous circulation, everywhere but the lungs
CuesPulmonary congestion; crackles, rhonchi, dyspnea (on exertion or at rest); orthopnea (tripod position); paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND); blood-tinged sputum; restless, confused; tachycardia; fatigue; possibly cyanoticJVD (the first place it goes, right outside the right atrium); hepatomegaly (tender RUQ) and splenic engorgement; ascites (third spacing); nausea and no appetite (engorged GI tract); peripheral edema (settles with gravity in the ankles; can be in the hands); scrotal edema in men (can be very painful); fatigue

Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND)

  • Paroxysmal = abrupt onset without warning; nocturnal = night; dyspnea = short of breath.
  • Asleep, then suddenly wakes and has to sit up to catch their breath: a sign of advanced left-sided failure.

Cor pulmonale is different

  • Enlargement of the right heart from pulmonary hypertension (for example in COPD): a type of right heart failure.
  • Right-sided failure that results from left-sided failure is not cor pulmonale.

Chronic HF vs Acute Decompensated HF

Chronic heart failure

  • Typically diagnosed outpatient; periods of acutely or slowly worsening function.
  • Often the consequence of damage from other cardiac events (CAD, hypertension). Can be insidious ("I've been feeling fatigued").

Acute decompensated (ADHF)

  • Diagnosed inpatient; no time to compensate. Can be life-threatening and need urgent therapy.
  • They may have 4+ edema and lots of ascites, but what brings them in is that they can't breathe.

Managing Chronic Heart Failure

Nursing measures

  • Target the underlying cause (hypertension: antihypertensives).
  • Oxygen 2 to 6 L (home, temporary or PRN) for dyspnea and fatigue.
  • Energy conservation and minimizing oxygen demand, but not sedentary: maintain activity.
  • Daily weights: same time of day, same scale, about the same clothing. Weight is the most consistent measure of fluid volume status.
  • Low-sodium diet ("salt sucks"): less water retention.

Drug therapy

  • ACE inhibitors and ARBs.
  • Beta blockers (carvedilol is usually the one).
  • Diuretics to get the fluid off.
  • Nitrates (coronary vasodilators such as nitroglycerin).
  • Digoxin (a cardiac glycoside): second line because of dysrhythmia risk, but HESI and NCLEX love it.
  • Spironolactone: a potassium-sparing diuretic, used here for suppressing sodium and water retention to offload the left ventricle; linked to fewer hospitalizations and cardiac deaths. It retains potassium and can worsen renal failure (loop and thiazide diuretics are potassium-losing).

Digoxin

What it does

  • Negative chronotrope: slows the heart rate (chrono = time, rate).
  • Positive inotrope: increases contractility.
  • Together: enhances cardiac output.
  • Narrow therapeutic index. Level 0.5 to 2 (won't be asked).

Digitoxicity

  • Digitalis + hypokalemia = digitoxicity. Monitor potassium, especially on a potassium-losing diuretic.
  • Signs: serious dysrhythmias and bradycardias, halos around lights, nausea and vomiting.
  • Antidote: Digibind.
Before every digoxin dose: take an apical heart rate for a full minute and hold it if the rate is below 60 ("commit to memory"). Teach the patient to take and record their own pulse daily.

LVAD (left ventricular assist device; not tested)

  • A bridge to a heart transplant, or destination therapy (prolonging life with no transplant planned).
  • Continuous flow: BP can't be read by machine, so take it manually with a Doppler. No S1 and S2, just a humming sound.
  • Unresponsive? Don't start CPR until you're sure the pump is completely off. Lots of patient and family teaching.

Acute decompensated HF care

  • Treat the cause; close vital signs and urine output (poor kidney perfusion lowers it); continuous telemetry and pulse oximetry; ABGs.
  • High Fowler's with the feet horizontal or dangling: decreases venous return and preload, easing the heart's workload.
  • BiPAP, face mask or non-rebreather; daily weights.
  • Meds: diuretics, vasodilators, morphine (decreases preload and afterload), positive inotropes such as digoxin.
  • Very sick: ICU and hemodynamic monitoring (not covered in med surg).

Arteries vs Veins

Arteries

  • A high-pressure system carrying oxygenated blood away from the heart.
  • Where the peripheral pulses come from. A needle in an artery gushes; we only stick arteries for ABGs or arterial lines.

Veins

  • A low-pressure system carrying deoxygenated blood back to the heart (often uphill from the legs).
  • Veins have valves to help venous return. IVs go in veins.

Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)

Blockages in the arteries of the periphery: the same idea as coronary artery disease. Risk factors: atherosclerosis plus smoking, diabetes, a really bad diet, elevated CRP, uncontrolled hypertension, family history. Put three or four together and PAD is no surprise.

Intermittent claudication

  • The classic PAD symptom: legs hurt when I walk, pain stops when I stop.
  • Why: perfusion is already poor, and the skeletal muscle pump of walking squeezes off some of the little arterial flow there is.
  • The pain's location matches where the occlusion is. Severe PAD: rest pain, a constant ache in bed.

Diagnosis

  • Doppler ultrasound: degree of blood flow.
  • Ankle brachial index (ABI): a non-invasive screening tool, office or bedside. "Yes, you do need to know how to do it."

Calculating the Ankle Brachial Index

Take a systolic pressure in all four extremities with a handheld Doppler. For one leg: that ankle's pressure divided by the higher of the two arm pressures.

PAD Collaborative Care

Medical and nursing care

  • Risk factor modification for the modifiable ones.
  • Statins, antihypertensives, possibly antiplatelets.
  • Pletal: specific for intermittent claudication; inhibits platelet aggregation and increases vasodilation.
  • Walk to the point of pain, stop and rest, then resume: builds collateral circulation.
  • Meticulous foot care: inspect the feet regularly (a nail stepped on may go unnoticed).

Procedures

  • Catheter-based (cath lab, usually the femoral artery; an alternative to open surgery): angioplasty (balloon), stent, atherectomy (plaque removal), angioplasty plus cold therapy. Like PCI for the heart.
  • Peripheral arterial bypass (like a CABG), named for what's bypassed: a fem-pop (femoral popliteal) bypass with a synthetic graft.
  • Amputation: the last resort.
After a PAD procedure: a meticulous peripheral vascular assessment, trended. Notify the provider of a dramatic increase in pain, a loss of pulse, extreme pallor, or any change. Avoid the knee-flexed position (impedes arterial flow). Early ambulation; meticulous foot care.

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)

An aneurysm is a permanent outpouching of the vessel wall. The aorta is a likely spot because of the incredibly high pressure it withstands. AAAs are often in the descending aorta.

Manifestations and risk

  • Usually no symptoms; often found on a routine exam: a mass, a bruit heard with the bell over the abdomen (you should not hear a bruit), or back pain.
  • The concern is rupture: life-threatening in a high-pressure system.
  • Familial tendency: monitor early in life if a family member had one.

Management

  • Small (under 4 cm): watchful waiting: decrease risk factors, keep the BP down, monitor the size annually.
  • Larger: surgery, ideally elective, because after a rupture mortality is very high (usually no time to reach the OR).

Raynaud's Phenomenon

What it is

  • An autoimmune vasospastic disorder of the small cutaneous arteries, usually the fingers (sometimes the toes).
  • May or may not occur with other diseases (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis). A young woman's disease.
  • Color changes from vasospasm: white, blue, sometimes red.
  • Triggers: cold, emotional stress, tobacco, caffeine.
  • Lasts a minute to hours: cold and numb, then throbbing, aching, tingling as perfusion returns.

Nursing management

  • Layered clothing, gloves; avoid extreme temperatures.
  • Immerse the hands in warm water to ease the spasms.
  • Avoid the triggers.
  • Severe: a slow-release calcium channel blocker, the first-line therapy, for the vessel spasms, not to lower BP.
