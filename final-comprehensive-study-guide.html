Inflammation Markers: CRP & ESR

CRP (C-reactive protein)

  • Indicates inflammation in the body; non-specific (it doesn't say where).
  • More sensitive than the ESR.
  • Peaks within 18 to 72 hours after an MI; failure to normalize might indicate ongoing damage to the heart muscle.
  • With an MI there is inflammation in addition to heart cell death.
  • Monitors more closely for the acute picture.

ESR (sed rate)

  • Also a non-specific indicator of inflammation; could possibly be infection.
  • More useful for monitoring chronic inflammation.
  • CRP and ESR often go hand in hand.
  • Neither tells you where in the body the inflammation is (it could be an arthritis flare, for instance).

BNP & Pro-BNP

Brain natriuretic peptide

  • Despite the word "brain," the main source of BNP is the ventricles of the heart.
  • Helps distinguish cardiac versus respiratory causes of dyspnea: a short-of-breath patient in the ED, where the provider wants to know if it is heart failure or respiratory failure.
  • An elevated BNP indicates left ventricular dysfunction.
  • Pro-BNP is what the instructor sees most often at UK. Plasma concentrations of BNP and pro-BNP are similar, but with left ventricular dysfunction (left-sided heart failure) pro-BNP rises much more significantly than BNP.
  • No ranges given: you don't need to memorize numbers for this, and NCLEX won't expect you to.
Rule of thumb from the lecture: BNP or pro-BNP elevated, think heart failure. Troponin elevated, think myocardial infarction.

Cholesterol & Lipoproteins

Total cholesterol

  • The main lipid associated with cardiovascular disease.
  • We eat it and we also manufacture it; high cholesterol can be inherited (familial).
  • Done as part of a lipid profile; not a totally accurate predictor of heart disease by itself.
  • Diurnal variation: a morning reading can differ from an afternoon reading.
  • Affected by malnutrition: if it is very low, also look at the serum albumin to see how malnourished the patient is.

HDL and LDL

  • An accurate predictor of heart disease.
  • HDL = the "good" cholesterol; removes cholesterol in the body.
  • LDL = the "bad" cholesterol; deposits cholesterol in the peripheral tissue.
  • We like high HDLs and low LDLs.
  • The values help determine whether the patient is low, moderate, or high risk for CAD.
The one number to know: total cholesterol less than 200. The instructor does not ask you to memorize the LDL, HDL, or triglyceride numbers; a test question would speak in general terms.

12-Lead EKG vs Telemetry

12-lead EKG

  • 12 perspectives at once.
  • A snapshot in time.
  • Can diagnose an MI.

Telemetry

  • A one-way perspective.
  • Continuous and ongoing.
  • Monitors only; cannot diagnose.

Ejection Fraction

Definition and values

  • The amount of blood pumped out of the left ventricle with each contraction; an indication of how effectively the heart is pumping.
  • Measured with an echocardiogram, and (per the lecture) also with a right-sided heart cath.
  • Normal: 55% to 70% of the blood sitting in the ventricle at the end of diastole is ejected (about half or a little more).
  • Low values, such as 40%, are seen in heart failure. A really, really low ejection fraction can mean the patient might need a heart transplant.
  • Very important information for a patient who might have heart failure.

Left vs Right Heart Cath

Left heart cath

  • Enter an artery (femoral, radial, or brachial).
  • The catheter goes retrograde up into the ascending aorta / arch of the aorta.
  • The goal is always to get to the coronary arteries; this is the absolute only way to reach them.
  • Used when a patient has chest pain or angina and the question is coronary occlusion.
  • Places examined: the aorta, right coronary artery, left anterior descending, circumflex, and left main coronary artery; the left ventricle sits at the apex.

Right heart cath

  • Enter the venous system.
  • Can measure ejection fraction and look at the pressures of the right side of the heart.
  • Cannot look at the coronary arteries.
  • Sometimes only one side is done; a lot of times both are done.

Before the Heart Cath

Pre-procedure

  • NPO; meds with sips of water may be allowed (provider decision).
  • Signed consent (definitely invasive); the patient and family need to understand the procedure and what to expect.
  • Very little pain, because blood vessels don't have nerve fibers.
  • Warn the patient about the warm flush / hot flash when the contrast dye is injected; it is an expected finding, so they don't think something is wrong.
  • Pre-procedure checklist; monitor BUN and creatinine (dye can be nephrotoxic); empty the bladder before going.
  • Local anesthesia and likely IV sedation to keep them from being anxious.

Contrast Nephrotoxicity

Acute kidney injury after dye

  • An acute kidney injury (AKI) that happens after a dye injection; a major cause of AKI in the hospitalized patient.
  • Increased risk with existing renal problems, diabetes, or fluid volume deficit.
  • Acetylcysteine, and sometimes sodium bicarbonate, are given before the procedure to offset the elevated BUN and creatinine.
  • Encourage the patient to force fluids afterward to dilute the dye going through the kidneys.

Part 2 — Week 1: CAD & Acute Coronary Syndrome

Coronary Arteries & Terminology

The basics

  • Coronary heart disease, coronary artery disease (CAD), and ischemic heart disease are used interchangeably.
  • The coronary arteries perfuse the heart, getting oxygen to the heart muscle. We want patent coronary arteries.
  • You can predict what area of the heart an artery perfuses from the anatomy. The instructor is not going to ask you to memorize the arteries, but the providers look at a few main ones: the left main, the left anterior descending (LAD), the circumflex, and the right coronary artery.
  • The left main and LAD perfuse the left ventricle, the powerhouse chamber. Complete blockage there is sometimes called the "widow maker", because those patients often never make it to the hospital.
  • Atherosclerosis ("hardening of the arteries") is responsible for almost all cases of CAD, regardless of type.

The CAD Continuum

Think of coronary artery disease as a continuum, from chronic ischemic heart disease to acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Once it starts looking like unstable angina, it is collectively called ACS, because time is of the essence if it advances to an MI.

Risk Factors for CAD

Non-modifiable

  • Age: the older someone gets, the more likely CAD is.
  • Gender: males are at greater risk than females.
  • Family history: especially relatives who developed CAD younger.

The four most firmly established modifiable

  • Elevated serum lipids (cholesterol, triglycerides); we want high HDLs and low LDLs.
  • Hypertension: untreated, the increased arterial pressure causes damage over time (the hardening). It can be silent and asymptomatic, so people don't perceive it as a big deal. Patients with CKD or diabetes have an even lower BP goal.
  • Tobacco use: risk is proportional to the number of cigarettes smoked.
  • Physical inactivity.

Other modifiable risk factors

  • Obesity: apple-shaped people (rotund in the belly) are at higher risk than pear-shaped people (hips wider than the abdomen).
  • Diabetes mellitus: poor glucose control aggravates coronary heart disease.
  • Depression or prolonged stress.

Chronic Stable Angina

Angina means chest pain from a temporary loss of oxygenation to the heart muscle, more often than not from an occluded coronary artery (but you can't know without the tests).

Where the pain can be

  • Substernal, possibly radiating up to the neck.
  • The classic: substernal, radiating down the left arm. It can radiate down both arms.
  • Epigastric (troponins help rule cardiac in or out; sometimes it is just gastric reflux).
  • Mid-scapular. The instructor once had a patient with pain in the elbows: it is not exactly predictable.

What makes it "stable"

  • Occurs with moderate to prolonged exertion, in a pattern familiar to the patient.
  • Frequency, duration, and intensity stay pretty much stable.
  • Only slight limitations in activity; regulated on medication.
  • Usually associated with fixed atherosclerotic plaque.
  • Relieved by rest and/or nitroglycerin.
  • Managed with drug therapy; rarely requires aggressive therapy.

Diagnosing Stable Angina

Workup

  • Physical exam and complete history; EKG.
  • Exercise stress test, or a pharmacological stress test if they can't use the treadmill or bike: a drug with positive inotropic and chronotropic effects (increased contractility and heart rate) to stress the heart on purpose.
  • Low and intermediate risk results: manage symptoms without an intervention.
  • High risk results: more testing, such as cardiac catheterization followed by revascularization (PCI or CABG), and/or an echo or TEE.

Goals and Medications for Stable Angina

Goal: decrease the workload on the heart and increase the oxygen supply, targeting the modifiable risk factors as appropriate.

Teaching about sublingual nitro

  • Take it at the beginning of chest pain, or prophylactically before activity known to cause it (walking, bike riding, sexual activity).
  • Works in less than 3 minutes; keep it accessible (in your pocket).
  • Protect it from light (brown bottle); replace the bottle every 6 months.
  • It should tingle as it dissolves; no tingle may mean it has expired. Continued pain or no tingle: activate EMS.
  • Never combine with erectile dysfunction drugs; this can be fatal.

Side effects (all from vasodilation)

  • Headache (not a reason to skip the nitro).
  • Lowered BP: fainting and postural hypotension.
  • Flushing.
Always know the blood pressure before giving nitroglycerin, because it is going to lower it. A patient with a low baseline (for example 90/60) can plummet. If the BP is too low, no more nitro is given; something different, like IV morphine, may be used.

Unstable Angina

How it differs from stable angina

  • Often occurs at rest, unannounced and unprecipitated; usually lasts more than 20 minutes.
  • New onset that markedly limits physical activity (stops the person in their tracks).
  • Increasing angina: more frequent, longer, with less exertion than the patient's previous stable angina.
  • Poorly relieved by rest or nitroglycerin.
  • Unpredictable and unstable. Angina is an emergency because unstable angina precedes an MI.

Atypical Presentations

Women and people with diabetes

  • Symptoms are sometimes atypical: no classic radiation down the left arm.
  • Fatigue is a more prominent manifestation (a very big symptom); shortness of air; indigestion; anxiety.
  • Older patients may also present atypically.
  • A silent MI is one the person didn't know they had. Most MIs are not silent.

Angina Pain vs MI Pain

Angina pain

  • Precipitated by exertion or stress.
  • Relieved by rest and/or sublingual nitroglycerin.
  • Lasts less than 15 minutes.

MI pain

  • Occurs without a cause; sometimes wakes people from early-morning sleep (the instructor has read 4 to 6 a.m. is peak, possibly tied to the normal morning cortisol surge).
  • Relieved only by opioids (morphine).
  • Lasts 20 minutes or longer.
  • Often with nausea, diaphoresis, shortness of breath, and marked anxiety; can be associated with dysrhythmias.
  • Severe and immobilizing; not relieved by rest, position change, or nitrates.
  • Described as heaviness, pressure, tightness, burning, constriction, crushing ("an elephant sitting on my chest").
Ask about pressure, not just pain. A cardiac patient may say no to "pain in your chest" and yes to "pressure in your chest." Choose words the patient understands.

How an MI Evolves

Time is muscle

  • Cells can stand about 20 minutes of ischemia before cell death starts.
  • The subendocardial layer is affected first; it takes about 4 to 6 hours for the entire thickness of the heart to necrose. (Chest pain at noon means full-thickness damage around 4 to 6 p.m.)
  • That is why ACS patients need to activate EMS and get to the hospital: waiting costs heart muscle.
  • The location of the MI correlates with the blocked coronary artery (an LAD blockage damages the apex, where the left ventricle is).
  • MIs are named by location: anterior, lateral, inferior, septal, posterior (for example, an inferior or inferolateral MI). You don't need to know this for the test; it is lingo you may hear.

Diagnosing ACS: EKG Changes

The ST segment

  • We look for changes in the QRS, the ST segment, and the T wave (more in the dysrhythmia content).
  • At rest the ST segment should sit on the isoelectric line.
  • ST elevation = above the isoelectric line; with a positive troponin, that is a STEMI. Points to infarction (cell death, permanent).
  • ST depression = below the isoelectric line; sometimes happens with ischemia, sometimes infarction. Points to ischemia (temporary).
  • A STEMI shows ST elevation in two anatomically contiguous leads (contiguous = sharing a common border, touching each other).
  • A STEMI cannot be diagnosed by telemetry; it takes a 12-lead EKG.
  • Serial EKGs may be ordered because ischemia and infarction can change over a few hours.

Initial Care of the ACS Patient

Assess and stabilize

  • Consider ACS with chest discomfort, shortness of air, or other suggestive symptoms; remember atypical presentations in women, older patients, and patients with diabetes.
  • 12-lead EKG within 10 minutes of arrival; repeat every 10 to 15 minutes if not definitive and MI is still suspected.
  • Airway, breathing, circulation. Sit the patient upright to support ventilation; supplemental oxygen per nasal cannula.
  • Baseline vital signs ASAP, including O2 saturation.
  • Telemetry ASAP to monitor for dysrhythmias (the number one complication) and ST changes. If you notice ST elevation on tele, the next best nursing action is a 12-lead EKG (cardiac nurses usually have a standing order).
  • IV access, for emergency or code medications.
  • Aspirin to chew and swallow (antiplatelet).
  • Assess pain with PQRST.

Pain Relief in ACS

Nitroglycerin first

  • 0.4 mg sublingual is pretty standard, one at a time, spaced 5 minutes apart for persistent chest pain.
  • Check BP before each dose; it is likely to drop (a potent coronary vasodilator lowers peripheral vascular resistance).
  • If BP gets too low, switch to morphine.

Morphine

  • Eases the workload on the heart.
  • Decreases preload and afterload.
  • Helps with anxiety.
  • Patients often tolerate IV morphine better than nitroglycerin.

Labs and Ongoing Assessment

What to monitor

  • Cardiac markers: troponin is the most specific to cardiac muscle (some providers still order CK-MB or myoglobin).
  • Electrolytes, especially potassium, calcium, and magnesium: imbalances can be the root cause of dysrhythmias.
  • Hemoglobin and hematocrit; coagulation status (bleeding risk).
  • Heart sounds: listen for a murmur, gallop, or rub.
  • Lung sounds: an infarction in the left ventricle can lead to heart failure, and crackles could point to left-sided heart failure.

PCI (Angioplasty, the "Balloon Procedure")

What happens

  • The heart cath shows whether blockages exist, where, how bad, and whether there is collateral circulation (little vessels the body grew around the blockage to perfuse the other side). It also evaluates left ventricular function.
  • It is a left heart cath (through the brachial or femoral artery) to reach the coronary arteries.
  • A guide wire and balloon catheter go to the occlusion; the balloon inflates and smashes the plaque back against the wall, then deflates and is removed.
  • Sometimes a stent is placed to reestablish patency.

Advantages over open-heart surgery

  • Percutaneous, through the vascular system; no cracking the chest.
  • Local anesthesia, not general.
  • Ambulatory shortly after.
  • Significantly shorter hospital stay; back to work and life sooner.
  • More PCIs are performed than CABGs.

PCI Nursing Care

What the CAD lecture adds for PCI

  • Monitor closely for MI progression: chest pain, EKG changes, new or different dysrhythmias, hemodynamic instability (unstable BP and heart rate).
  • Dual antiplatelet therapy: aspirin plus a drug like clopidogrel (Plavix). Possibly IV heparin during the procedure. It is a delicate balance; we don't want the patient to bleed out.
  • The sheath is usually removed 4 to 6 hours after the procedure; an arterial puncture needs manual pressure for a good 20 minutes.
  • Assess distal to the puncture: for a left groin site, compare the left foot's DP and PT pulses and cap refill to the right foot.
  • Watch for bleeding externally (the dressing) and internally: a heart rate creeping up can be the body compensating for lost blood volume.

Thrombolytic Therapy ("Clot Busters")

The drugs

  • Fibrinolytics: they lyse fibrin to dissolve thrombi in the coronary arteries and restore blood flow.
  • Examples: tPA and reteplase.
  • Per the lecture, given during the cardiac catheterization.
  • PCI is more commonly performed than thrombolytic therapy.
  • Monitor closely for bleeding after administration.

Who should NOT get them

  • History of a brain bleed or stroke, particularly hemorrhagic stroke.
  • Recent abdominal surgery (bleeding risk).
  • Active bleeding.
  • Coagulation studies that are out of range.

CABG (Coronary Artery Bypass Graft)

The procedure

  • The surgeon sews (anastomoses) a graft proximal and distal to the blockage, literally bypassing it. Up to six grafts (for example "CABG times one," "CABG times three").
  • The patient's own vessels are used, such as the internal mammary artery or the saphenous vein (the long vein from the groin down the medial leg to the ankle). Veins have valves, so the graft is turned in favor of blood flow. Which vessel is the surgeon's preference; nothing you need to know as the nurse.
  • Valve replacement is sometimes done at the same time.

Ongoing Care & Recovery

After the procedure

  • Continuous telemetry; rest, comfort, activity restriction for a while; good pain control.
  • Expect anxiety; teach at the level where the patient is, since everyone is in a different place. The nurse can be a game changer.
  • Timing matters for emotional and behavioral reactions. Example: resuming sexual activity, especially if that is what brought them in. Have those conversations before discharge so they understand their limitations.
  • Needs at home span physiological, psychological, mental, spiritual, economic, and vocational areas.

Part 3 — Week 1: Pericarditis

From Pericarditis. The first of three voiceovers on inflammatory, infective, and valvular heart disease. The friction-rub details from the CAD lecture (where pericarditis came up as an MI complication) are gathered here too, so the rub lives in one place.

The Pericardium

Layers and the potential space

  • The pericardium covers the heart. "-itis" means inflammation or infection: with pericarditis think inflammation (endocarditis is more about infection).
  • The fibrous pericardium is the outside; it circles back around into the visceral and parietal pericardium, with the pericardial space in between.
  • The pericardial space is a potential space holding usually 5 to 10 mL of fluid, just enough to keep the heart lubricated and smooth while it pumps.
  • Beneath are the myocardium and the endocardium.

Causes

CauseWhat the Lecture Says
IdiopathicOften we really don't know why it happens.
Infective organismsA variety sometimes precede pericarditis.
After an MIA complication of an MI; usually shows up a few days after, from the MI's inflammation.
Connective tissue diseasesThe pericardium is connective tissue. Examples: rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and lupus.

Pericarditis Pain

How it presents

  • Substernal / precordial (behind the breastbone), radiating to the left side of the neck, shoulder, and back.
  • A grating, oppressive feel (oppressive: hard to catch your breath).
  • Aggravated by breathing (mainly inspiration), coughing, and swallowing.
  • Worse lying down; better sitting up and leaning forward (leaning forward minimizes the stretch on the pericardium). A positional thing.

Pericarditis Pain vs MI Pain

After an MI you have to tell the two apart, since pericarditis is an MI complication.

Pericarditis pain

  • Onset usually more sudden.
  • Substernal, left precordial; may radiate.
  • Sharp and stabbing, or aching and dull; oppressive.
  • Worse on inspiration.
  • Body movement can increase it; leaning forward makes it better.
  • Can wax and wane.
  • Sublingual nitroglycerin won't help: nitro is a coronary vasodilator, and pericarditis has nothing to do with coronary vasodilation.

MI pain

  • Position is not going to matter.
  • Breathing isn't going to impact the pain.
  • Ischemia is what nitroglycerin addresses.

The Pericardial Friction Rub

The hallmark finding of pericarditis is the pericardial friction rub.

What it sounds like and why

  • A "squeaky heart"; a grating, continuous grinding sound, like two pieces of sandpaper rubbing together.
  • The visceral and parietal pericardium normally have a little fluid between them. Inflammation takes away that fluidity, and the two layers rub (CAD lecture).
  • Same idea as a pleural friction rub, when the visceral and parietal pleura are inflamed (for example, pleurisy).

How to listen

  • Stethoscope at the lower left sternal border.
  • Patient leaning forward (brings the heart closer to the chest wall).
  • Ask the patient to hold their breath to eliminate breath sounds and focus on the heart.
  • From the CAD lecture: if the grating disappears when the patient holds their breath, it is not cardiac in origin (it is respiratory).

Complications: Pericardial Effusion & Tamponade

Pericardial Effusion

Fluid in the pericardial space

  • Pericardial effusion is to the pericardium what pleural effusion is to the pleural space: third spacing, a collection of fluid that serves absolutely no purpose.
  • Symptoms come from the fluid squeezing the heart.
  • Cues: cough, tachypnea, difficulty breathing, and heart sounds that aren't crisp (fluid between the stethoscope and the heart).
  • The pericardial space doesn't expand much. As it fills, the heart gets squished, can't contract and release like it should, and cardiac output suffers.
  • Seen on an echocardiogram (or TEE), which delineates the heart's structures.

Part 4 — Week 1: Infective Endocarditis

From Infective Endocarditis. With pericarditis think inflammation; with endocarditis think infection. "Infective endocarditis" is the longer name, but it is usually just called endocarditis.

What It Is

The endocardium and the valves

  • The endocardium is the inside of the heart, which is a cue to where the problem is.
  • The valves are a very movable part of the heart, and a lot of the manifestations of endocarditis relate to the valves.
  • Endocarditis occurs when blood flow within the heart lets the organism infect previously damaged valves or another endothelial surface.

Subacute vs Acute

Subacute

  • Affects those with pre-existing heart problems.
  • Typically milder in presentation.
  • Can be more chronic.

Acute

  • Affects healthy valves.
  • Manifests itself quicker.
  • Therapy needs to be prompt and vigorous.

Who Gets It & What Causes It

Two things have to happen

  • A history of damaged epithelium, such as a valve replacement or a defect.
  • A port of entry for infection to reach the endocardium: a sore, a dental procedure, IV drug use.

Causative organisms

  • Most common: Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus viridans (bacterial).
  • Other possible pathogens: fungi and viruses, so not all endocarditis is bacterial.

Vegetations & Emboli

Why vegetations matter

  • Infection on the moving valves develops vegetations, for example mitral valve vegetation between the left atrium and left ventricle. Bacterial endocarditis of the mitral valve covers it with large, irregular vegetations.
  • The valves are constantly opening and closing, so vegetations can slough off and become emboli, dispersed into the vascular system.

Left-sided vegetation

  • Leaves the heart into the organs.
  • Can cause a limb infarction or an infarction in any organ of the body.

Right-sided vegetation

  • Blood from the right side goes to the lungs.
  • Like a pulmonary embolus.

Manifestations

Very non-specific at first

  • Timing: the transcript says it typically occurs "within 10 two weeks" of the infectious organism (unclear wording in the transcript, so no firm number is given here).
  • Low-grade fever, chills, malaise, fatigue, anorexia, joint pain, muscle pain. If you didn't know better, you'd think it was the flu.
  • Clubbing of the fingers is the odd one out, and it comes later in the disease process, not early.

Who gets prophylaxis

  • A mechanical heart valve.
  • A history of endocarditis.
  • A history of congenital heart disease.
  • A heart transplant.

Before which procedures

  • Any dental visit.
  • A biopsy of any kind.
  • Tonsil and adenoid removal.
  • A wound infection or urinary tract infection.

  • Typically hospitalized initially; medications usually given IV.
  • Complete eradication may take weeks upon weeks; relapses are common.

  • Refer IV drug users to drug rehab.
  • Monitor for the non-specific signs and symptoms that indicate a recurrence.

Part 5 — Week 1: Valvular Heart Disease

From Valvular Heart Disease. The last of the three inflammatory, infective, and valvular voiceovers. The instructor says you really need to know your valves: where they are and what they do.

Valve Review

What the valves do

  • Valves let blood leave one chamber and go into the next. When the next chamber contracts, the valve slams shut, keeping blood moving in a unidirectional path. Stenosis, prolapse, or regurgitation messes with that and causes backflow.
  • AV valves: tricuspid and mitral. Semilunar valves: pulmonic and aortic.
  • The mitral lies between the left atrium and left ventricle; the aortic lies after the left ventricle, before systemic circulation.
  • Left heart valves take more of a beating, because the left side is the high-pressure system. A repaired or replaced valve is, more times than not, the mitral or the aortic.

Replacing the Valve: Mechanical vs Biologic

Mechanical valve

  • More durable; lasts longer.
  • Needs lifetime anticoagulation: the body sees the mechanical pieces as foreign, and clots might form. That is the disadvantage.
  • For someone who will be compliant with lifetime anticoagulation.
  • You will hear a click (it is metal). Some say you can hear it from the bedside without a stethoscope.

Biologic valve (usually porcine, a pig valve)

  • Less durable; doesn't last as long (less than 8 to 10 years).
  • No need for anticoagulation.
  • For someone older with a shorter life expectancy, or someone unwilling or unable to adhere to anticoagulation.
  • No click; you hear only the closure of the valve.
Adventitious heart sounds so far: a rub, a murmur, a gallop, and now a click. Who has a click? Someone with a mechanical valve.

Cataracts

The lens becomes cloudy and stiff (often with age), decreasing visual acuity and the ability to accommodate. Vision is consistently fuzzy, "like there's Vaseline on my eyeball," with trouble from glare (light scatter from the opacities), especially driving. Cataract surgery is the most common surgical procedure for Americans older than 65.

Before surgery: watch and adapt

  • Adjust the glasses prescription; read with extra light; do things close up.
  • Limit driving to daytime (night glare).

When surgery is chosen

  • Those measures stop working and safety is affected.
  • The lens is raising the IOP (which can cause blindness and leads to glaucoma).
  • The cataract blocks the view of the retina in someone who needs regular diabetic retinopathy exams.

The PSA Screening Controversy

Benefits

  • A small survival benefit in randomized trials.
  • When PSA screening first came out (way back in the 90s), its use was associated with a 50% drop in prostate cancer.
  • Early detection of aggressive cancer saves lives.

Harms

  • 48 diagnoses to prevent one death, so 47 men were potentially treated unnecessarily.
  • Only 1 in 3 men with a positive PSA has prostate cancer.
  • Risks of the biopsy and of treatment (surgery, radiation) can outweigh benefits.
  • Overdiagnosis: cancer that never becomes clinically relevant. Many men live and die with prostate cancer, not from it.
Nursing implication: make sure the patient is informed of the risks and benefits of PSA screening before deciding. American Cancer Society: average-risk men begin screening at 50; not recommended over 75 (harms outweigh benefits).

Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

The prostate enlarges and grows inward, narrowing the urethra and disrupting urine flow. Caused by age and androgens; very prevalent ("if a male patient lives long enough, he's going to have BPH").

If the prostate gets large enough, the patient cannot urinate: an emergency.

Irritative symptoms (inflammation, infection)

  • Nocturia, often the first symptom (up 5 or 6 times a night).
  • Frequency, urgency, dysuria, bladder pain, incontinence.

Obstructive symptoms (bladder straining against a narrow urethra)

  • Decreased caliber and force of the stream.
  • Difficulty starting; intermittency (stopping and starting).
  • Dribbling at the end.

Together these are called lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS).

BPH Management

Watch and teach

  • Monitor (labs and diagnostics) unless it gets worse.
  • Avoid decongestants and antihistamines: they tighten the prostate muscles so urine can't easily leave the bladder.
  • Restrict evening fluids (nocturia).
  • Double voiding: void, wait a moment, then try again to empty the rest.

Medications and herbs

  • Finasteride and tamsulosin (different mechanisms), or a combination. Specific drug details are not on this instructor's test (pathopharm).
  • Saw palmetto: no FDA evidence it helps, but it's fine if the provider knows (check for drug-herb interactions).
  • Minimally invasive procedures in Lewis: not on the exam.

  • Clots are expected the first day or two. A large amount of bright red blood may mean hemorrhage; if color worsens for no apparent reason, notify the provider.

Calculating urine output

  • CBI only irrigates the bladder; it never enters the intravascular space, so there is no risk of fluid volume excess from changing the rate.
  • True urine = total drained minus irrigation infused. Example: 400 mL in the bag with 200 mL of irrigation = 200 mL urine.
  • Women can also have CBI if needed.

Prostate Cancer

Incidence rises rapidly after 50; 80% of men who get it are diagnosed after 65. Among the most common cancers in men, but most grows very slowly and many cases never become clinically relevant (men die of other causes). PSA screening was widely adopted before the evidence supported it.

Screening and risk

  • Informed decision about screening at 50.
  • Higher risk: discuss at 45: a first-degree relative with prostate cancer before 65, and African Americans.
  • Screening = PSA with or without a DRE.

Treatment

  • Early stage: watchful waiting with repeated DREs and trended PSAs.
  • Radical prostatectomy (removal of the prostate). Adverse outcomes: erectile dysfunction (gradually returns over a couple of years) and urinary incontinence (gradually returns over a couple of months, but not always). Teach that dribbling can happen.
  • Radiation, alone or with surgery: internal brachytherapy (seeds) and external teletherapy.
  • Androgen deprivation therapy; chemotherapy if metastatic.

Part 8 — Week 2: STDs: Sexually Transmitted Infections

From STDs: Sexually Transmitted Infections. Built from the two Week 2 STI voiceovers: chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomoniasis, genital herpes, genital warts (HPV) and syphilis, plus nursing management. The lecture uses STI and STD interchangeably.

STI Basics

Infectious diseases spread through sexual contact (penis, vagina, mouth) or sexual fluids. Typically very treatable and ultimately curable, but complications can be serious (infertility, cancer) and the psychosocial impact is large. The nurse educates, counsels and refers.

How they spread

  • Mucosa and genitals most susceptible; also rectum and mouth.
  • Direct skin-to-skin contact; blood products; mother to baby through the birth canal.
  • Autoinoculation: touching an infected area, then another, spreads it within your own body.
  • Not spread by casual contact.

Key facts

  • Very common in the US; having one STI increases the risk of others (you can have more than one).
  • Incubation period: infectious before symptoms or a positive test, so asymptomatic spread happens.
  • The male condom is the best protection (besides abstinence, which is unrealistic for most, so safer sex is pushed). Hormonal birth control and IUDs do not prevent STIs.
Three reportable STIs in the US: gonorrhea, chlamydia and syphilis (the three bacterial ones). The lab or provider must notify the health department.

Factors raising incidence

  • Early reproductive maturity, longer sexual lifespan, greater sexual freedom.
  • Inconsistent or incorrect barrier use; media emphasis on sex without safer-sex messages.
  • Substance use (poor decision making).

Higher-risk groups

  • Young people; social or economic disadvantage; drug and alcohol use, shared needles, multiple partners, inconsistent barriers.
  • History of STIs, missed vaccinations, repeated HIV prophylaxis.
  • Men who have sex with men, transgender patients (and, per nursing management, women who have sex with women).
  • Health equity barriers: fear and distrust of providers, poor access to quality care.

Chlamydia

Sites and manifestations

  • Spread through vaginal, anal or oral sex; more likely transmitted man to woman; can be reinfected many times.
  • Men: urethra (urethritis). Women: cervix (cervicitis). Both: rectum and oropharynx.
  • Usually no symptoms (so it keeps spreading).
  • Men sometimes: painful urination, urethral discharge; rarely testicular pain or swelling.
  • Women sometimes: mucopurulent discharge, bleeding, dysuria, pain with intercourse.
  • Rectal: anorectal pain, discharge, bleeding, itching, tenesmus (feeling of incomplete defecation), mucus-coated stools, painful BMs.

Complications

  • Infertility, usually in women, from pelvic inflammatory disease (PID).
  • PID damages the fallopian tubes: ectopic pregnancy risk, chronic pelvic pain, infertility.
  • Regular screening for high-risk populations.

Gonorrhea

Manifestations

  • Prior infection does not prevent reinfection.
  • Men: usually symptomatic within a few days: dysuria, purulent urethral discharge, epididymitis (the epididymis is the duct behind the testes that carries sperm; once on a HESI).
  • Women: usually asymptomatic or minor symptoms (often overlooked): more discharge, burning, frequency, bleeding after sex, red swollen cervix with purulent exudate.
  • Rectal: inflammation, redness, painful BMs.
  • Screen annually (with chlamydia): young sexually active females, men who have sex with men, other high-risk groups.

Complications

  • Men get treated (symptomatic), so fewer complications; possible infertility from epididymitis.
  • Women, being asymptomatic, get more complications: inflamed Bartholin's glands (behind the labia: enlarged, red, irritated labia), PID.
  • Pregnancy: passed to the neonate, who can develop conjunctivitis.
  • Diagnosis: sexual history, exam, culture of any discharge.

Trichomoniasis

Manifestations

  • More common in women; in men three-fourths are asymptomatic and it often resolves within 10 days.
  • Ranges from asymptomatic carrier to acute severe inflammation.
  • Men: burning with urination and ejaculation, urethral discharge.
  • Women: painful urination, vaginal itching, painful intercourse, bleeding after sex, yellow-green, foul-smelling discharge, strawberry cervix (red, inflamed).

Complications and screening

  • Untreated inflammation makes another STI (such as HIV) more likely.
  • Not related to PID.
  • Routine screening for high-risk groups, including those with HIV; screening men not necessarily recommended.

Genital Herpes

HSV-1 above the waist, HSV-2 below (most of the time): HSV-1 = oral-labial disease, cold sores, fever blisters; HSV-2 = anogenital. Both types can infect both sites.

How it behaves

  • Usually spread by someone unaware they're infected. HSV-2 is twice as common in women; Hispanic and Black populations more likely infected.
  • Enters through mucous membranes or broken skin; lives permanently in the dorsal root ganglia and reactivates (outbreaks).
  • Most contagious with symptoms, but asymptomatic viral shedding also transmits it.
  • Lesions: clear, grouped vesicles on an erythematous base; can become pustules and coalesce into ulcers.

Episodes

  • Primary episode (previously seronegative): often no recognizable symptoms; maybe regional lymphadenopathy and flu-like symptoms; about 3 weeks.
  • Recurrences: usually within the first year; less severe, heal faster, fewer over time. Triggers: stress, fatigue, illness, immunosuppression.
  • Greatest transmission risk with active lesions.

Complications: neurologic (blindness, encephalitis, meningitis); genital ulcers raise HIV risk; transfer to the baby in pregnancy; big psychosocial impact (incurable), so counseling. Diagnosed by symptoms, sometimes culture to tell HSV-1 from HSV-2.

Genital Warts (HPV)

  • Benign growths on anogenital skin. HPV is usually transient (clears in 1 to 2 years) but can persist without warts; removing warts doesn't clear the virus or decrease spread.
  • Most people don't know they're infected. Warts are discrete, flesh-colored or hyperpigmented, 1 to 10, can coalesce into a larger mass. Men: penis or scrotum. Women: inner thighs, vulva, vagina.
  • Early lesions are hard to see; warts can be confused with other STI lesions (secondary syphilis can look similar), so rule out the others.
  • Few long-term concerns from warts themselves, but high-risk HPV strains are linked to cancer: more screening.
  • HPV vaccine covers 90% of genital warts and 70 to 90% of the strains that cause cervical cancer; now recommended for everyone. Anal warts are harder to treat than vulvar ones.

Syphilis

Spread by direct contact with a syphilitic ulcer, the chancre (external or internal); can be transmitted in pregnancy. Can have serious long-term complications if not treated early.

Problems and assessment

  • Lack of knowledge; big psychosocial issues (depression, anxiety, isolation); risk for infection.
  • History taking is so important: be aware of gender identity, current anatomy and sexual practices.

  • Promote HPV vaccines, routine testing and prevention.
  • Know your health department's reporting requirements.

Conduction Review

The chambers

  • Atria on top, ventricles on the bottom. Right side pumps to the lungs; left side pumps to the body.
  • The left ventricle is the powerhouse: it's where cardiac output (stroke volume) happens.
  • We can live with quivering atria (atrial fibrillation). We cannot live with quivering ventricles (V-fib).
  • Electrical conduction drives the mechanical pumping; without it, the heart does not pump.

The pathway

  • SA (sinoatrial) node: the initiator, in the right atrium. Intrinsic rate 60 to 100 per minute. Below 60 = bradycardia; above 100 = tachycardia.
  • It fires, a tiny delay, then the atria contract.
  • The impulse reaches the AV (atrioventricular) node, the gateway to the ventricles; a tiny pause.
  • Then the bundle of His and Purkinje fibers, and the ventricles contract.

Dysrhythmias are abnormal cardiac rhythms (the name changed from "arrhythmia," which implies no rhythm at all). They require a prompt assessment, and the nurse decides whether to do something about it.

Reading the Strip: Boxes and Six Seconds

The boxes

  • Each tiny box = 0.04 seconds (you need to know this).
  • Five tiny boxes (one big box) = 0.20 seconds.
  • Used to measure the PR interval and QRS width.

Heart rate from a strip

  • Every test strip is a 6-second strip; the marks at the top are 3 seconds apart, so two of them make 6 seconds.
  • Count the QRSs and multiply by 10: 9 QRSs = a heart rate of 90.

Telemetry

Electrode placement (5-lead)

  • White on the right: right below the right clavicle.
  • Black ("smoke"): just below the clavicle on the MCL.
  • Red ("fire"): smoke over fire.
  • Green ("grass"): lower chest, just to the right of the belly button. Snow (white) over grass.
  • Brown ("chocolate close to my heart"): 4th ICS, right sternal border.
  • Telemetry is 3-lead or 5-lead. Students can put electrodes on and replace ones that come off.

Leads and monitoring

  • Changing the lead at the monitor (lead I, II, III) just changes the "camera angle"; it does nothing to the patient. Lead I reads left to right; lead II (right shoulder to groin) is the usual choice.
  • A strip that looks strange may just be in lead III: change the lead to get a better look.
  • Central monitoring (as at UK) and alarms are not fail-proof.
Something changes on the monitor? Go check on your patient. It might be a lead that fell off, but something else could be going on. Artifact (brushing teeth, chest physiotherapy, moving a lot in bed, a low battery) looks scary but isn't the patient's heart: check the patient, the connections and the electrodes, and replace them if needed.

Classifying Dysrhythmias

Two ways: by early, slow or fast (premature complexes, bradydysrhythmias below 60, tachydysrhythmias above 100), or by the site of origin in the heart (sinus, atrial, ventricular).

Bradydysrhythmia (HR below 60)

  • ☺ Reduces myocardial oxygen demand (why beta blockers slow the rate on purpose).
  • ☺ Longer diastole = more time to perfuse the coronary arteries (they perfuse during diastole).
  • ☹ If too slow, coronary perfusion falls (angina).
  • May be tolerated if the BP is OK (an athlete with a resting HR of 54). If BP isn't adequate: myocardial ischemia or infarct, dysrhythmias, hypotension, heart failure.

Tachydysrhythmia (HR above 100)

  • ☹ Shortens diastole, so less coronary perfusion time (angina).
  • ☺ At first may raise cardiac output and BP (compensation).
  • ☹ If sustained: less ventricular filling, so stroke volume, cardiac output and BP fall. About 104 is usually tolerable; at 120 to 130 this starts happening.
  • ☹ More cardiac workload = more myocardial oxygen demand. Can lead to MI, unstable angina, dysrhythmias, low BP, heart failure.

Causes

  • Vagal (parasympathetic) stimulation: carotid sinus massage (a provider-only procedure), vomiting or gagging, the Valsalva maneuver (bearing down), applying eyeball pressure.
  • Parasympathomimetic drugs (given on purpose to slow the rate).
  • Digoxin toxicity (narrow therapeutic index; can be life threatening).
  • Hyperkalemia (slows depolarization).
  • Myocardial infarction.

What the nurse does

  • HR of 52? Go check on the patient and take a full set of vital signs (BP is the indirect measure of cardiac output).
  • Asymptomatic ("I'm feeling fine") with OK vitals: monitor, don't treat.
  • Symptomatic (lightheaded, nauseous, "I feel like I'm going to pass out," low BP): give atropine.
Symptomatic bradycardia: atropine 1 mg IV every 3 to 5 minutes, 3 mg max (a vagolytic: it blocks the vagus nerve). Still symptomatic? Transcutaneous pacing right here, right now: pads on, push the button, and you essentially create the SA node through the skin. Persistent bradycardia that doesn't respond may eventually need a permanent pacemaker (under the skin below the clavicle; fires when the SA node isn't cooperating, every beat or only some).

Sinus Tachycardia

Causes

  • Exertion (running up the stairs), anxiety, pain, stress, fever.
  • Anemia (not enough "buses," so the heart speeds up to circulate the ones you have); hypoxia.
  • Compensation for decreased BP: cardiac output = heart rate x stroke volume, so low stroke volume raises the HR (for example MI, heart failure).

Treatment: treat the cause

  • Not one size fits all, unlike atropine for bradycardia.
  • Low fluid volume: replace fluid. Pain: analgesic. Fever: antipyretic. Panic attack: anxiolytic (a benzodiazepine).
  • Beta blockers reduce HR and myocardial oxygen demand, but try to treat the cause first.
  • Symptoms if not tolerated: the key-features table (palpitations are the tachy-specific one).

Premature Atrial Contraction (PAC)

  • A one-time early discharge of an ectopic beat outside the SA node: an isolated premature atrial beat. A PAC is to the atria what a PVC is to the ventricles.
  • On the strip: equal R to R, then one early beat, then back to equal R to R (normal sinus rhythm with one PAC).
  • Causes: usually benign; stress, stimulants; electrolyte imbalances; pathology.
  • Benign: do nothing. Pathologic: intervene, with the same medications as for A-fib.
New-onset or increasing PACs in a hospital patient: check the electrolytes (especially magnesium, calcium and potassium) and let the provider know. Pathologic PACs can be a precursor to A-fib, so time can be of the essence: medication may prevent the conversion.

Treating A-Fib and A-Flutter

Number one: control the ventricular rate. Then control the rhythm, and prevent an embolic stroke. Rate is the priority.

Stable but symptomatic

  • Hemodynamically stable (vital signs OK) but symptomatic: slow the ventricular rate with an IV calcium channel blocker, beta blocker, digoxin or amiodarone.
  • Usually a bolus, then a drip: the bolus raises the drug level immediately; a drip alone would take too long. The bolus wears off as the drip takes over.

Unstable

  • Hemodynamically unstable (vital signs unstable, not tolerating it): synchronized cardioversion.
  • It's the choice of therapy for hemodynamically unstable supraventricular tachydysrhythmias (tachy rhythms that start above the ventricle).

Synchronized Cardioversion

  • Done with the LifePak (the "life pack"). It delivers a countershock on the R wave of the QRS. "Cardioversion" always means synchronized, even when the word is dropped.
  • You must have an R wave to cardiovert. A-fib and A-flutter have one, so these patients are cardioverted, not defibrillated.
  • Turn the sync switch ON (the sync button sits under the shock button). Unsynchronized, the shock fires whenever you push the button, and if it lands on the wrong part of the QRS it can trigger a life-threatening dysrhythmia.
  • Non-emergency: sedate first. Start low, usually 50 to 100 joules, and increase if necessary (the joules go up if the first try doesn't work).
  • Make sure all personnel are clear and no one is touching the bed.
  • If the patient becomes pulseless, the QRS is lost: turn the sync off and defibrillate.
  • Nursing care: patent airway (sedated), supplemental oxygen, vital signs (hemodynamic stability), level of consciousness, monitor for dysrhythmias, emotional support, document the results.

Otherwise a successful cardioversion restores a strong atrial kick that can throw the clot to the brain (stroke). An emergent cardioversion in a very unstable patient: likely a low molecular weight heparin or a heparin drip.

Long-Term Anticoagulation

Warfarin

  • If treatment doesn't work, long-term anticoagulation: A-fib for life means warfarin for life.
  • Disadvantage: the INR must be monitored regularly ("therapeutically thin, but not too thin").
  • Antidote: vitamin K, or FFP (not a nursing decision).

The newer anticoagulants

  • Some don't need routine lab testing (an advantage).
  • The instructor doesn't think they have an antidote (a disadvantage).
  • They cost more and are contraindicated with impaired renal function: check the creatinine before giving them.
  • Which one: the provider's decision.

Non-Drug Treatments: Catheter Ablation and the Maze Procedure

Drug therapy for dysrhythmias, while frequently effective, is limited by high failure rates and potential drug toxicity.

Catheter ablation

  • Invasive, in the cath lab. After electrophysiology studies and mapping locate the irritable focus, the provider destroys (ablates) it.

Maze procedure

  • A surgical procedure: intentional incisions in the atrium create a one-way path from the SA node to the AV node.

PSVT (Paroxysmal Supraventricular Tachycardia)

  • Supraventricular = the impulse starts above the ventricle (above the bifurcation of the bundle of His): the SA node or anywhere in the atria. A generic catch-all term.
  • A run of repeated premature beats that usually starts with a PAC.
  • Paroxysmal = abrupt onset and abrupt termination. Sustained SVT starts abruptly but doesn't stop on its own, so something must be done.
  • Causes: in a normal heart, overexertion, stress, stimulants (not terribly uncommon). More serious: digoxin toxicity and various heart diseases.
  • Presentation depends on how long it lasts and how fast the ventricular rate is: too fast means poor ventricular filling, lower stroke volume and cardiac output (the tachydysrhythmia key features).

Heart Failure: The Basics

Know the circulation well, or left- and right-sided failure get mixed up: right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonic valve, pulmonary artery, lungs (oxygenated), left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve, aorta, body. Right-sided failure backs blood up on the right side; left-sided failure backs it up on the left. The manifestations depend on where the backup happens.

CauseWhat It Means
PreloadThe blood sitting in the heart during diastole. More blood volume = more preload.
AfterloadThe resistance the left ventricle must overcome to get blood out. High blood pressure raises afterload and, untreated, causes heart failure.
Myocardial contractilityThe muscle itself can't contract, regardless of fluid volume (for example a myocardium damaged by an MI).
Heart rateToo fast or too slow for a long period of time.

Ejection fraction

  • Stroke volume divided by the end-diastolic volume (the blood in the left ventricle before ejection).
  • Normal 55 to 70%. 40% or lower: heart failure. Very, very low is incompatible with life: an LVAD or heart transplant candidate.

HFrEF vs HFpEF (you'll see these in clinical)

  • HFrEF (reduced EF): a defect in systolic function: the LV isn't contracting effectively.
  • HFpEF (preserved EF): a defect in diastolic function (filling): the muscle contracts normally but is stiff and doesn't relax; EF normal or near normal.

Left-Sided vs Right-Sided Heart Failure

NCLEX and HESI questions tease out the difference, but it's a closed circuit: left failure eventually causes right failure, and vice versa, so patients often have both.

  • Paroxysmal = abrupt onset without warning; nocturnal = night; dyspnea = short of breath.
  • Asleep, then suddenly wakes and has to sit up to catch their breath: a sign of advanced left-sided failure.

Cor pulmonale is different

  • Enlargement of the right heart from pulmonary hypertension (for example in COPD): a type of right heart failure.
  • Right-sided failure that results from left-sided failure is not cor pulmonale.

Chronic HF vs Acute Decompensated HF

Chronic heart failure

  • Typically diagnosed outpatient; periods of acutely or slowly worsening function.
  • Often the consequence of damage from other cardiac events (CAD, hypertension). Can be insidious ("I've been feeling fatigued").

Acute decompensated (ADHF)

  • Diagnosed inpatient; no time to compensate. Can be life-threatening and need urgent therapy.
  • They may have 4+ edema and lots of ascites, but what brings them in is that they can't breathe.

Managing Chronic Heart Failure

Nursing measures

  • Target the underlying cause (hypertension: antihypertensives).
  • Oxygen 2 to 6 L (home, temporary or PRN) for dyspnea and fatigue.
  • Energy conservation and minimizing oxygen demand, but not sedentary: maintain activity.
  • Daily weights: same time of day, same scale, about the same clothing. Weight is the most consistent measure of fluid volume status.
  • Low-sodium diet ("salt sucks"): less water retention.

Drug therapy

  • ACE inhibitors and ARBs.
  • Beta blockers (carvedilol is usually the one).
  • Diuretics to get the fluid off.
  • Nitrates (coronary vasodilators such as nitroglycerin).
  • Digoxin (a cardiac glycoside): second line because of dysrhythmia risk, but HESI and NCLEX love it.
  • Spironolactone: a potassium-sparing diuretic, used here for suppressing sodium and water retention to offload the left ventricle; linked to fewer hospitalizations and cardiac deaths. It retains potassium and can worsen renal failure (loop and thiazide diuretics are potassium-losing).

Digoxin

What it does

  • Negative chronotrope: slows the heart rate (chrono = time, rate).
  • Positive inotrope: increases contractility.
  • Together: enhances cardiac output.
  • Narrow therapeutic index. Level 0.5 to 2 (won't be asked).

Digitoxicity

  • Digitalis + hypokalemia = digitoxicity. Monitor potassium, especially on a potassium-losing diuretic.
  • Signs: serious dysrhythmias and bradycardias, halos around lights, nausea and vomiting.
  • Antidote: Digibind.
Before every digoxin dose: take an apical heart rate for a full minute and hold it if the rate is below 60 ("commit to memory"). Teach the patient to take and record their own pulse daily.

LVAD (left ventricular assist device; not tested)

  • A bridge to a heart transplant, or destination therapy (prolonging life with no transplant planned).
  • Continuous flow: BP can't be read by machine, so take it manually with a Doppler. No S1 and S2, just a humming sound.
  • Unresponsive? Don't start CPR until you're sure the pump is completely off. Lots of patient and family teaching.

Acute decompensated HF care

  • Treat the cause; close vital signs and urine output (poor kidney perfusion lowers it); continuous telemetry and pulse oximetry; ABGs.
  • High Fowler's with the feet horizontal or dangling: decreases venous return and preload, easing the heart's workload.
  • BiPAP, face mask or non-rebreather; daily weights.
  • Meds: diuretics, vasodilators, morphine (decreases preload and afterload), positive inotropes such as digoxin.
  • Very sick: ICU and hemodynamic monitoring (not covered in med surg).

Arteries vs Veins

Arteries

  • A high-pressure system carrying oxygenated blood away from the heart.
  • Where the peripheral pulses come from. A needle in an artery gushes; we only stick arteries for ABGs or arterial lines.

Veins

  • A low-pressure system carrying deoxygenated blood back to the heart (often uphill from the legs).
  • Veins have valves to help venous return. IVs go in veins.

Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)

Blockages in the arteries of the periphery: the same idea as coronary artery disease. Risk factors: atherosclerosis plus smoking, diabetes, a really bad diet, elevated CRP, uncontrolled hypertension, family history. Put three or four together and PAD is no surprise.

Intermittent claudication

  • The classic PAD symptom: legs hurt when I walk, pain stops when I stop.

  • The pain's location matches where the occlusion is. Severe PAD: rest pain, a constant ache in bed.

Diagnosis

  • Doppler ultrasound: degree of blood flow.
  • Ankle brachial index (ABI): a non-invasive screening tool, office or bedside. "Yes, you do need to know how to do it."

PAD Collaborative Care

Medical and nursing care

  • Risk factor modification for the modifiable ones.
  • Statins, antihypertensives, possibly antiplatelets.
  • Pletal: specific for intermittent claudication; inhibits platelet aggregation and increases vasodilation.
  • Walk to the point of pain, stop and rest, then resume: builds collateral circulation.
  • Meticulous foot care: inspect the feet regularly (a nail stepped on may go unnoticed).

Procedures

  • Catheter-based (cath lab, usually the femoral artery; an alternative to open surgery): angioplasty (balloon), stent, atherectomy (plaque removal), angioplasty plus cold therapy. Like PCI for the heart.
  • Peripheral arterial bypass (like a CABG), named for what's bypassed: a fem-pop (femoral popliteal) bypass with a synthetic graft.
  • Amputation: the last resort.
After a PAD procedure: a meticulous peripheral vascular assessment, trended. Notify the provider of a dramatic increase in pain, a loss of pulse, extreme pallor, or any change. Avoid the knee-flexed position (impedes arterial flow). Early ambulation; meticulous foot care.

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)

An aneurysm is a permanent outpouching of the vessel wall. The aorta is a likely spot because of the incredibly high pressure it withstands. AAAs are often in the descending aorta.

Manifestations and risk

  • Usually no symptoms; often found on a routine exam: a mass, a bruit heard with the bell over the abdomen (you should not hear a bruit), or back pain.
  • The concern is rupture: life-threatening in a high-pressure system.
  • Familial tendency: monitor early in life if a family member had one.

Management

  • Small (under 4 cm): watchful waiting: decrease risk factors, keep the BP down, monitor the size annually.
  • Larger: surgery, ideally elective, because after a rupture mortality is very high (usually no time to reach the OR).

Raynaud's Phenomenon

What it is

  • An autoimmune vasospastic disorder of the small cutaneous arteries, usually the fingers (sometimes the toes).
  • May or may not occur with other diseases (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis). A young woman's disease.
  • Color changes from vasospasm: white, blue, sometimes red.
  • Triggers: cold, emotional stress, tobacco, caffeine.
  • Lasts a minute to hours: cold and numb, then throbbing, aching, tingling as perfusion returns.

Nursing management

  • Layered clothing, gloves; avoid extreme temperatures.
  • Immerse the hands in warm water to ease the spasms.
  • Avoid the triggers.
  • Severe: a slow-release calcium channel blocker, the first-line therapy, for the vessel spasms, not to lower BP.

Part 13 — Week 3: Chronic Venous Insufficiency & Venous Thromboembolism

From Chronic Venous Insufficiency & Venous Thromboembolism. Built from the last Week 3 voiceover: the two venous disorders. Veins are a low-pressure system with valves whose job is to get blood back to the heart; when they can't, you get venous congestion, pooling and peripheral edema.

Chronic Venous Insufficiency (CVI)

What it looks like

  • Varicose veins (tortuous vessels): a venous finding.
  • Lipodermatosclerosis: thick, leathery skin of the lower leg with a characteristic brownish ("brawny") discoloration.
  • Pitting edema.
  • Eczema with itching.
  • Patients can have both arterial and venous disease, with a mixture of cues.

Venous ulcers

  • Typically above the medial malleolus; they look and behave differently from arterial ulcers.
  • Can be quite painful, worse when dependent (arterial ulcers often aren't, from lost nerve endings).
  • More common than arterial ulcers.
  • Untreated: deep, wide ulcers at increased risk for infection.

CVI Collaborative Care

Nursing measures

  • Avoid standing or sitting for long periods (impedes venous return).
  • Elevate the legs above the level of the heart: promotes venous return and decreases swelling.
  • A daily walking program (keeps both venous and arterial circulation moving).
  • Proper foot and leg care.
  • High-calorie, high-protein diet for skin healing.

Compression therapy

  • Two types, static and dynamic; all of them promote venous return by squeezing blood back to the heart. Static = graded compression from distal to proximal.
  • Prescription stockings may need a vascular specialist to fit them. A stocking donner helps put them on correctly.
  • White TED hose are seen less: put on wrong, they can impede blood flow.
  • SCDs (intermittent compression devices) are used for venous insufficiency, but are not recommended with an actual VTE (they can move the clot).
Elevate for veins, not for arteries. Raising the legs above the heart helps venous return, but in arterial disease it turns the legs pale.

Venous Thromboembolism (VTE)

What used to be called a DVT and a PE is now lumped together as a VTE. We can anticipate them, so the goal is prevention: target the modifiable risk factors.

  • Unilateral leg edema: one leg larger than the other is concerning. Bilateral edema is unlikely to be a clot (more likely venous insufficiency).
  • Can be painful or tender to palpation (not always).
  • Dilated superficial veins; a sense of fullness or heaviness in the thigh or calf.
  • Numbness; warm, reddened skin and a low-grade temp (inflammation).
  • Usually the lower extremity.

  • Early and aggressive mobilization (patients with clots used to be kept in bed; not necessarily anymore). If not, turn every 2 hours.

  • Anticoagulation is very important, but it puts them at risk for bleeding.

The Negative Feedback System

The endocrine system works on a negative feedback system that creates homeostasis, like the thermostat in your living room: set it at 72, the temperature drops below 72, the heater kicks on until it reaches 72, then shuts off. "An important concept to understand with this content": every hormone works this way.

ADH: The Regulator of Pure Water

  • ADH (antidiuretic hormone, also called vasopressin) comes from the posterior pituitary. It is "the regulator of pure water supply" ("write that down").
  • The loop: serum osmolality rises (concentrated blood), ADH is released, the kidney tubules reabsorb water, plasma volume rises and osmolality falls, and the normal osmolality then inhibits ADH.
ADH moves pure water, not potassium. Retaining water from ADH does not retain potassium; losing water from too little ADH (diabetes insipidus) does not make the patient hypokalemic. It's not like diuresing with Lasix. "That's going to trip you up if you don't stop and understand this."

  • Secretes T3 and T4: T4 is the most abundant; T3 is the most potent.

Parathyroids

  • A different gland, buried inside the thyroid; regulate calcium and phosphorus.

Adrenal Gland and Pancreas

  • Two glands in one: the cortex covers (outside) and the medulla is in the middle.
  • They do totally different things (see Adrenal Disorders).

Pancreas

  • The islets of Langerhans secrete insulin.
  • In diabetes mellitus, the origin of the problem is the pancreas.

Part 15 — Week 4: ADH Disorders: SIADH & Diabetes Insipidus

From ADH Disorders: SIADH & Diabetes Insipidus. Built from the Week 4 ADH disorders voiceover. Too much ADH is SIADH; not enough is diabetes insipidus (DI). "If you understand what ADH does, all you have to do is think about what happens when I have too much and what happens when I don't have enough." Don't confuse diabetes insipidus with diabetes mellitus: two totally different things.

Sodium, Osmolality and ADH

  • Sodium makes up most of the serum osmolality: hypernatremic = hyperosmolar; hyponatremic = hypo-osmolar. Cells shrink or swell, and a sodium that's far enough off means a confused patient.
  • High osmolality or decreased circulating volume triggers two things: thirst (you drink more) and ADH secretion (you hold on to water). Circulating volume rises, which turns off ADH and thirst.
  • ADH, along with the thirst mechanism, is the major regulator of the body's water. Potassium is not impacted by ADH.

  • Restrict fluids (re-concentrates the serum); daily weights; I&O.

Neurogenic (central) DI

  • The pituitary is damaged, for example by head trauma (a closed head injury from a car accident).
  • Abrupt onset; usually self-limiting (resolves on its own), but the symptoms must be treated while it lasts.

  • The pituitary is OK, but the kidneys don't respond to ADH.
  • Most common cause: lithium.
  • Less abrupt onset and less severe symptoms. (A third type, psychogenic, isn't covered.)

  • Desmopressin (DDAVP): an exogenous, synthetic form of vasopressin (ADH), in very small doses: nasal spray, PO, IV or subQ (usually IV in the hospital).
  • Hormone therapy and fluid restriction are the cornerstones of treatment for neurogenic DI.

Cushing's Syndrome

  • Cushing's disease (usually a tumor) is very rare. Most of the time it's Cushing's syndrome, a collection of signs and symptoms caused by long-term oral systemic steroids.
  • Steroids are wonderful anti-inflammatories and immunosuppressants, and sometimes nothing else works, but these are the side effects. (Why NSAIDs are loved: they don't cause Cushing's.)
  • Why each sign happens: excess glucose availability (hyperglycemia); fragile capillaries (bruising); protein breakdown and fat redistribution (truncal obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, skinny extremities, osteoporosis); suppressed immune and inflammatory response (poor healing, infection); CNS excitability (moods, insomnia); high aldosterone (sodium and fluid retention, hypertension, hypokalemia).

Priority problems

  • Risk for infection (immunosuppression).
  • Fluid balance; skin integrity (thin skin; any break is an infection risk).
  • Risk for injury (thin bones).
  • Ineffective coping (moods); body image.
  • Unstable blood glucose; potential hypokalemia.

Nursing and collaborative care

  • Rebalance the hormones. Disease (a tumor): adrenalectomy, or removing a tumor secreting ectopic ACTH (another paraneoplastic disorder). Drugs for the disease: not tested.
  • Syndrome (steroids for RA or COPD, for example): gradually decrease the drug, reduce the dose, or try every-other-day dosing, all provider supervised.
  • Monitor fluid balance, glucose, BP, infection risk and mood.
  • Diet: increase protein and potassium; decrease calories and sodium.

Addison's Disease

  • An insufficiency of the glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids (cortisol and aldosterone, plus androgens). The famous example: John F. Kennedy (tan, bronze appearance; thin frame).
  • Usually a slow degenerative destruction: subtle and insidious. Occasionally rapid onset: severe, abrupt, even life-threatening.
  • Low aldosterone: low sodium, low blood volume, low BP, salt cravings, dehydration, high potassium. Low cortisol: no energy, hypoglycemia, weakness, fatigue. Unsuppressed ACTH from the anterior pituitary causes the hyperpigmentation.

Treatment and Teaching

Hormone replacement (the mainstay)

  • Hydrocortisone (cortisol): two-thirds in the morning, one-third in the late afternoon, matching the diurnal rhythm (more cortisol in the morning).
  • Fludrocortisone (Florinef) for missing aldosterone, taken in the morning.
  • Possibly salt additives for heat and humidity.

Teaching is critical

  • Follow the dosing schedule closely; never stop abruptly. Replacement is forever.
  • The 3 x 3 rule: when stressed (sick, a big exam, a marathon), take 3 times the dose for 3 days, depending on the severity of the stress.
  • Keep an emergency supply (an emergency kit with IM hydrocortisone) and wear a medic alert bracelet.
Addisonian crisis: a major, abrupt adrenal insufficiency (for example a big stressor without the cortisol supplement) that can be fatal untreated. Treatment: IV cortisol replacement, saline and dextrose; oral hydrocortisone once they can take it, until the maintenance dose is reached, plus fludrocortisone if aldosterone is low. Nursing: these patients can be extremely hemodynamically unstable, so trend the vital signs closely, and keep the environment calm and stress-free.

Pheochromocytoma

What it is

  • A tumor of the adrenal medulla that secretes too much epinephrine and norepinephrine.
  • Often missed at first: only 0.1% of high blood pressures are caused by one.
  • Diagnosis is quite easy once suspected: serum catecholamines, possibly a 24-hour urine.

Cues and treatment

  • Intractable hypertension (can't be controlled).
  • The triad: palpitations, headache, sweating (diaphoresis).
  • Treatment: remove the tumor.

Symptoms

  • The three P's: polydipsia (excessive thirst), polyphagia (excessive hunger), polyuria (excessive urination).
  • Fatigue; recurrent infections (lower immune function); slow wound healing.

Type 2 risk factors

  • Non-modifiable: family history; over 45; African American, Hispanic, American Indian and Pacific Islander (higher risk than Caucasians); a history of gestational diabetes.
  • Modifiable (the target): physical inactivity, overweight or high body fat, high blood pressure, high cholesterol.

Prediabetes

  • Impaired glucose tolerance, impaired fasting glucose, or both. High risk for type 2; usually no symptoms, but damage from insulin resistance may already be happening.
  • Nursing is mostly teaching: lifestyle modification, monitor glucose and A1C, follow up with the primary care provider, avoid sugary foods, count and monitor carbohydrates. Insulin resistance can be reversed.

Medications

Oral medications

  • Used more in type 2; started low and increased gradually based on the A1C and fasting (morning) glucose.
  • In the hospital, oral diabetes meds are often stopped and replaced with insulin while acutely ill (sugar is hard to control). Explain this, and that they go back to oral meds at home.
  • Bring forward pathopharm concepts, such as holding metformin before specific procedures and the drug classes (for example, know that an SGLT-2 inhibitor treats diabetes). No specific side-effect questions.

Insulin: mimic the body

  • The body spikes insulin with each meal. A basal-bolus regimen copies this: a basal (long-acting) insulin once a day, usually at bedtime (glargine), plus rapid or short-acting boluses before each meal.
  • Inhaled insulin: be aware of it; not tested.

Insulin is a high-alert medication. Never give insulin without knowing the current blood glucose ("always, always, always"), and know the normal range. Monitoring and Sick Days

Self-monitoring blood glucose

  • A crucial part of management; frequency depends on the glycemic goal, the type, the regimen, supplies and willingness. A new type 2 on metformin: maybe morning and bedtime. On insulin: multiple times a day.
  • CGM (continuous glucose monitor): reads every 1 to 5 minutes to a phone or watch; more common in type 1; better control but expensive.
  • Insulin pumps: a continuous subQ infusion of rapid or regular insulin (a basal rate, plus self-given boluses). Still check fingersticks at least 4 times a day (before meals and bedtime), unless using a CGM.

Insulin pumps in the hospital

  • Acute care: deactivate the pump and switch to a sliding scale for tighter control.
  • Check the sites for infection (high glucose raises infection risk: "bacteria love to eat sugar").
  • Risk of DKA: a malfunctioning pump may go unnoticed while sugars climb.

Sick-Day Management

Illness is a stress that releases hormones that raise blood sugar, and steroids (oral or IV, common when acutely ill) can raise it dramatically, so home insulin may need adjusting. Sick patients are more prone to DKA (or HHS, the type 2 equivalent).

Teach the patient to

  • Let the provider know they're sick.
  • Check blood sugar more often, even every 2 to 4 hours.
  • Keep taking their medications, even insulin as long as the blood sugar tolerates it (oral meds work long-term, so take them even with a stomach virus).
  • Prevent dehydration; meet carbohydrate needs with food or liquids; rest.

Call the provider if

  • Urine is positive for ketones.
  • Blood sugar is greater than 250.
  • A fever not getting better with Tylenol.
  • Confused, disoriented, or rapid breathing.
  • Ongoing nausea, vomiting and diarrhea; can't tolerate liquids; illness lasting longer than 2 days.

This list is patient teaching for home. A hospitalized patient with a blood sugar over 250 on a test question doesn't automatically mean "call the provider."

Hypoglycemia and Hyperglycemia

Hypoglycemia (below 70)

  • Patients can have symptoms above 70 too, especially if they usually run high.
  • Sweating, blurry vision, irritability, shakiness; they can feel like they're going to die. Untreated: seizures and death.
  • Common after rapid-acting insulin when they then don't eat or go for a procedure.
  • An unresponsive patient: after the airway, check the blood sugar.

Hyperglycemia

  • Causes: illness, infection, self-management problems (running out of insulin, which is expensive), extreme stress, corticosteroids.
  • Weak, very tired, blurry vision, headache, upset stomach. Usually gradual and vague, so patients often don't notice until a crisis.
  • Usually means over 250, even 300; check urine ketones. Treat with insulin, fluids to prevent dehydration, and teaching (you may need more insulin when sick).
  • DKA and HHS (sugars in the 600s and 700s) are emergencies covered later: the electrolyte abnormalities can be fatal.
The rule of 15: confirm with a fingerstick (below 70). If awake and able to swallow, give 15 grams of simple carbohydrate: 4 to 6 oz of orange juice or regular soda, 3 glucose tabs, 1 tablespoon of honey, or 5 to 8 Lifesavers (no candy bars: fat delays absorption). This should raise the sugar about 50 in 15 minutes (54 to about 104). Recheck in 15 minutes; still below 70, repeat; once stable, eat a regular meal. Unresponsive or nearly passed out: IM glucagon or IV dextrose (25 to 50 mL); at home, a glucose gel under the tongue.

Chronic Complications

Caused by long-term hyperglycemia damaging the blood vessels (angiopathy), the usual cause of death in diabetes: about 68% cardiovascular and about 16% stroke.

Macrovascular (large vessels)

  • Coronary, cerebral and peripheral vessels. A much higher cardiovascular risk: women 4 to 6 times, men 2 to 3 times.
  • Prevent: stop smoking, control BP, modify high-fat diets, keep the A1C as low as possible, treat all the conditions (together they make metabolic syndrome, which raises the risk of death).

Microvascular (small vessels)

  • Eyes: frequent eye exams.
  • Kidneys: diabetes is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease in the US; treat hypertension too.
  • Nerves: neuropathy in about 60 to 70%, the major contributor to amputation. Only real treatment: early screening and lower blood sugars.

Neuropathy and Foot Care

  • Loss of protective sensation: a cut or a too-tight shoe goes unnoticed, especially on the bottoms of the feet. Ulcers can become neuro-ischemic (lost blood flow), won't heal without nutrients and white cells, and can lead to losing toes. Smoking and PAD compound the risk.
  • Know diabetic foot care (expect a select-all question): for example no commercial remedies for calluses; a mild foot powder or lanolin is OK; well-fitting shoes.
  • Monofilament test (a bit outdated): measures how much sensation is lost on the bottom of the feet.

Nutrition, Exercise and the Hospitalized Patient

Nutrition

  • A balanced, high-fiber, low-fat, low-cholesterol diet (especially type 2).
  • Don't avoid carbs: about 45 to 65% of daily calories, from whole grains, fruits, legumes and milk; limit simple carbs like pasta and bread (never "never eat pasta").
  • Low saturated and trans fat; polyunsaturated fats (fish, nuts) are best.
  • Fiber (beans, vegetables, oats, whole grains) improves carbohydrate metabolism and lowers cholesterol.
  • Protein about 15 to 20%, from lean meats like chicken and fish (red meat has a higher fat content).
  • Alcohol: limit to 1 a day for women, 2 for men; it can raise sugar and then cause a rebound drop.
  • Alternative sweeteners don't raise blood sugar and are acceptable.
  • UK uses consistent carbohydrate diets; type 1 patients often carb count (dosing insulin per 15 g of carbs).

Exercise and the hospital

  • Exercise is incredibly important, but it can lower blood sugar. Don't exercise below 80 or above 250. Best after a meal; a carbohydrate snack first if it's been over an hour since eating or before high-intensity exercise.
  • Medical alert bracelet; well-fitting shoes.
  • Hospitalized: stress and surgery push sugars much higher than at home (reassure them it's normal). Wound healing is impaired and infection risk is higher: watch the WBC count, fever and abnormal exudate.
  • Skin changes from uncontrolled hyperglycemia (reddish-brown spots on the legs, brown-black thickening in skin folds, red patches around blood vessels): not life-threatening, but preventable, and in someone undiagnosed, a reason to check the A1C.

Emergency Care and the Three Goals

Emergency care

  • Airway, breathing, circulation; assess for a head injury.
  • Splint the fracture as quickly as possible, in normal body alignment, and immobilize it.
  • Elevate the body part if possible (venous return, less swelling).
  • Cold pack: vasoconstriction decreases the (often severe) swelling.

Reduce, immobilize, restore function

  • Reduce: put the bone fragments back in their anatomic position.
  • Closed reduction (preferred): non-surgical, manual realignment without an incision, often in the ED, possibly with local anesthesia.
  • Open reduction: through an incision, often coupled with internal fixation (nails, screws, plates): ORIF.
  • Immobilize afterward so the approximated bones can heal (not a quick process), with external or internal devices.

Peripheral Neurovascular Assessment

For anyone with a bone break (casted or not) and anyone with an internal device, assess distal to the break. "Very vigilant."

The 5 P's: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis. Pain is the first sign: increasing pain despite pain medication (anything trending up is significant). By the time there's no pulse, pallor or paralysis, "you've missed the boat."

External Devices

Casts

What they are

  • Circumferential (all the way around the limb), temporary immobilization; common after a closed reduction. Arm, leg, body (body spica) casts.
  • Fiberglass today, but standardized exams still ask about plaster of Paris: it's heated, handled with wet palms (no finger imprints; it takes a while to set), and the edges are petaled (turned outward so they don't dig into the skin).
  • Until set: don't cover it (ventilation), and reposition every 1 to 2 hours.

After it goes on

  • Neurovascular checks distal to the cast every hour for 24 hours: swelling can increase and the hard cast won't expand.
  • Rule of thumb: a finger or two should fit inside.
  • Ice for the first 24 to 36 hours (vasoconstriction, less swelling).
  • Complications: infection, circulation problems, peripheral nerve damage, complications of immobility, and pressure areas over bony prominences (medial malleolus, olecranon process, tibial tuberosity): listen to complaints of pain and discomfort.

Splints, Immobilizers and Traction

Splints and immobilizers (a Thomas leg splint, braces; some can dial in the allowed flexion) are removable, unlike a cast. Traction applies a pulling force on the fractured extremity, and it is continuous unless the provider orders otherwise.

Buck's traction

  • The simplest form: a straight pull on the affected extremity: running traction (one direction), so the patient constantly slides down to the foot of the bed.
  • Temporary: for muscle spasms, or to immobilize before surgery (buys time for the swelling to go down before an ORIF the next day).

Russell's traction

  • Still skin traction, but it pulls both ways: counter traction.
  • Lets the patient move a little in bed and permits knee flexion; helps relieve muscle spasms and back pain. Also temporary.

Balanced suspension traction (Russell's or skeletal) is preferred: counter-traction pulls in opposite directions, so the patient doesn't slide down; moving the bed or patient doesn't change the pull; more mobility and autonomy. A trapeze bar lets patients lift themselves and pull up in bed.

Caring for a patient in traction: maintain constant traction with no interruption in the weights; the weights always hang free, off the floor; and never let anything interrupt the line of pull.

External Fixators and Pin Care

  • An external fixator ("ex-fix") is used with an open reduction but without suspended skeletal traction. It looks horrific, but the patient is not confined to bed (can get in a wheelchair, though not necessarily bearing weight). Examples: arm, jaw, halo, hand, leg.
  • Ex-fix and skeletal traction both break the skin, so infection is the concern; infection in the bone is a real problem.
  • Assess pin sites: the pin shouldn't wiggle; clear serous drainage is OK; purulent drainage likely means infection.
  • Pin care: typically half-strength peroxide with a sterile swab. No ointments or anything gooey (attracts bacteria). Teach the patient not to touch it.

Internal Devices and Nursing Care After a Fracture

Pins, screws and plates mean the patient had surgery: an open reduction with internal fixation (ORIF). The neurovascular assessment applies again.

Neurovascular dysfunction and pain

  • Elevate the extremity above the heart for the first day or two if not contraindicated; ice compresses.
  • Notify the provider immediately if pain increases and is unrelieved by medication; teach the signs of neurovascular dysfunction.
  • Fracture pain is ongoing and predictable, so give around-the-clock dosing: be proactive, talk about the pain regimen at the start of the shift (many patients don't ask). Non-drug measures supplement, not replace, medication for severe pain.

Infection, skin and mobility

  • Watch pin sites; strict aseptic technique; culture anything suspicious; antibiotics per order; watch temperature and WBC.
  • Petal plaster edges. Nothing inside the cast (no coat hangers to scratch, even when healing itches). Report warmth, worsening pain or odor; keep ventilation; turn if allowed (friction, shearing).
  • Pain control before range of motion; active ROM preferred, passive if they can't, or passive-assistive; PT.
  • Know whether the patient is weight bearing or non-weight bearing (check, double check, triple check the mobility orders); teach assistive devices.

Compartment Syndrome and Fasciotomy

There are about 26 compartments in a leg. Swelling inside a compartment has nowhere to go because the surrounding fascia (the body's internal "cling wrap") won't budge, and it can cause vascular compromise very quickly: miss it on the morning shift and the patient could lose the limb by night.

Compartment syndrome is an emergency. The classic cue is severe pain not fixed with opioids. The procedure that relieves it is a fasciotomy (opening the fascia); once the swelling goes down, it is closed.

Fat Embolism Syndrome

  • Long bone fractures (the femur, the humerus) put patients at risk for clots, including a fat embolism: a fat globule released from the fracture.
  • Prompt surgical stabilization of the fracture reduces its occurrence.
  • Red flags: rising respiratory rate, cyanosis, anxiety, tachycardia, falling O2 sat, chest pain.
  • Neurologic features often precede the pulmonary ones by 6 to 12 hours: LOC changes, confusion, apprehension.
  • A petechial rash (chest, eyes); fluffy shadows on the chest x-ray.
  • Treatment is mainly supportive: oxygen, fluids.

Amputations

Terms and candidates

  • Most often the lower extremity: BKA (below the knee), AKA (above the knee), disarticulation (right at the joint).
  • A residual limb matters for a prosthetic: a BKA keeps the knee joint, a big plus over an AKA.
  • Candidates: arterial insufficiency / peripheral vascular disease (often years of uncontrolled diabetes, older patients); peripheral neuropathy (an unfelt foot sore becomes infected); trauma; thermal injuries; osteomyelitis; tumors; congenital limb disorders.

Types

  • Open (guillotine): the wound bed is left open, for an infected area that must become aseptic before the edges are approximated. Closed is the alternative.
  • Elective (planned, time for anticipatory grieving) vs traumatic (life-saving; the patient may wake up without a limb). Both are very difficult to process.

Joint Surgeries (Arthroplasty)

Why and who

  • An arthroplasty is a reconstruction or replacement of a joint. The hip and knee are the most common.
  • It often comes down to the patient being ready because of pain: the x-ray may not look bad but the patient has had it, or the reverse (bone on bone, doing fine, so they hold off).
  • Goals: relieve pain, improve joint motion, correct deformity or malalignment, remove intra-articular causes of erosion.
  • Candidates: osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, congenital deformities.

Nursing problems

  • Ongoing, predictable pain; impaired physical mobility; risk for injury; impaired skin integrity.
  • Risk for peripheral neurovascular dysfunction: always check perfusion distal to the joint.
  • The number one post-op complication of a joint replacement is venous thromboembolism (DVT/PE), from manipulation of the bone.
  • The original surgical dressing is removed only by the surgeon.

Knee Replacement

  • Priority post-procedure: restoring strength, muscle tone and range of motion.
  • A patient who can't do (or comply with) their own ROM may go on a continuous passive motion (CPM) machine: range and speed are dialed in and it moves the leg continuously (passive ROM).
  • PT exercises to build up the quads in the hospital, then regularly at home.

Osteomyelitis

A severe infection of the bone, bone marrow and surrounding soft tissue. Circulating blood doesn't reach bone well, so it is hard to treat. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common organism. Delayed identification can lead to chronic pain, infection and drainage, loss of limb function, amputation, or death from sepsis.

Risk factors

  • History of drug use, diabetes, immunosuppression, blood infections.
  • Pressure ulcers or chronic wounds.
  • Malnutrition, alcoholism, liver failure.
  • Entry: indirect (through the bloodstream) or direct (into the wound).

Acute vs chronic

  • Acute (less than a month): local constant bone pain that worsens with activity and isn't relieved by rest, swelling, tenderness, warmth, restricted movement; systemic fever, chills, night sweats, nausea, malaise, restlessness.
  • Chronic: has been there a long time or failed initial antibiotics; continuous, or remissions and flares. Fewer systemic signs, but the local signs remain.

Diagnosis

  • Bone and tissue biopsy to find the causative organism; wound and blood cultures.
  • WBC and ESR elevated.
  • X-rays don't change until about 10 days after the infection: less than a week in, the x-ray may not show it.
  • Bone scan: increased uptake. MRI.

Treatment

  • Vigorous IV antibiotics, likely through a central line (often going home with it).
  • Wound debridement; antibiotic beads implanted near the bone.
  • Mobilization: maybe bed rest at first, then up if it's long term (provider preference).
  • Pain control.

Herniated Disc

The disc is a spongy cushion between the vertebrae that prevents bone on bone, allows movement, and lets the nerves exit the vertebral column. Degenerative, bulging, herniated and thinning discs, plus osteophytes, create bone on bone. The consequence is pressure on the nerve root and radiating neuropathies (lumbar disease pressing on the sciatic nerve: pain down the back of the buttock and leg). The bump at the base of the neck is the spinous process of C7.

Diagnosis: MRI, CT scan, myelogram (shows the stenosis).

Conservative measures (try first)

  • Brace or corset.
  • Local heat and ice, massage, possibly traction.
  • A TENS unit (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulator): a non-drug intervention placed where it hurts, intensity dialed up or down.
  • NSAIDs, short-term narcotics, muscle relaxants.
  • Back and core strengthening exercises.

Surgery if conservative care fails

  • Laminectomy: removes the lamina (posterior arch) to free up space for the nerves and reach the protruding disc.
  • Microsurgical discectomy (MSD): removes part of the disc to decompress the nerve root.
  • Spinal fusion with a bone graft (synthetic or tissue) to keep bone off bone and stabilize; recovery takes longer.

After Back Surgery

  • The patient initially lies flat (sitting up puts pressure on the spinal column).
  • Log roll to keep body alignment (no twisting): three people; the person at the head holds the head stable and calls the count, and the two helpers move at exactly the same time.
  • Pain management.
  • CSF leak: the patient complains of a headache, and drainage is clear, light yellow.
  • Peripheral neurovascular checks; monitor a donor site such as the iliac crest.
  • No sitting or standing for long periods until healed; avoid twisting (teach how to get in and out of the car).

Osteoporosis

Bone is dynamic tissue that is constantly remodeling. Osteoporosis is loss of bone density ("porous" bone), which breaks more easily (pathological fractures). It is a silent disease (you can't feel it), and it hits the wrist, hip and vertebral column: the same three places a DEXA scans.

Who is at risk

  • Women are 8 times more likely than men (lower calcium content and bone mass, smaller frames, menopause, longer life), but men get it too.
  • Family history; low body weight; steroids for 3 months or more; advancing age.
  • Diet low in calcium; lack of load-bearing exercise.
  • Cigarettes and excessive alcohol.

Clinical manifestations

  • Compression fractures: porous vertebrae collapse and crumble on one another.
  • Progressive vertebral deformities: kyphosis and getting shorter with age.
  • Vertebrae review: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic (don't move much; rib attachments), then lumbar, sacrum, coccyx. The lumbar and cervical are the most movable, so they have the most problems.

Prevention and Management

  • The disease is preventable, and prevention is easier than treatment.
  • Calcium supplements: carbonate or citrate; chewable is recommended, taken with food (mega doses are hard to absorb). Vitamin D is always recommended with calcium.
  • Load-bearing exercise (walking, running, weight lifting), stated in the voiceover as 30 minutes three times a day.
  • Avoid tobacco and excessive alcohol; a corset helps prevent vertebral collapse.
Alendronate: take with water on an empty stomach and stay upright for 30 minutes afterward, or it causes esophagitis. Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) are also used.

Rheumatic Disorders: The Big Split

Osteoarthritis is a local joint disorder with only minimal local inflammation. Rheumatoid arthritis and lupus are systemic, with a lot of inflammation. Gout (gouty arthritis) is the fourth.

Osteoarthritis (OA)

Progressive (more symptomatic over time), with a low level of destructive inflammation at the joint, but no systemic manifestations: it's all local. Not a normal part of aging: there are things you can do to minimize and prevent it.

Cues

  • Joint pain (why they seek care) that worsens with use and is initially relieved by rest.
  • Temperature and barometric pressure changes trigger pain ("the rain is coming").
  • Stiffness worsens with inactivity; morning stiffness resolves quickly once they move (unlike RA).
  • Crepitus (snap, crackle, pop); osteophytes; a bowlegged appearance if bad enough; typically asymmetrical (unlike RA).
  • Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes (not inflamed, not tender); the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint; squaring off of the thumb joint.
  • Body parts: the neck and lower back (cervical and lumbar spine are the most mobile) and other joints.

Diagnosis

  • Bone scans, CT, MRI can show early changes.
  • X-rays stage the progression (bone on bone, osteophytes) but don't correlate with the degree of pain.
  • No biomarkers for OA.
  • ESR is normal unless there's synovial inflammation.
  • Synovial fluid is clear yellow with no signs of inflammation (unlike RA's synovitis).

  • A thorough multidimensional baseline pain assessment.

  • Nursing problems: acute and chronic pain, impaired mobility, self-care deficit, overweight, depression.
  • Team: rheumatologists, PT, OT, nutritionists; arthritis.org for resources.

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)

Autoimmune, symmetrical, progressive and insidious: a highly inflammatory connective tissue disorder. Early symptoms are systemic: nonspecific fatigue, not wanting to eat, weight loss, generalized stiffness, then pain, worsening stiffness, limited motion, deformities and possibly disability. Morning stiffness is prolonged (OA improves in less than 30 minutes). RA has remissions and exacerbations. Often young patients, which can limit their ADLs and roles.

Extra-articular changes

  • Sjögren's syndrome (about 1 in 10 with RA): diminished lacrimal and salivary secretions: dry, gritty eyes, decreased tearing, dry mouth; very photosensitive. Also seen with other rheumatic disorders (lupus).
  • Rheumatoid nodules (2 or 3 in 10): benign, but a nuisance and a body image issue; on the elbow they risk impaired skin integrity.

Hand deformities

  • Ulnar deviation, knuckle and wrist subluxation (partial dislocation).
  • Swan neck fingers, boutonnière deformity, Z-shaped thumbs.
  • Joints: the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) and metacarpophalangeal (MCP, the knuckles), plus the wrists and feet (never mentioned with OA).

Diagnosing RA

  • Inflammatory arthritis in 3 or more joints; symptoms lasting more than 6 weeks.
  • RF positive in 80% (poorly specific, but providers order it).
  • ACPA (anti-CCP): more specific; may be positive very early.
  • ESR and CRP elevated.

RA Collaborative Care

Non-drug care

  • Rest inflamed joints, but maintain physical fitness: a happy medium.
  • 8 to 10 hours of sleep plus a nap.
  • Regular exercise (pain makes them not want to move, but mobility gets worse): ROM, isometric, isotonic and isokinetic exercise; swimming, cycling, walking for endurance.
  • Hand and finger splints (maintain alignment; may be worn while sleeping); PT and OT.
  • Heat and cold, most effective alternating, limited to 20 minutes or less.
  • Good dietary habits; biofeedback and cognitive behavioral therapy (education reduces depression and disability).

Drugs: time is of the essence

  • Drugs are the cornerstone of active RA treatment: goal is remission and preventing further damage. The earlier, the better.
  • DMARDs (disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs) reduce inflammation, reduce and prevent joint damage, preserve structure.
  • Methotrexate: improves symptoms in 4 to 6 weeks; start as soon as possible (much less effective if you wait).
  • Hydroxychloroquine: fullest benefit takes longer, about 2 to 3 months.
  • NSAIDs: more immediate pain relief but don't reduce long-term damage; must be taken continuously, around the clock, for several weeks for the anti-inflammatory effect; discontinued once the DMARDs are working.
  • Steroids: wonderful anti-inflammatories, but short term, early, the least possible dose for the shortest time (PO, IV or into the joint).

Gout

Terms

  • Gout: the metabolic problem, a disruption in uric acid metabolism, with urate salt deposits in tissues in and around joints.
  • Gouty arthritis: how gout affects the joint: acute, painful inflammation; recurring; often the great toe (not always).
  • Chronic tophaceous gout (less common, advanced): about 10 years after onset; tophi are small white nodules of urate crystals that are painless (body image, loss of hand use).
  • Cause: too much uric acid production or not enough excretion (kidneys), or lots of purine foods (purine is metabolized to uric acid). Primary (most) or secondary (some drugs; don't need to know which).

A flare

  • The joint looks "angry": very intense pain; even touching the bedsheets or wearing socks hurts.
  • Usually the great toe, usually in the early morning.
  • Edema, swelling, tenderness, erythema; possible fever and malaise.
  • Peaks in 1 to 2 days; the flare takes 5 to 10 days to dissipate.

Modifiable Risk Factors and Diet

  • Modifiable: hyperuricemia, obesity, high blood pressure, hyperlipidemia, cardiac disease, diabetes, CKD, dietary factors, alcohol: all teaching opportunities.
  • Weight loss decreases gout (proven better outcomes): a calorie-restricted diet, more protein, complex carbs in place of refined, less saturated fat.
  • Protein from low-fat dairy and plant sources, not red meat and fish.
  • Avoid frequent sugar-sweetened beverages, fatty meals, organ-rich foods, beer and distilled spirits, and anything that causes a flare.
  • A strict low-purine diet only lowers urate 15 to 20%, so the trend is weight loss, medication and the diet above: most gout patients will be on medication anyway.

Diagnosis, Care and Drugs

Diagnosis and nursing care

  • Serum uric acid elevated; a 24-hour urine for uric acid shows whether it's decreased renal excretion or overproduction.
  • Synovial fluid aspiration (needle-like crystals) is the gold standard, but usually not done: 80% are diagnosed without it.
  • Address modifiable risks; pain control; joint immobilization during a flare.
  • Keep the sheets off the toes with a little tent; no socks.
  • Encourage fluids to increase urine volume and prevent uric acid precipitation in the renal tubules (dehydration on a hot day triggered a flare).

Drugs (primary therapy)

  • Colchicine for an acute attack: usually dramatic relief within a day.
  • NSAIDs (ibuprofen) also for acute gout.
  • Allopurinol: prophylactic, for recurrent flares; takes 2 to 6 weeks to decrease flares.

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

The "look good, feel bad" disease. Autoimmune, multisystem and inflammatory; far more in women aged 20 to 40 (a productive phase of life); genetic, hormonal, environmental and immunologic components. Chronic and unpredictable, with remissions and flares. Affects the skin, joints, serous membranes (pleural and pericardial effusions), kidneys, blood and nervous system. African Americans have an increased risk; 50% develop damage in one or more organs. Patients are usually hospitalized for the complications, not the lupus itself.

  • A steroid-sparing immunosuppressant: methotrexate.
  • Severe, life-threatening disease: intensive immunosuppressants and the highest-dose steroids to halt tissue injury.

Collaborative care

  • Build rapport with the patient and family; be sympathetic to the challenges of a chronic, unpredictable disease.
  • During exacerbations: fever, joint inflammation, limited movement, discomfort, fatigue, anxiety, and steroid adverse effects.
  • Space out activities; extra rest and naps (10+ hours at night plus a nap or two).
  • Monitor neuro status (lupus cerebritis; steroids can also cause mental changes).

Parkinson's Disease (PD)

Chronic, progressive, insidious (it happens over time without being noticed) and neurodegenerative. The pathology: not enough dopamine, so dopamine and acetylcholine are out of balance.

The Four Cardinal Features

FeatureKey Points
1. TremorA pill-rolling rest tremor: most notable when not doing purposeful activity, and it gets better with purposeful activity (unlike the action tremors of essential tremor or MS). The least debilitating cardinal feature. Present in about half; an internal tremulousness others can't see may come first. Starts on one side and spreads to the other over several years.
2. Muscle rigidityIncreased resistance to passive range of motion: "cogwheel," jerky or stuck when the elbow is flexed and extended. Also starts on one side and spreads.
3. BradykinesiaGeneralized slowness of movement, including the flat affect. Arguably the most major cause of disability, and the hardest for patients to describe ("I feel weak and uncoordinated," "super tired").
4. Postural instabilityComes later (the first three happen early). They lose their balance easily: high fall risk.

Non-motor cues (97% have them; average of 8 each)

  • Cognitive dysfunction and dementia; psychosis and hallucinations; mood disorders.
  • Olfactory dysfunction may come before the motor symptoms.
  • Sleep disturbances; insomnia is one of the most disturbing complaints.
  • Autonomic dysfunction (GI tract, kidneys); sexual dysfunction; pain (face, abdomen, genitals, joints) and sensory disorders.

What a PD patient looks like

  • Flat affect, blank facial expression; tilting forward.
  • Soft, slow, monotonous speech; dysarthria (slurred speech); hypophonia (soft voice).
  • Tremors and a shuffling gait; freezing while walking.
  • Micrographia: progressively shrinking handwriting (smaller when "off," bigger with effective medication).

Diagnosis and Drug Therapy

  • No specific diagnostic test: based on history and clinical features; at least two or three signs of the classic triad.
  • The ultimate confirmation is a trial of anti-Parkinson's drugs: do they respond?
  • No cure: treatment relieves symptoms by correcting the neurotransmitter imbalance (enhance dopamine release or antagonize the cholinergic neurons).
  • Levodopa is the drug of choice and the most effective for symptomatic PD (especially bradykinesia), with many side effects. It only works for a certain time (typically wearing off within about 5 years), so the provider is very particular about when to start it.
  • Also dopamine agonists as monotherapy and/or anticholinergics.

The on-off phenomenon

  • The invariable consequence of long-term levodopa: "on" = mobile, "off" = immobile.
  • At first, wearing off happens at the end of dosing intervals; eventually it becomes unpredictable.
  • Managed by shortening the interdose interval, controlled-release medications, and avoiding high-protein meals.

Deep brain stimulation

  • The most common surgical treatment for advanced PD.
  • Looks like a pacemaker, with leads into the brain; adjustable to control symptoms, and reversible.

Nursing Care for PD

Care and the team

  • Extra time to respond to questions and for ADLs (OT); a listening ear.
  • Give medications promptly and watch for side effects.
  • Prevent complications of immobility: constipation, contractures, skin breakdown (PT).
  • Speech therapy for dysphagia and hypophonia; prevent aspiration.
  • Dietitian: calorie-dense, nutrient-rich foods, more fluids, bite-sized pieces, small frequent meals.
  • Assess for depression and anxiety; psychosocial support; caregiver burden; support groups.

Teaching for freezing while walking

  • Consciously think about stepping over an imaginary line (or a small object, like a rice kernel).
  • Rock side to side to get momentum going.
  • Lift the toes when stepping.
  • Take one step backward, then two forward.
  • Remove rugs and other tripping hazards.

Myasthenia Gravis (MG)

"Grave muscle weakness." Acquired, autoimmune, progressive, characterized by muscle weakness from a reduction of acetylcholine receptors. It has remissions and exacerbations (flares), triggered by anything that stresses the person: pregnancy, illness, heat. Typically peaks within 2 years of onset. Severity ranges from mild to respiratory failure from muscle weakness. Takeaway: grave muscle weakness in the head area.

Presenting symptoms

  • Ocular MG (eyes and face) is the majority; the other type is generalized muscle weakness.
  • 50% present with ocular symptoms: ptosis (a droopy eyelid from muscle weakness, not sleepiness; one or both sides, can switch eyes, fluctuates through the day) and diplopia (blurry, then double vision).
  • 15% have bulbar symptoms (midbrain, pons, medulla): dysarthria (garbled speech), dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), fatigue when chewing (one patient desaturated every time he ate).
  • Less than 5% have proximal weakness alone.

Diagnosis

  • Bedside tests for ptosis: easy and sensitive, but many false positives.
  • Tensilon test: an IV acetylcholinesterase inhibitor with a very quick onset, lasting 5 to 10 minutes; watch whether the ptosis improves.
  • Ice pack test: ice in a surgical glove on the eyelid for 2 minutes; ptosis improves (neuromuscular transmission improves at lower muscle temperatures).
  • Labs for autoantibodies; EMG and nerve conduction tests.

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (first line)Relief in minutes; dosing may vary day to day with the symptoms. The main drug: pyridostigmine bromide ("the only one I would commit to memory").
ImmunotherapySuppresses the immune system: prednisone, Imuran, cyclosporine.

MG Collaborative Care

  • Hospital admission is usually for a respiratory infection or a myasthenic crisis.
  • Focus on neurologic deficits and their impact on ADLs.
  • A balanced diet that can be chewed and swallowed: usually semi-solid because of the dysphagia.
  • Schedule medications to peak at mealtimes (enough jaw muscle activation to eat).
  • Diversional activities that need minimal exertion (they fatigue so easily).
  • Teach what causes flares, the meds, the complications, and support groups.

Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

A chronic, inflammatory, autoimmune disorder causing demyelination and axonal injury in the brain and spinal cord. Like MG, it has remissions and exacerbations; it is potentially disabling, with a highly variable pace and many atypical forms. Lhermitte's sign is a specific cue of MS (a student reported it on a HESI).

Cues (plentiful and all over the place)

  • Changes in limb sensation; visual changes, diplopia, nystagmus (eyes moving back and forth).
  • Motor changes, gait and balance problems, limb ataxia; vertigo; facial sensations.
  • Spasticity (spastic, not flaccid, muscles).
  • Intention tremors: worse with purposeful movement, not at rest (the opposite of Parkinson's).
  • Bladder and bowel problems; sexual dysfunction.
  • Cognitive problems: subtle personality changes, memory loss; depression, fatigue.
  • Ringing in the ears and hearing loss; pain (uncommon).
  • Heat intolerance: heat causes flares (no more hot yoga).

Screening and diagnosis

  • Medical history and an in-depth neuro exam (gait, posture, coordination, balance).
  • MRI to reveal MS lesions.
  • Evoked potentials (the visual checkerboard test).
  • Lumbar puncture: the CSF may show increased WBCs and/or protein.
  • Requires a pattern, such as two or more exacerbations, separated by a month, each lasting more than 24 hours, with recovery; or a defined history of flares and remissions; or slow progression over months.
  • Often a story of uncertainty and frustration and multiple doctors before the diagnosis.

MS Plan of Care

  • Drug therapy is not tested in 405: it's highly individualized. The three goals: modify the disease, treat the relapses, manage the symptoms.
  • PT and OT: adapting daily tasks, mobility assistance.
  • Counseling and coping strategies for the patient and family; maintain normal activities, friends, hobbies, support groups.
  • Teach self-care: learn the flare triggers and avoid them; extra rest to reduce fatigue and flares.
  • Regular aerobic exercise (not lying in bed): muscle tone, balance, coordination, depression. Swimming is excellent.
  • Environmental control: avoid very hot weather; they do better in cooler environments.
  • No special diet: just well balanced.

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

Lou Gehrig's disease: a relentlessly progressive, presently incurable neurodegenerative disorder causing muscle weakness, disability and eventually death. Median survival is 3 to 5 years; 10% live 10 years or longer. A devastating trajectory: the patient remains cognitively intact while physically wasting away.

Cues

  • Asymmetric limb weakness is the most common presentation (one side, not the other).
  • Upper extremity: often a weak hand or shoulder girdle. Lower extremity: often weak dorsiflexion (foot drop: they stumble).
  • Symptoms spread within the area of onset, then to other regions in a fairly predictable pattern.
  • Fatigue; dysarthria (garbled speech) and dysphagia (difficulty swallowing).
  • Muscle atrophy and spasticity; fasciculations (twitching, as in the face or tongue).
  • Ultimately respiratory muscle weakness (intercostals, diaphragm): life-threatening.

Plan of care

  • Riluzole: the only FDA-approved drug for ALS; not a cure, but it extends survival.
  • PT consult: moderate-intensity endurance exercises for the trunk and limbs to reduce spasticity.
  • Support cognitive and emotional function; facilitate communication with speech therapy.
  • Keep the patient NPO once the risk for aspiration begins.
  • Pain assessment: they may not move well, but they still have sensation and still feel pain.
  • Minimize injury risk; diversional activities; anticipatory grieving for the patient and family.

Seizure Disorders

Seizures are transient, uncontrolled electrical discharges of neurons in the brain that interrupt normal function. Many things cause seizures; if the underlying cause can be removed, it isn't called a seizure disorder or epilepsy.

Generalized: tonic-clonicBoth sides of the brain. Stiffening and jerking; loss of consciousness, falling, possible salivation and incontinence. Then the postictal phase: no memory, nearly unarousable. Keep them safe and let them rest (don't shake them awake).

The Four Phases

Diagnosis

  • An accurate, comprehensive description of the seizure activity; health history.
  • An EEG, ideally as soon as possible after the seizure; it only helps diagnostically if the results are abnormal.
  • Rule out metabolic disorders and other causes.

During and After a Seizure

First aid during the seizure

  • ABCs: a patent airway; suction handy; breathing assistance afterward if needed.
  • Some agencies pad the rails.
  • Don't force anything into the mouth (no tongue blade).
  • Don't restrain unless something places the patient in danger.
  • Stay with the patient until the seizure ends.
  • Turn the head to the side if possible, but don't force it.

Monitor and document

  • Glasgow Coma Scale (eyes, motor, verbal) and pupil size and reactivity.
  • Onset and duration of the seizure.
  • Cyanosis (seen with tonic-clonic; usually self-limiting, but note it).
  • Let the person rest until fully awake; be reassuring and supportive.
Status epilepticus is a medical emergency: a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or repeated (back-to-back) seizures over 30 minutes. The airway is the number one concern; call the rapid response team (ICU if unresolved). The drugs of choice are benzodiazepines: lorazepam or diazepam ("-pam"). Seizures lasting uninterrupted more than 10 minutes can cause death, so the seizure activity must be interrupted.

Long-Term Management

Drugs and safety

  • A patient at risk for seizures in the hospital needs IV access (like a patient at risk for dysrhythmias); a medical alert ID.
  • Antiepileptic drugs: start with one and titrate to control the seizures.
  • Older drugs have a narrow therapeutic index, so drug levels are drawn. The classic example is phenytoin (Dilantin): a favorite of the NCLEX and HESI.
  • Phenytoin side effect to remember: gingival hyperplasia.
  • Teach adherence and never stop abruptly (that can cause a seizure). Half of ER seizure visits in one study were from non-adherence.

Surgery and psychosocial care

  • Surgical options: resecting part of the brain, or a vagal nerve stimulator ("a pacemaker for the brain") under the skin that sends regular mild pulses through the vagus nerve to prevent seizures.
  • Psychosocial ramifications are the most common complication of living with seizures: loss of independence, employment, insurance, the ability to drive, self-esteem.
  • Driving laws vary by state (all require a period without driving after a seizure); not tested.
  • Referrals, counseling, epilepsy support groups.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

An acute, immune-mediated polyneuritis: progressive, usually ascending, fairly symmetric muscle weakness with absent or depressed deep tendon reflexes (DTRs, not mentioned with the other disorders). Very often preceded by a viral infection, immunization, trauma or surgery.

Cues and course

  • Patients present a few days to a week after symptoms start; weakness ranges from mild difficulty walking to nearly complete paralysis.
  • Paresthesia in the hands and feet is very common; pain in the back and extremities.
  • Dysautonomia (the "automatic" nervous system): tachycardia, labile vital signs, ileus, urinary retention.
  • Progresses over about 2 weeks; by 4 weeks, 9 of 10 have reached the nadir (the peak).

Diagnosis

  • History and physical.
  • CSF from a spinal tap shows changes only after 1 week of onset.
  • Nerve conduction tests / EMG: confirm the diagnosis and help with the prognosis.
GBS can deteriorate quickly: 15 to 30% end up on mechanical ventilation. Vigilance: assess the gag and corneal reflexes, take frequent vital signs (autonomic changes), and expect ICU care if very hemodynamically labile.

GBS Care and Treatment

  • Nutrition: careful with any dysphagia; otherwise a nutrient-dense diet.
  • Neuropathic pain management (likely medication).
  • Monitor that the patient is voiding (autonomic disruption); support and encouragement through a variable recovery.
  • Plasmapheresis (removes circulating antibodies, complement and soluble biologic response modifiers): every other day over 10 days.
  • IVIG over 5 days. Both have equal efficacy, and must be given within 4 weeks of onset: time is of the essence.
  • Prognosis is very good: 95% survive, 75% recover completely, 25 to 30% have residual weakness after 3 years, and only 3% have weakness and tingling many years later.

Also called a CVA (cerebrovascular accident) or a "brain attack" (the National Stroke Association's term, to convey urgency like a heart attack). Stroke is an umbrella term, like cancer: about 87% of strokes are ischemic.

Risk Factors and TIA

Modifiable risk factors (teach these)

  • High blood pressure, heart disease, diabetes, smoking, obesity (often with sleep apnea), metabolic syndrome.
  • Lack of exercise, poor diet, drug and alcohol use.
  • They're interconnected: weight loss, for example, can improve diabetes and sleep apnea.
  • Kentucky sits in the Stroke Belt ("the belt buckle").

Transient ischemic attack (TIA)

  • A TIA is to a stroke as angina is to an MI: transient ischemia, a warning, with no tissue death.
  • Symptoms usually last less than an hour.
  • Unpredictable: one third never have another, one third have more TIAs, and one third progress to stroke.

Clinical Manifestations

The cues depend on the location of the stroke, not on whether it is ischemic or hemorrhagic. A stroke on one side of the brain causes weakness on the opposite side of the body (right brain, left-sided weakness, and vice versa). Right-brain strokes may make patients impulsive (dangerous: they act without thinking); left-brain strokes involve more emotion. (The side-specific details won't be teased out on the exam.)

Prevention and Acute Treatment

Prevention

  • Health promotion: healthy diet, weight control, exercise, no smoking, limited alcohol, BP management, routine checkups.
  • Antiplatelet drugs after a TIA: usually aspirin 81 mg.
  • For a TIA from carotid disease: carotid endarterectomy (removing the plaque-filled lining), carotid stenting, or transluminal angioplasty.
  • Patients do better on a dedicated stroke unit.

tPA for ischemic stroke

  • Most strokes are treatable, and time is of the essence; the time of onset is critical to collect (triage).
  • The fibrinolytic alteplase (recombinant tPA) re-establishes blood flow through the blocked artery to prevent cell death.
  • FDA-approved within 3 hours; the American Stroke Association endorses up to 4.5 hours under certain criteria. Patients are carefully screened.
  • Blood on the CT means no tPA ("you can't see a hemorrhage and give tPA").
  • Newer formulations are an IV push (the original was a bolus plus an infusion over an hour).

Ischemic stroke: the acute phase

  • Goals: preserve life, prevent further brain damage, reduce disability.
  • Circulation, airway and breathing; a baseline neuro assessment, then close monitoring: many patients get worse in the first 24 to 48 hours.
  • Elevated BP is common right after a stroke (possibly protective, maintaining cerebral perfusion): expect specific BP guidelines from the provider.
  • Fluid and electrolyte balance, adequate hydration; measures to manage ICP.
  • Once stable: anticoagulants and platelet inhibitors to prevent more clots, and statins (atherosclerosis is a big risk factor).
  • Mechanical thrombectomy with a stent retriever (see below).

Hemorrhagic stroke

  • The same goals and ABCs; these patients often have unstable intracranial pressures.
  • Surgery depends on the cause: evacuation of the hematoma, resection, radiosurgery, or clipping or coiling an aneurysm.
  • Clipping cuts off the blood supply to the aneurysm so it's less likely to rupture.
  • Coiling (a GD detachable coil): soft platinum coils threaded from a vessel in the leg pack the aneurysm until it's filled, so blood can't circulate in it and the rupture risk drops.

Acute Care Nursing Assessment

  • Primary assessment: cardiac, respiratory and neurologic status. If stable: the onset, duration, nature and changes of the illness; prior similar events; current meds; risk factors; family history of stroke, aneurysm or cardiovascular disease.
  • Secondary assessment: a comprehensive neuro exam: LOC; the NIH Stroke Scale (cognition and motor abilities; know what it is, no specific scoring); cranial nerves; sensation; proprioception (finger to nose, heel to shin: awareness of position and movement); cerebellar function (motor control); deep tendon reflexes.

Neurointerventional Radiology (NIR)

Percutaneous procedures done through the blood vessels to fix problems in the brain or in the circulation feeding the brain and spinal cord ("glorified plumbers"). NIR treats: stroke (thrombectomy), carotid stenosis (stenting), aneurysms, AV malformations and fistulas, venous problems (venous stents), and chronic subdural hematomas (MMA embolization), and performs diagnostic angiograms (contrast and pictures of the brain's vessels).

Stroke Numbers and Scales

Why speed matters

  • Over 800,000 strokes a year in the US, close to 6 million survivors; a leading cause of disability in adults.
  • A large vessel occlusion (LVO) is stroke's equivalent of a STEMI: the patient loses almost 2 million neurons per minute, and the chance of a good outcome drops quickly.

The "stroke alphabet soup"

  • NIH Stroke Scale: the most important; a regimented neuro assessment scored from 0 (no stroke) up into the 20s to 40s (severe).
  • Modified Rankin Scale (mRS): outcome at 90 days. 0 to 2 is an OK outcome; at 3 and above the main thing is they can't walk.
  • TICI score: how well the blocked vessel was reopened.
  • Cincinnati (prehospital) Stroke Scale: a quick EMS field screen, not used in the hospital.

tPA and Mechanical Thrombectomy

  • tPA (tissue plasminogen activator, a clot-busting drug) arrived in the 1990s: a 55% relative increase in good outcomes at 3 months. It works well for small vessels, but not so much for the large vessels at the base of the brain.
  • Number needed to treat (NNT): how many patients must be treated for one to get the full benefit; the lower the NNT, the more effective the treatment.
  • Thrombectomy for large vessels: like snaking a drain, catheters are guided into the brain and the clot is removed with suction or a stent retriever (the stent expands into the clot, and the stent and clot are pulled out together).
  • In 2015, three trials were halted early because thrombectomy was so clearly beneficial that withholding it was no longer ethical. Patients presenting up to 24 hours after onset can still benefit.
  • Myths dispelled: a patient who got full-dose tPA can still have a thrombectomy; intubating a stroke patient is fine (you can examine an intubated patient, and not oxygenating makes the stroke worse); not every stroke needs a comprehensive center.
  • An AI-powered app sends CT scans from referring hospitals straight to the team's phones, cutting image review from about an hour to 12 minutes.

The NIR Nurse's Role

The neuro exam is key. Unlike most post-op patients, stroke and NIR patients can have major exam changes, and it is often the nurses who catch problems first through routine use of the NIH Stroke Scale. Also monitor the access site (often the femoral artery).
  • Stent patients need aspirin and Plavix so the stent doesn't clot off.
  • Many patients (especially in Kentucky) don't respond normally to Plavix. A VerifyNow test checks the response: a high value means not responding (switch to Brilinta and load), and a very low value means a hyper-responder (very sensitive; may need a different drug or rescheduling).
