Types of Stroke

Also called a CVA (cerebrovascular accident) or a "brain attack" (the National Stroke Association's term, to convey urgency like a heart attack). Stroke is an umbrella term, like cancer: about 87% of strokes are ischemic.

Risk Factors and TIA

Modifiable risk factors (teach these)

  • High blood pressure, heart disease, diabetes, smoking, obesity (often with sleep apnea), metabolic syndrome.
  • Lack of exercise, poor diet, drug and alcohol use.
  • They're interconnected: weight loss, for example, can improve diabetes and sleep apnea.
  • Kentucky sits in the Stroke Belt ("the belt buckle").

Transient ischemic attack (TIA)

  • A TIA is to a stroke as angina is to an MI: transient ischemia, a warning, with no tissue death.
  • Symptoms usually last less than an hour.
  • Unpredictable: one third never have another, one third have more TIAs, and one third progress to stroke.

Clinical Manifestations

The cues depend on the location of the stroke, not on whether it is ischemic or hemorrhagic. A stroke on one side of the brain causes weakness on the opposite side of the body (right brain, left-sided weakness, and vice versa). Right-brain strokes may make patients impulsive (dangerous: they act without thinking); left-brain strokes involve more emotion. (The side-specific details won't be teased out on the exam.)

Prevention and Acute Treatment

Prevention

  • Health promotion: healthy diet, weight control, exercise, no smoking, limited alcohol, BP management, routine checkups.
  • Antiplatelet drugs after a TIA: usually aspirin 81 mg.
  • For a TIA from carotid disease: carotid endarterectomy (removing the plaque-filled lining), carotid stenting, or transluminal angioplasty.
  • Patients do better on a dedicated stroke unit.

tPA for ischemic stroke

  • Most strokes are treatable, and time is of the essence; the time of onset is critical to collect (triage).
  • The fibrinolytic alteplase (recombinant tPA) re-establishes blood flow through the blocked artery to prevent cell death.
  • FDA-approved within 3 hours; the American Stroke Association endorses up to 4.5 hours under certain criteria. Patients are carefully screened.
  • Blood on the CT means no tPA ("you can't see a hemorrhage and give tPA").
  • Newer formulations are an IV push (the original was a bolus plus an infusion over an hour).

Ischemic stroke: the acute phase

  • Goals: preserve life, prevent further brain damage, reduce disability.
  • Circulation, airway and breathing; a baseline neuro assessment, then close monitoring: many patients get worse in the first 24 to 48 hours.
  • Elevated BP is common right after a stroke (possibly protective, maintaining cerebral perfusion): expect specific BP guidelines from the provider.
  • Fluid and electrolyte balance, adequate hydration; measures to manage ICP.
  • Once stable: anticoagulants and platelet inhibitors to prevent more clots, and statins (atherosclerosis is a big risk factor).
  • Mechanical thrombectomy with a stent retriever (see below).

Hemorrhagic stroke

  • The same goals and ABCs; these patients often have unstable intracranial pressures.
  • Surgery depends on the cause: evacuation of the hematoma, resection, radiosurgery, or clipping or coiling an aneurysm.
  • Clipping cuts off the blood supply to the aneurysm so it's less likely to rupture.
  • Coiling (a GD detachable coil): soft platinum coils threaded from a vessel in the leg pack the aneurysm until it's filled, so blood can't circulate in it and the rupture risk drops.

Acute Care Nursing Assessment

  • Primary assessment: cardiac, respiratory and neurologic status. If stable: the onset, duration, nature and changes of the illness; prior similar events; current meds; risk factors; family history of stroke, aneurysm or cardiovascular disease.
  • Secondary assessment: a comprehensive neuro exam: LOC; the NIH Stroke Scale (cognition and motor abilities; know what it is, no specific scoring); cranial nerves; sensation; proprioception (finger to nose, heel to shin: awareness of position and movement); cerebellar function (motor control); deep tendon reflexes.

Discharge is to home, long-term care or rehab (targeting physical, mental and social well-being: preventing further loss, balance training, coping strategies), plus stroke support groups.

Neurointerventional Radiology (NIR)

Percutaneous procedures done through the blood vessels to fix problems in the brain or in the circulation feeding the brain and spinal cord ("glorified plumbers"). NIR treats: stroke (thrombectomy), carotid stenosis (stenting), aneurysms, AV malformations and fistulas, venous problems (venous stents), and chronic subdural hematomas (MMA embolization), and performs diagnostic angiograms (contrast and pictures of the brain's vessels).

Stroke Numbers and Scales

Why speed matters

  • Over 800,000 strokes a year in the US, close to 6 million survivors; a leading cause of disability in adults.
  • A large vessel occlusion (LVO) is stroke's equivalent of a STEMI: the patient loses almost 2 million neurons per minute, and the chance of a good outcome drops quickly.

The "stroke alphabet soup"

  • NIH Stroke Scale: the most important; a regimented neuro assessment scored from 0 (no stroke) up into the 20s to 40s (severe).
  • Modified Rankin Scale (mRS): outcome at 90 days. 0 to 2 is an OK outcome; at 3 and above the main thing is they can't walk.
  • TICI score: how well the blocked vessel was reopened.
  • Cincinnati (prehospital) Stroke Scale: a quick EMS field screen, not used in the hospital.

tPA and Mechanical Thrombectomy

  • tPA (tissue plasminogen activator, a clot-busting drug) arrived in the 1990s: a 55% relative increase in good outcomes at 3 months. It works well for small vessels, but not so much for the large vessels at the base of the brain.
  • Number needed to treat (NNT): how many patients must be treated for one to get the full benefit; the lower the NNT, the more effective the treatment.
  • Thrombectomy for large vessels: like snaking a drain, catheters are guided into the brain and the clot is removed with suction or a stent retriever (the stent expands into the clot, and the stent and clot are pulled out together).
  • In 2015, three trials were halted early because thrombectomy was so clearly beneficial that withholding it was no longer ethical. Patients presenting up to 24 hours after onset can still benefit.
  • Myths dispelled: a patient who got full-dose tPA can still have a thrombectomy; intubating a stroke patient is fine (you can examine an intubated patient, and not oxygenating makes the stroke worse); not every stroke needs a comprehensive center.
  • An AI-powered app sends CT scans from referring hospitals straight to the team's phones, cutting image review from about an hour to 12 minutes.

Thrombectomy-capable ("primary plus")Primary stroke centers that do thrombectomy: few and far between.

Other NIR Procedures

ProblemKey Points
Carotid stenosisPlaque builds up where the carotid bifurcates. Treated with a carotid endarterectomy (in the OR) or a carotid stent. Inflating the balloon stimulates nerve endings that regulate heart rate and BP and can make the heart stop, so the patient is premedicated with glycopyrrolate.
Subarachnoid hemorrhageMost often from trauma; when non-traumatic, most often an aneurysm. 30% die, and half the survivors have deficits. Classic cue: a "thunderclap," worst headache of my life, with nausea, vomiting, neck pain and rigidity, lethargy.
Brain aneurysmAn outpouching of a weak vessel wall, usually at turns or branch points; about 5% of adults have one. Care: ABCs first, then CT; possibly a drain or ventriculostomy to divert CSF and relieve pressure; an angiogram; then surgical clipping (craniotomy) or, more commonly now, endovascular occlusion (coils, a WEB device, a pipeline stent), often through a needle stick in the wrist.
AVMs and AV fistulasTangles of vessels (AVMs are usually from birth; fistulas can follow trauma) that can bleed; shut down with a micro-catheter and a liquid glue (Onyx).
Chronic subdural hematomaCommon in older patients. Draining works but they can come back; MMA embolization closes the middle meningeal artery (which feeds the dura, not the brain) so the collection can go away.

The NIR Nurse's Role

The neuro exam is key. Unlike most post-op patients, stroke and NIR patients can have major exam changes, and it is often the nurses who catch problems first through routine use of the NIH Stroke Scale. Also monitor the access site (often the femoral artery).
  • Stent patients need aspirin and Plavix so the stent doesn't clot off.
  • Many patients (especially in Kentucky) don't respond normally to Plavix. A VerifyNow test checks the response: a high value means not responding (switch to Brilinta and load), and a very low value means a hyper-responder (very sensitive; may need a different drug or rescheduling).
