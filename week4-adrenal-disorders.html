Adrenal Disorders: Cushing's, Addison's & Pheochromocytoma

Built from the Week 4 adrenal disorders voiceover. Too much adrenal cortex hormone is Cushing's; not enough is Addison's disease; too much from the adrenal medulla (often a tumor) is a pheochromocytoma. Adrenal (Addisonian) crisis is covered in pathopharm with the endocrine emergencies.

Adrenal Hormones

Adrenal medulla (in the middle)

  • Secretes the catecholamines: epinephrine and norepinephrine.

Adrenal cortex (covers)

  • Glucocorticoids: the primary one is cortisol, which responds to stress by raising blood sugar and making energy available.
  • Mineralocorticoids: the primary one is aldosterone, the regulator of sodium. More aldosterone = more sodium = more water retention.
  • Sex steroids (androgens; not focused on here).
Aldosterone moves potassium; ADH doesn't. When aldosterone rises, sodium rises and potassium falls. ADH retains or loses pure water with no effect on potassium. ("Commit that to memory.")

Cushing's Syndrome

  • Cushing's disease (usually a tumor) is very rare. Most of the time it's Cushing's syndrome, a collection of signs and symptoms caused by long-term oral systemic steroids.
  • Steroids are wonderful anti-inflammatories and immunosuppressants, and sometimes nothing else works, but these are the side effects. (Why NSAIDs are loved: they don't cause Cushing's.)
  • Why each sign happens: excess glucose availability (hyperglycemia); fragile capillaries (bruising); protein breakdown and fat redistribution (truncal obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, skinny extremities, osteoporosis); suppressed immune and inflammatory response (poor healing, infection); CNS excitability (moods, insomnia); high aldosterone (sodium and fluid retention, hypertension, hypokalemia).

Priority problems

  • Risk for infection (immunosuppression).
  • Fluid balance; skin integrity (thin skin; any break is an infection risk).
  • Risk for injury (thin bones).
  • Ineffective coping (moods); body image.
  • Unstable blood glucose; potential hypokalemia.

Nursing and collaborative care

  • Rebalance the hormones. Disease (a tumor): adrenalectomy, or removing a tumor secreting ectopic ACTH (another paraneoplastic disorder). Drugs for the disease: not tested.
  • Syndrome (steroids for RA or COPD, for example): gradually decrease the drug, reduce the dose, or try every-other-day dosing, all provider supervised.
  • Monitor fluid balance, glucose, BP, infection risk and mood.
  • Diet: increase protein and potassium; decrease calories and sodium.

Addison's Disease

  • An insufficiency of the glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids (cortisol and aldosterone, plus androgens). The famous example: John F. Kennedy (tan, bronze appearance; thin frame).
  • Usually a slow degenerative destruction: subtle and insidious. Occasionally rapid onset: severe, abrupt, even life-threatening.
  • Low aldosterone: low sodium, low blood volume, low BP, salt cravings, dehydration, high potassium. Low cortisol: no energy, hypoglycemia, weakness, fatigue. Unsuppressed ACTH from the anterior pituitary causes the hyperpigmentation.

Treatment and Teaching

Hormone replacement (the mainstay)

  • Hydrocortisone (cortisol): two-thirds in the morning, one-third in the late afternoon, matching the diurnal rhythm (more cortisol in the morning).
  • Fludrocortisone (Florinef) for missing aldosterone, taken in the morning.
  • Possibly salt additives for heat and humidity.

Teaching is critical

  • Follow the dosing schedule closely; never stop abruptly. Replacement is forever.
  • The 3 x 3 rule: when stressed (sick, a big exam, a marathon), take 3 times the dose for 3 days, depending on the severity of the stress.
  • Keep an emergency supply (an emergency kit with IM hydrocortisone) and wear a medic alert bracelet.
Addisonian crisis: a major, abrupt adrenal insufficiency (for example a big stressor without the cortisol supplement) that can be fatal untreated. Treatment: IV cortisol replacement, saline and dextrose; oral hydrocortisone once they can take it, until the maintenance dose is reached, plus fludrocortisone if aldosterone is low. Nursing: these patients can be extremely hemodynamically unstable, so trend the vital signs closely, and keep the environment calm and stress-free.

Pheochromocytoma

What it is

  • A tumor of the adrenal medulla that secretes too much epinephrine and norepinephrine.
  • Often missed at first: only 0.1% of high blood pressures are caused by one.
  • Diagnosis is quite easy once suspected: serum catecholamines, possibly a 24-hour urine.

Cues and treatment

  • Intractable hypertension (can't be controlled).
  • The triad: palpitations, headache, sweating (diaphoresis).
  • Treatment: remove the tumor.
