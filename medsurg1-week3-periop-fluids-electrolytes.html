Perioperative Care, Fluids & Electrolytes

Med Surg I (NUR 334) review content. Built from the eleven NUR 334 Week 3 voiceovers: perioperative overview and pre-op (two parts), intra-op, post-op (two parts), fluid volume imbalances, and the sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus imbalances.

Perioperative Overview

PhaseWhat Happens
Pre-opThe anesthesiologist assesses and introduces; care is planned with the patient. The surgical site is marked (preventing wrong-site surgery). Very anxiety-producing for the patient.
Intra-opFrom being wheeled into the OR until transfer to the PACU. Nursing roles: scrub nurse, circulating nurse, RN first assist.
Post-opThe PACU: airway first, recovery from general anesthesia, pain management, close vital signs (patients can be hemodynamically unstable and change quickly). Then back to the ICU, step-down, or med surg unit.

Purposes and settings

  • Purposes: diagnosis, cure, palliation (treating symptoms), prevention, exploration, cosmetic improvement.
  • Ambulatory: home the same day. Same-day admit: surgery in the morning, admitted overnight (usually one night). Inpatient: in the hospital before and after.

Timing

  • Elective: not urgent; improves quality of life (cataracts). Low risk, but not no risk.
  • Semi-elective: more time-sensitive, but not needed within 24 hours (gallstones, fibroid bleeding).
  • Urgent: within 24 hours of diagnosis (hip fracture, an appendix about to rupture).
  • Emergency: can't be delayed, ideally within 2 hours (unstable GI bleed, subdural hematoma).

Pre-op

The Pre-op History

Steps in the interview

  • Review what is documented before the interview and just double-check it (asking the same questions repeatedly is annoying). Can happen in advance (a phone call) or on the day.
  • Confirm understanding of the procedure (teach-back: "tell me about the procedure you're going to have").
  • Previous surgeries and anesthetics; family history of anesthesia problems (malignant hyperthermia is familial).
  • All medications: prescription, over-the-counter, herbals.
  • Allergies and intolerances, including latex (see below).
  • Illicit drug use: not optional to ask, even if sensitive.
  • Tobacco: quitting 6 weeks before surgery is worth it (smoking compromises oxygen binding); quitting any time before is better than not at all.
  • Possibility of pregnancy.
  • End with: "Is there anything else you'd like to share with me?"
Latex allergy clues: allergy to kiwis, bananas, avocados, chestnuts, or a poinsettia plant; daily latex exposure; past reactions to balloons or condoms; a history of allergies and asthma. Latex allergy can be anaphylactic.

The Joint Commission requires a history and physical (H&P) in the chart before the OR. Diagnostic results must be in the chart too. Teach the patient what they'll see and feel (a cold room, warm blankets, bright lights) and the process rules (family, waiting areas).

Informed Consent

The surgeon's job

  • Full disclosure: diagnosis, purpose, risks (even common non-serious and serious uncommon ones), alternatives, the risk of no treatment, who performs it, short- and long-term costs, hospital stay, pain, recovery, loss of function, scarring.
  • The patient must show comprehension; signing is voluntary.

The nurse's job

  • Witness the signature and act as the patient advocate.
  • You can answer follow-up questions, but if the patient expresses uncertainty about the procedure, explore it, and the next best action is to let the provider know they are having second thoughts.
  • Minors, unconscious, or mentally incompetent patients: a legal representative; an emancipated minor can consent. A true medical emergency can override consent when life is at stake.

Day of Surgery & the Pre-op Checklist

Fasting and checklist

  • No more blanket "NPO after midnight." Typically liquids are held a few hours and food 8 or more hours, but follow the surgeon's orders (don't memorize the table; gut surgery may mean longer NPO).
  • Checklist: height and weight; baseline vital signs; H&P in the chart; signed consent; type and crossmatch; latest labs (H&H, coags/PT-INR); surgical site marked; ID and allergy bands; NPO since when; recent glucose; skin prepped (CHG wipes at UK).
  • Valuables (dentures, hearing aids, wigs, glasses): send them with family, or secure them per protocol.

Intra-op

RoleWhat They Do
Circulating nurseNot scrubbed, gowned, or gloved (but masked and capped); stays in the unsterile area, "runs the show," records nursing care.
Scrub nurseScrubbed in, gowned and gloved; stays inside the sterile field.
RN first assistA nursing role assisting the surgeon (at UK, often medical students assist).
CRNACertified registered nurse anesthetist; extensive schooling after nursing school plus ICU experience.

The four intra-op concepts: identification, safety, positioning, asepsis

  • At least two patient identifiers.
  • Site marking by the surgeon (and often the patient's initials) when there is a left and right or multiple structures (fingers, toes, spinal levels); as close to the incision as possible and visible after draping.
  • Time-out: the whole team stops before the incision to verify the patient, site, level, procedure, position, and implants or special equipment.
  • Positioning (supine, prone, lithotomy, side-lying) with secured extremities and padding: improper positioning over many hours can cause muscle strain, joint damage, pressure ulcers, and nerve damage.
  • A meticulously sterile field.
AnesthesiaWhat It Is
GeneralInhaled and/or injected drugs: loss of all sensation and consciousness, with amnesia. Phases: pre-induction, induction, maintenance, emergence. Given by an anesthesiologist or CRNA.
RegionalInjected near nerves to block an area: nerve block, Bier block, spinal, epidural.

Monitored anesthesia care (MAC)Formerly "conscious sedation": no inhaled agents; conscious but sedated (a colonoscopy, for example).

Intra-op nursing concerns: infection, positioning injury, and imbalanced body temperature (patients can become hypothermic in the OR).

Malignant Hyperthermia

Malignant hyperthermia: an inherited muscle disorder triggered by certain volatile anesthetics; rare, but high mortality if untreated ("all hands on deck"). The most common initial sign is an unexpected rise in end-tidal CO2 (hypercarbia), not a fever. Also tachycardia, masseter (jaw) muscle spasm or generalized rigidity, muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis), and metabolic acidosis. Hyperthermia comes later. Antidote: dantrolene IV (worth memorizing).

Not predictable

  • Familial tendency, but some patients have no family history.
  • Over 50% had uneventful general anesthetics before, so past uneventful surgeries don't rule it out.

Post-op

PACU basics

  • All orders must be rewritten after surgery; pre-op orders don't carry over. Read every new order.
  • PACU handoff: patient history, intra-op management, vital signs, lab results, estimated blood loss.
  • Initial PACU assessment: airway, breathing, circulation, pain, level of consciousness, voiding, bowel sounds, safety needs.
  • Possible problems: neuro/psych, GI (delayed gastric emptying, paralytic ileus), urinary (trouble voiding, infection with a Foley), respiratory (especially with lung history or smoking), cardiovascular, integument (dehiscence, hematoma, infection), fluids and electrolytes.

Respiratory

Problems

  • Hypoxia from shallow breathing, anesthesia, obesity, respiratory depression (especially first-time narcotics), laryngeal spasm.
  • Atelectasis and pneumonia.

Prevention

  • Pulmonary toilet; head of bed elevated; vital signs and O2 sat; suction if needed.
  • Effective coughing with splinting for abdominal or thoracic incisions (a pillow; CABG patients get a red heart pillow). Coughing after these surgeries is mandatory.
  • Early mobilization.
  • Around-the-clock pain dosing so they can cough and move.

Cardiovascular

Decreased cardiac output and VTE

  • Monitor vital signs (BP is the indirect measure of cardiac output), skin temperature, and color (peripheral perfusion). Trend and compare to baseline.
  • DVT (most often elderly, obese, immobilized) can become a PE; together called VTE. Prevent with leg and ankle exercises (dorsiflex, plantar flex, circles: they mimic walking's muscle pump), SCDs, early ambulation, phlebitis assessment.
  • TED hose must fit correctly (an ill-fitting pair caused a stage 2 pressure ulcer); SCDs are used more now.

Neurologic & Psychological

Emergence delirium and more

  • A restless patient always means think hypoxia first (do they need more oxygen?); also pain, anesthetic agents, the ET tube.
  • Post-op delirium is more common in the elderly. Post-op depression exists (lack of sleep, lack of home support, body image, pain): assess mood and allow time to talk.
  • Assess LOC, orientation, memory, following commands, grips, moving extremities, pupils; always compare to baseline (was that weak left grip there before?).

Pain & Temperature

Pain

  • Post-op pain is continuous and predictable: around-the-clock dosing, not waiting for the patient to ask.
  • Pain increases as anesthesia wears off. Opioids and non-opioids first for severe pain, supplemented by non-drug measures.
  • Watch every patient on opioids for respiratory depression. Document outcomes.

Temperature

  • Often cold after surgery: warm blankets; check the temperature.
  • First 48 hours: a low-grade temp is usually expected, the inflammatory response to surgery; too soon for infection. Above 100.4, think lung congestion or dehydration: pulmonary toilet and fluids.
  • After 48 hours: an elevated temp raises concern for infection (wound, lungs); check the WBC and neutrophils.

GI

Paralytic ileus and more

  • Abdominal discomfort is common; mobilization often helps most.
  • Paralytic ileus is most likely after bowel manipulation. After colon surgery, large intestine motility may take 2 to 7 days (small intestine sooner). Signs: distended, tender belly; absent or high-pitched bowel sounds.
  • No bowel sounds at 24 to 48 hours isn't necessarily an ileus; peristalsis may not have kicked in yet. Watch for bowel sounds and passing gas.
  • Ileus treatment: NG tube to low wall suction, NPO ("don't feed a paralytic ileus").
  • Treat nausea (antiemetic); advance the diet as tolerated (NPO, clear liquids, full liquids); document intake; ambulate; hydrate; monitor bowel sounds every shift; privacy.
  • Opioids cause constipation, and patients never develop tolerance to it: be proactive about stool softeners (ask the provider if none is ordered).

Urinary

Voiding and output

  • Get the Foley out as soon as possible; watch for UTI (in older patients, the first cue is often confusion).
  • Urine output greater than 30 mL/hour (rule of thumb). Urine output reflects renal perfusion: falling BP and cardiac output lower it.
  • Assess the urge to void, palpate the bladder, use the bladder scanner, normal voiding position, running water.
  • No void within 6 to 8 hours after surgery: get an order to catheterize (usually in-and-out).

Integument, Tubes & Drains

Wounds

  • Assess drainage (amount, color, odor, consistency) and erythema; purulent drainage is not expected.
  • Dehiscence: the wound opens. Evisceration: the insides come out. Full care in Week 5: Complications of Wounds
  • At risk: large or obese, older, coughing a lot.
  • Dressings clean, dry, intact; meticulous asepsis; nutrition is paramount (no wound heals without it).

Tubes, drains, catheters to know

  • Indwelling and suprapubic catheters; ostomies; G-tube (gastrostomy) to drainage; Tenckhoff (peritoneal dialysis) catheter; rectal tube/fecal management system; NG to low wall suction.
  • Penrose drain; closed suction drains (Hemovac, Jackson-Pratt); a T-tube; chest tube after pleural/lung surgery.
  • Know what each is for and monitor its output.

Fluid Volume Imbalances

Review from 325

  • Oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure: albumin, the primary colloid, has "pulling power" that keeps fluid in the intravascular space. Low albumin: fluid leaks into the interstitium and can third-space.
  • Hydrostatic pressure: "pushing power." In heart failure, backed-up fluid raises it and pushes fluid into the interstitium.
  • Third spacing: fluid collecting where it serves no purpose (pleural effusion, peritoneum, pericardium). Pulmonary edema = fluid in the lungs; peripheral edema = in the ankles.

Nursing management

  • Daily weights are the key, most reliable measure: same scale, same clothing, same time of day. I&O is less reliable (urine not saved, unreported drinks).
  • Serum and urine osmolality (particles per solution: how concentrated).
  • Trend vital signs; respiratory status (pulmonary edema brings patients in); safety with orthostatic hypotension (assist when up); skin care (dry skin or pitting edema both risk breakdown).
Delegation to a UAP (NCLEX loves these): daily weights, vital signs, I&O (abnormal numbers come back to you to interpret), frequent oral care (helps on a fluid restriction), skin care and repositioning, elevating edematous extremities (promotes venous return), and encouraging fluids for a deficit.

Electrolytes

The two ranges to commit to memory: sodium 135 to 145 and potassium 3.5 to 5. You don't need to memorize calcium, magnesium, or phosphorus numbers; a question will imply whether they are high or low.

Sodium

The major extracellular cation. "Salt sucks, and water follows salt": sodium governs osmolality (most of the body's particles per solution). It also activates muscle and nerve cells.

FeatureHypernatremia (greater than 145)Hyponatremia (less than 135)
CausesExcess sodium intake (hypertonic IV fluids; tube feeding without free water). Inadequate water intake (the elderly's lost thirst; anorexia; depression). Excess water loss (insensible loss, diarrhea, osmotic diuretics, DKA, diabetes insipidus, Cushing syndrome).Excess sodium loss (GI losses, diuretics, burns and wounds); not taking in enough sodium. Excess water gain (hypotonic fluids such as half-normal saline or D5W). Heart failure, kidney failure, end-stage liver cirrhosis, SIADH (dilution). Common on med surg.
ManifestationsMental status changes (cells shrink): irritability, trouble concentrating, confusion, seizures, coma. With fluid deficit: postural hypotension, tachycardia, weakness.Mental status changes (cells swell): confusion (unsafe behavior), seizures. With fluid excess: fluid weight gain, increased BP.
TreatmentTreat the cause: water lost = add water back (hypotonic IV fluid); excess sodium = remove sodium. Monitor serum sodium and osmolality.Mild: fluid restriction (concentrates the serum). Severe: 3% (hypertonic) saline, three times the sodium of 0.9% normal saline: a big deal, given over 24 to 48 hours (closer to 48). Safe environment; seizure precautions.
Sodium out of balance? Think mental status changes. And correct sodium slowly (over a couple of days): fast fluid shifts in brain cells can be life-threatening. Potassium can be corrected faster than sodium.

The four usual reasons for a fluid restriction: heart failure, kidney failure (end-stage CKD), hyponatremia, and severe liver disease.

Potassium

The major intracellular cation; regulates cell excitability and electrical status. Out of range? Think cardiac dysrhythmias (EKG changes, ventricular dysrhythmias): these patients go on telemetry.

FeatureHyperkalemia (greater than 5)Hypokalemia (less than 3.5)
CausesIV fluids with KCl plus renal insufficiency; shifts out of the cell (acidosis, tissue breakdown, intense exercise); failure to eliminate (kidney disease with oliguria: the main reason seen in the hospital); adrenal insufficiency (Addison's, later).Loop and thiazide diuretics (furosemide, HCTZ); GI losses (vomiting, diarrhea); excessive sweating; dialysis overshooting; shift into the cell (insulin therapy, lots of D5, DKA treatment, alkalosis); NPO without potassium in IV fluids; not eating.
ManifestationsEKG changes and dysrhythmias; fatigue, irritability, muscle weakness, loss of tone, paresthesia, confusion.EKG changes and dysrhythmias; muscle weakness, cramps, paresthesia. Hypokalemia precipitates digoxin toxicity (digoxin + loop diuretics is common; narrow therapeutic index).
TreatmentHave the provider stop potassium-containing IV fluids. Increase excretion: loop or thiazide diuretics (if the kidneys work), dialysis (anuric CKD), Veltassa or Kayexalate (take hours to days). Dangerously high (6.5 or 7): act now (emergency dialysis); temporary fixes that push K back into the cell: insulin and dextrose, sodium bicarbonate if acidotic. Continuous EKG monitoring.Increase intake: PO foods; IV KCl (for example 10 mEq every hour × 4, then recheck). IV KCl is always diluted, never pushed, never more than 10 mEq/hour outside the ICU with a central line. It is a vesicant and can burn (slowing the rate helps).

Potassium safety points

  • A patient on a loop or thiazide diuretic should usually have a potassium supplement ordered; if not, check that morning's potassium and ask. Never give a supplement without knowing the potassium.
  • IV fluids with potassium are labeled in red at UK (for example normal saline with 20 or 40 mEq KCl). Never bolus a potassium-containing bag: get plain 0.9% normal saline for the bolus.
  • Know high-potassium foods (select-all-that-apply favorites) to teach patients on potassium-losing diuretics, or to avoid when potassium is high.

Calcium

Mostly in bone (strength); needed for membrane potential and nerve excitability. Only 1% is in the cells: half protein-bound, half ionized (available).

FeatureHypercalcemiaHypocalcemia
CausesHyperparathyroidism (number one; the four parathyroids regulate serum calcium); less commonly cancers, including paraneoplastic secretion (for example, lung cancer).Renal failure (number one); parathyroid deficiency or removal; blood transfusions (the citrate anticoagulant binds calcium), especially massive transfusion protocol.
Manifestations"Excess calcium is like a sedative": reduced muscle and nerve excitability, confusion, psychosis, seizures, coma.Tetany (excess nerve excitability, sustained muscle contraction); Chvostek's sign (tap at the mandible/cheek: twitching) and Trousseau's sign (BP cuff inflated: carpopedal spasm), which NCLEX loves; EKG changes.
TreatmentMild: conservative (decrease calcium intake, weight-bearing, hydration). Severe: a bisphosphonate (pamidronate, the gold standard if from cancer), IV isotonic saline, calcitonin (short-term; increases renal calcium excretion).Mild: calcium-rich foods, calcium supplements, vitamin D. Symptomatic: IV calcium gluconate.

Magnesium

Stabilizes cardiac and smooth muscle cells.

FeatureHypermagnesemiaHypomagnesemia
CausesRenal failure (most common): a CKD patient taking Maalox for an upset stomach; too much IV magnesium sulfate (OB).Malnutrition plus GI losses (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, wound drainage); chronic alcohol abuse.
ManifestationsEverything slowed down: lethargy, drowsiness, muscle weakness, decreased DTRs.Everything revved up (like hypocalcemia): cramps, tremors, possible seizures, vertigo, hyperactive DTRs, Chvostek's and Trousseau's, increased pulse and BP, dysrhythmias (torsades; not needed yet).
TreatmentAvoid magnesium-containing drugs (Maalox, Mylanta); limit dietary magnesium (green vegetables, nuts, bananas, oranges, peanut butter, chocolate); force fluids if the kidneys work, dialysis if not; IV calcium gluconate if symptomatic (opposes magnesium's effect on the heart).Replace: oral (Mylanta, magnesium sulfate) or IV magnesium sulfate, slowly over several days (faster can cause hypotension); monitor closely.

Phosphorus

Calcium and phosphorus are always inversely related: calcium up, phosphorus down; phosphorus up, calcium down.
FeatureHyperphosphatemiaHypophosphatemia
CausesKidney failure (number one; it also causes low calcium); phosphorus-containing laxatives; an underactive parathyroid.Malnutrition (chronic alcohol abuse); lower GI losses.
ManifestationsUsually asymptomatic by itself: the symptoms (muscle spasms, paresthesia, tetany, Chvostek's, Trousseau's) come from the accompanying hypocalcemia.CNS depression and muscle weakness, including respiratory muscles (life-threatening).
TreatmentCalcium-based phosphate binders (calcium carbonate); dialysis if severe.Replace (IV or oral, over hours); watch calcium alongside phosphorus (often CKD patients).
