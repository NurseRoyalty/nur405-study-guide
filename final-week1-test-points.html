CV Labs & Diagnostics

Know for the exam

  • Troponin is the most sensitive marker of myocardial damage: up in 2 to 3 hours, back to normal in 10 to 14 days.
  • Rule of thumb: BNP or pro-BNP elevated, think heart failure; troponin elevated, think MI.
  • ★ CK-MB is historically important and NCLEX still likes it: not specific to the heart, up about 3 hours after an MI.
  • ★ The one lipid number to know: total cholesterol less than 200. Other lipid questions are in general terms.
  • A 12-lead EKG can diagnose an MI; telemetry only monitors. ST elevation on tele means get a 12-lead.
  • Ejection fraction (by echo or cath) is very important information for a patient who may have heart failure.
  • Contrast dye: check BUN and creatinine first and force fluids afterward (nephrotoxic).
  • After a femoral heart cath: watch for bleeding and hematoma, hold pressure, bed rest with the leg straight and the head of bed below 30°, and check distal pulses, color and sensation.
  • TEE: NPO beforehand and until the gag reflex returns.

Not Tested

  • Myoglobin.
  • Normal ranges for BNP, LDL, HDL or triglycerides (only total cholesterol under 200).

CAD & Acute Coronary Syndrome

Know for the exam

  • Stable angina is predictable and relieved by rest or nitro; unstable angina, NSTEMI and STEMI make up ACS.
  • Troponin separates unstable angina (normal) from NSTEMI and STEMI (elevated).
  • At home: if chest pain isn't better 5 minutes after one nitro, call 911 and chew an aspirin (the old three-nitro teaching is out of date).
  • Always check the BP before nitro; never combine nitro with ED drugs; never defibrillate over a nitro patch.
  • Ask about pressure, not just pain. 12-lead within 10 minutes of arrival.
  • Time is muscle: PCI within 90 minutes, thrombolytics within 30.
  • A rising heart rate after PCI can mean internal bleeding.
  • No thrombolytics with a history of hemorrhagic stroke, recent abdominal surgery, active bleeding or abnormal coagulation studies.
  • Dysrhythmia is the most common complication of an MI.

Not Tested

  • The MI location lingo (inferior, lateral, anterior septal).
  • Which vessel the surgeon uses for a CABG graft.

Pericarditis

Know for the exam

  • The hallmark finding is the pericardial friction rub, at the lower left sternal border.
  • Pain is positional: better leaning forward, worse on inspiration, not relieved by nitro.
  • Diffuse ST elevation in almost all leads (an MI shows it in two contiguous leads).
  • The hallmark treatment is good old-fashioned NSAIDs (naproxen, high-dose ibuprofen); antibiotics only if bacterial; bed rest with head of bed at 45°.
  • Cardiac tamponade is time-sensitive and life-threatening: muffled heart sounds, JVD, narrowed pulse pressure, pulsus paradoxus; emergency pericardiocentesis.

Infective Endocarditis

Know for the exam

  • ★ Commit these findings to memory ("great NCLEX questions"; not seen in any other condition): splinter hemorrhages, petechiae, Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, Roth spots.
  • Osler nodes are tender (fingertips and toes); Janeway lesions are painless and flat (palms and soles).
  • Needs damaged epithelium plus a port of entry; flu-like at first; a new-onset murmur.
  • Three blood cultures; weeks of IV antibiotics. The most common complication is heart failure.
  • Vegetations embolize: left side to organs and limbs, right side to the lungs.
  • Prophylactic antibiotics before dental work and other listed procedures for high-risk patients.

Valvular Heart Disease

Know for the exam

  • ★ "You really need to know your valves": their location (landmarks) and purpose.
  • Stenosis won't open, regurgitation won't close, prolapse buckles; all end up looking like heart failure.
  • Repair before replace. Mechanical valves last longer but need lifetime anticoagulation and click; biologic valves need none but wear out sooner.
  • Mechanical valve teaching: INR monitoring for life, vitamin K is the warfarin antidote, consistent vitamin K intake, electric razor, no MRI, medical alert bracelet.
