Rheumatic Disorders: The Big Split

Osteoarthritis is a local joint disorder with only minimal local inflammation. Rheumatoid arthritis and lupus are systemic, with a lot of inflammation. Gout (gouty arthritis) is the fourth.

Rheumatic Blood Tests

C-reactive protein (CRP)Nonspecific; an indicator of an acute inflammatory process. Often ordered with the ESR.

ANA (antinuclear antibody)Antibodies that can destroy the nucleus of tissue cells. Positive in virtually everyone with SLE; may also be positive in RA (and in a few people with neither). Normally negative.

Osteoarthritis (OA)

Progressive (more symptomatic over time), with a low level of destructive inflammation at the joint, but no systemic manifestations: it's all local. Not a normal part of aging: there are things you can do to minimize and prevent it.

Cues

  • Joint pain (why they seek care) that worsens with use and is initially relieved by rest.
  • Temperature and barometric pressure changes trigger pain ("the rain is coming").
  • Stiffness worsens with inactivity; morning stiffness resolves quickly once they move (unlike RA).
  • Crepitus (snap, crackle, pop); osteophytes; a bowlegged appearance if bad enough; typically asymmetrical (unlike RA).
  • Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes (not inflamed, not tender); the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint; squaring off of the thumb joint.
  • Body parts: the neck and lower back (cervical and lumbar spine are the most mobile) and other joints.

Diagnosis

  • Bone scans, CT, MRI can show early changes.
  • X-rays stage the progression (bone on bone, osteophytes) but don't correlate with the degree of pain.
  • No biomarkers for OA.
  • ESR is normal unless there's synovial inflammation.
  • Synovial fluid is clear yellow with no signs of inflammation (unlike RA's synovitis).

OA Collaborative Care

  • A thorough multidimensional baseline pain assessment.
  • Non-drug interventions are the foundation: orthotics, assistive devices, avoid prolonged immobilization (no long bed rest: a balance of activity and rest), heat or cold, weight reduction, exercise and aerobic exercise, yoga, complementary therapies.
  • Glucosamine and chondroitin (OTC): fine to try, but no hard evidence they work.
  • Nursing problems: acute and chronic pain, impaired mobility, self-care deficit, overweight, depression.
  • Team: rheumatologists, PT, OT, nutritionists; arthritis.org for resources.
OA DrugKey Points
Acetaminophen (first)The drug of choice for mild to moderate pain without synovitis (not NSAIDs, despite the "-itis"). 3 g daily is the limit; 4 g only if limited duration and medically supervised, monitoring LFTs (hepatotoxic).
NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen)If acetaminophen isn't working or inflammation is present. Nephrotoxic, increased bleeding times, ulcers: monitor GI side effects and the kidneys.
COX-2 inhibitor (celecoxib)For GI side effects: inhibits only COX-2 (NSAIDs hit COX-1 and COX-2, and COX-1 inhibition causes much of the side effects).
Topical NSAIDOver the counter; the lecture described its active ingredient as chili peppers.
Steroid joint injectionsWhen nothing else works; temporary, unpredictable relief; possible brief flare after; infection risk; frequent injections damage the joint, so no more than 3 or 4 a year.
Hyaluronate injectionsMake joints more slippery; same side effects as steroid injections; usually for patients awaiting a joint replacement.
Systemic steroidsNot given for OA.

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)

Autoimmune, symmetrical, progressive and insidious: a highly inflammatory connective tissue disorder. Early symptoms are systemic: nonspecific fatigue, not wanting to eat, weight loss, generalized stiffness, then pain, worsening stiffness, limited motion, deformities and possibly disability. Morning stiffness is prolonged (OA improves in less than 30 minutes). RA has remissions and exacerbations. Often young patients, which can limit their ADLs and roles.

Extra-articular changes

  • Sjögren's syndrome (about 1 in 10 with RA): diminished lacrimal and salivary secretions: dry, gritty eyes, decreased tearing, dry mouth; very photosensitive. Also seen with other rheumatic disorders (lupus).
  • Rheumatoid nodules (2 or 3 in 10): benign, but a nuisance and a body image issue; on the elbow they risk impaired skin integrity.

Hand deformities

  • Ulnar deviation, knuckle and wrist subluxation (partial dislocation).
  • Swan neck fingers, boutonnière deformity, Z-shaped thumbs.
  • Joints: the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) and metacarpophalangeal (MCP, the knuckles), plus the wrists and feet (never mentioned with OA).

Diagnosing RA

  • Inflammatory arthritis in 3 or more joints; symptoms lasting more than 6 weeks.
  • RF positive in 80% (poorly specific, but providers order it).
  • ACPA (anti-CCP): more specific; may be positive very early.
  • ESR and CRP elevated.

RA Collaborative Care

Non-drug care

  • Rest inflamed joints, but maintain physical fitness: a happy medium.
  • 8 to 10 hours of sleep plus a nap.
  • Regular exercise (pain makes them not want to move, but mobility gets worse): ROM, isometric, isotonic and isokinetic exercise; swimming, cycling, walking for endurance.
  • Hand and finger splints (maintain alignment; may be worn while sleeping); PT and OT.
  • Heat and cold, most effective alternating, limited to 20 minutes or less.
  • Good dietary habits; biofeedback and cognitive behavioral therapy (education reduces depression and disability).

Drugs: time is of the essence

  • Drugs are the cornerstone of active RA treatment: goal is remission and preventing further damage. The earlier, the better.
  • DMARDs (disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs) reduce inflammation, reduce and prevent joint damage, preserve structure.
  • Methotrexate: improves symptoms in 4 to 6 weeks; start as soon as possible (much less effective if you wait).
  • Hydroxychloroquine: fullest benefit takes longer, about 2 to 3 months.
  • NSAIDs: more immediate pain relief but don't reduce long-term damage; must be taken continuously, around the clock, for several weeks for the anti-inflammatory effect; discontinued once the DMARDs are working.
  • Steroids: wonderful anti-inflammatories, but short term, early, the least possible dose for the shortest time (PO, IV or into the joint).

Gout

Terms

  • Gout: the metabolic problem, a disruption in uric acid metabolism, with urate salt deposits in tissues in and around joints.
  • Gouty arthritis: how gout affects the joint: acute, painful inflammation; recurring; often the great toe (not always).
  • Chronic tophaceous gout (less common, advanced): about 10 years after onset; tophi are small white nodules of urate crystals that are painless (body image, loss of hand use).
  • Cause: too much uric acid production or not enough excretion (kidneys), or lots of purine foods (purine is metabolized to uric acid). Primary (most) or secondary (some drugs; don't need to know which).

A flare

  • The joint looks "angry": very intense pain; even touching the bedsheets or wearing socks hurts.
  • Usually the great toe, usually in the early morning.
  • Edema, swelling, tenderness, erythema; possible fever and malaise.
  • Peaks in 1 to 2 days; the flare takes 5 to 10 days to dissipate.

Modifiable Risk Factors and Diet

  • Modifiable: hyperuricemia, obesity, high blood pressure, hyperlipidemia, cardiac disease, diabetes, CKD, dietary factors, alcohol: all teaching opportunities.
  • Weight loss decreases gout (proven better outcomes): a calorie-restricted diet, more protein, complex carbs in place of refined, less saturated fat.
  • Protein from low-fat dairy and plant sources, not red meat and fish.
  • Avoid frequent sugar-sweetened beverages, fatty meals, organ-rich foods, beer and distilled spirits, and anything that causes a flare.
  • A strict low-purine diet only lowers urate 15 to 20%, so the trend is weight loss, medication and the diet above: most gout patients will be on medication anyway.

Diagnosis, Care and Drugs

Diagnosis and nursing care

  • Serum uric acid elevated; a 24-hour urine for uric acid shows whether it's decreased renal excretion or overproduction.
  • Synovial fluid aspiration (needle-like crystals) is the gold standard, but usually not done: 80% are diagnosed without it.
  • Address modifiable risks; pain control; joint immobilization during a flare.
  • Keep the sheets off the toes with a little tent; no socks.
  • Encourage fluids to increase urine volume and prevent uric acid precipitation in the renal tubules (dehydration on a hot day triggered a flare).

Drugs (primary therapy)

  • Colchicine for an acute attack: usually dramatic relief within a day.
  • NSAIDs (ibuprofen) also for acute gout.
  • Allopurinol: prophylactic, for recurrent flares; takes 2 to 6 weeks to decrease flares.

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

The "look good, feel bad" disease. Autoimmune, multisystem and inflammatory; far more in women aged 20 to 40 (a productive phase of life); genetic, hormonal, environmental and immunologic components. Chronic and unpredictable, with remissions and flares. Affects the skin, joints, serous membranes (pleural and pericardial effusions), kidneys, blood and nervous system. African Americans have an increased risk; 50% develop damage in one or more organs. Patients are usually hospitalized for the complications, not the lupus itself.

SystemCues
JointsPolyarthralgia in 9 of 10: an early symptom that may precede others by years (insidious). Pain and stiffness move from one body part to another and usually don't affect both sides the same; only a few joints at a time (fingers, wrists, knees). Severe cases can deform the fingers like RA (swan neck).
SkinPhotosensitivity (a rash after light exposure); the butterfly rash in half (lasts a couple of days, can return). Most common on the face, shoulders and arms (sun-exposed); unusual or patchy hair loss, brittle hair; painless ulcers in the mouth and nose.

Diagnosing SLE

  • Based on criteria from history, physical exam and labs: no single test definitively diagnoses SLE.
  • ANA positive in virtually all patients at some point.
  • Anti-DNA antibody: more specific (rarely found in other disorders).
  • ESR and CRP elevated; serum complement decreased.
  • CBC: low RBCs, WBCs and platelets.
  • Urinalysis with lupus nephritis: proteinuria, hematuria, blood cell casts.

SLE Drugs and Nursing Care

Drug therapy (highly individualized)

  • Hydroxychloroquine is the cornerstone, used in nearly all patients.
  • Mild symptoms: NSAIDs, plus short-term low-dose prednisone (kept under 7.5 mg a day), tapered once hydroxychloroquine takes effect.
  • A steroid-sparing immunosuppressant: methotrexate.
  • Severe, life-threatening disease: intensive immunosuppressants and the highest-dose steroids to halt tissue injury.

Collaborative care

  • Build rapport with the patient and family; be sympathetic to the challenges of a chronic, unpredictable disease.
  • During exacerbations: fever, joint inflammation, limited movement, discomfort, fatigue, anxiety, and steroid adverse effects.
  • Space out activities; extra rest and naps (10+ hours at night plus a nap or two).
  • Monitor neuro status (lupus cerebritis; steroids can also cause mental changes).
  • Risk for infection (low WBC) and bleeding (low platelets); impaired skin integrity, body image, role performance.
SLE teaching: avoid triggers such as sunlight; don't stop the drugs abruptly; avoid infections (they cause flares); lupus doesn't go away; pregnancy and sexual counseling before becoming pregnant.
