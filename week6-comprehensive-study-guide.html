Electroencephalogram (EEG)

The test

  • Measures the brain's electrical activity: "the EEG is to the brain as the EKG is to the heart."
  • Assesses for the presence of different types of seizures.
  • Withhold stimulants for 8 hours beforehand (no activity from artificial stimulants).
  • Noninvasive and painless; at the bedside or in an EEG lab; usually 20 to 40 minutes.

The epilepsy monitoring unit

  • For patients with intractable seizures not helped by traditional therapies.
  • Their seizure medications are stopped before they come in, and they go on a continuous EEG.
  • The goal is for the patient to have a seizure there, so its location and severity can be recorded: like a cardiac EP study, deliberately triggering the problem in a controlled environment.

Lumbar Puncture (Spinal Tap)

A myelogram is an x-ray of the spinal cord after contrast is injected into the subarachnoid space. A lumbar puncture is different: the provider taps the spinal column to obtain cerebrospinal fluid.

Before and during

  • It's invasive: a consent form.
  • Have the patient empty the bladder (a smaller abdomen, more comfortable).
  • Position: lateral recumbent, curled in the fetal position, to open the space between the vertebrae. Some sit on a hard surface and lean over. The lecture also mentions a tilt table.

After

  • The patient may or may not need to lie flat for an hour. For years patients lay flat for hours to prevent a spinal headache, but current evidence doesn't show that longer bed rest means less headache. Follow the provider's orders.
  • Monitor for a spinal headache, caused by losing fluid from the spinal column.
Treating a spinal headache: start with bed rest and analgesics (possibly opioids; they can be severe). Next, a blood patch: blood drawn from the periphery is injected into the dura. IV caffeine has also been shown to help.

The tests named here (PET, EMG, repetitive nerve stimulation, single fiber EMG, evoked potentials, lumbar puncture) are explained on Neuro Labs & Dx.

Parkinson's Disease (PD)

Chronic, progressive, insidious (it happens over time without being noticed) and neurodegenerative. The pathology: not enough dopamine, so dopamine and acetylcholine are out of balance.

The Four Cardinal Features

FeatureKey Points
1. TremorA pill-rolling rest tremor: most notable when not doing purposeful activity, and it gets better with purposeful activity (unlike the action tremors of essential tremor or MS). The least debilitating cardinal feature. Present in about half; an internal tremulousness others can't see may come first. Starts on one side and spreads to the other over several years.
2. Muscle rigidityIncreased resistance to passive range of motion: "cogwheel," jerky or stuck when the elbow is flexed and extended. Also starts on one side and spreads.
3. BradykinesiaGeneralized slowness of movement, including the flat affect. Arguably the most major cause of disability, and the hardest for patients to describe ("I feel weak and uncoordinated," "super tired").
4. Postural instabilityComes later (the first three happen early). They lose their balance easily: high fall risk.

Non-motor cues (97% have them; average of 8 each)

  • Cognitive dysfunction and dementia; psychosis and hallucinations; mood disorders.
  • Olfactory dysfunction may come before the motor symptoms.
  • Sleep disturbances; insomnia is one of the most disturbing complaints.
  • Autonomic dysfunction (GI tract, kidneys); sexual dysfunction; pain (face, abdomen, genitals, joints) and sensory disorders.

What a PD patient looks like

  • Flat affect, blank facial expression; tilting forward.
  • Soft, slow, monotonous speech; dysarthria (slurred speech); hypophonia (soft voice).
  • Tremors and a shuffling gait; freezing while walking.
  • Micrographia: progressively shrinking handwriting (smaller when "off," bigger with effective medication).

Diagnosis and Drug Therapy

  • No specific diagnostic test: based on history and clinical features; at least two or three signs of the classic triad.
  • The ultimate confirmation is a trial of anti-Parkinson's drugs: do they respond?
  • No cure: treatment relieves symptoms by correcting the neurotransmitter imbalance (enhance dopamine release or antagonize the cholinergic neurons).
  • Levodopa is the drug of choice and the most effective for symptomatic PD (especially bradykinesia), with many side effects. It only works for a certain time (typically wearing off within about 5 years), so the provider is very particular about when to start it.
  • Also dopamine agonists as monotherapy and/or anticholinergics.

The on-off phenomenon

  • The invariable consequence of long-term levodopa: "on" = mobile, "off" = immobile.
  • At first, wearing off happens at the end of dosing intervals; eventually it becomes unpredictable.
  • Managed by shortening the interdose interval, controlled-release medications, and avoiding high-protein meals.

Deep brain stimulation

  • The most common surgical treatment for advanced PD.
  • Looks like a pacemaker, with leads into the brain; adjustable to control symptoms, and reversible.

Nursing Care for PD

Care and the team

  • Extra time to respond to questions and for ADLs (OT); a listening ear.
  • Give medications promptly and watch for side effects.
  • Prevent complications of immobility: constipation, contractures, skin breakdown (PT).
  • Speech therapy for dysphagia and hypophonia; prevent aspiration.
  • Dietitian: calorie-dense, nutrient-rich foods, more fluids, bite-sized pieces, small frequent meals.
  • Assess for depression and anxiety; psychosocial support; caregiver burden; support groups.

Teaching for freezing while walking

  • Consciously think about stepping over an imaginary line (or a small object, like a rice kernel).
  • Rock side to side to get momentum going.
  • Lift the toes when stepping.
  • Take one step backward, then two forward.
  • Remove rugs and other tripping hazards.

Myasthenia Gravis (MG)

"Grave muscle weakness." Acquired, autoimmune, progressive, characterized by muscle weakness from a reduction of acetylcholine receptors. It has remissions and exacerbations (flares), triggered by anything that stresses the person: pregnancy, illness, heat. Typically peaks within 2 years of onset. Severity ranges from mild to respiratory failure from muscle weakness. Takeaway: grave muscle weakness in the head area.

Presenting symptoms

  • Ocular MG (eyes and face) is the majority; the other type is generalized muscle weakness.
  • 50% present with ocular symptoms: ptosis (a droopy eyelid from muscle weakness, not sleepiness; one or both sides, can switch eyes, fluctuates through the day) and diplopia (blurry, then double vision).
  • 15% have bulbar symptoms (midbrain, pons, medulla): dysarthria (garbled speech), dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), fatigue when chewing (one patient desaturated every time he ate).
  • Less than 5% have proximal weakness alone.

Diagnosis

  • Bedside tests for ptosis: easy and sensitive, but many false positives.
  • Tensilon test: an IV acetylcholinesterase inhibitor with a very quick onset, lasting 5 to 10 minutes; watch whether the ptosis improves.
  • Ice pack test: ice in a surgical glove on the eyelid for 2 minutes; ptosis improves (neuromuscular transmission improves at lower muscle temperatures).
  • Labs for autoantibodies; EMG and nerve conduction tests.

MG Treatment

TreatmentKey Points
Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (first line)Relief in minutes; dosing may vary day to day with the symptoms. The main drug: pyridostigmine bromide ("the only one I would commit to memory").
ImmunotherapySuppresses the immune system: prednisone, Imuran, cyclosporine.
Short-term treatmentsUntil the medication takes effect, after surgery, or after a myasthenic crisis.
IVIGDials down the person's production of their own antibodies, calming the body until the medications take effect.
PlasmapheresisA plasma exchange (looks like hemodialysis) that removes the antibodies from the plasma to decrease symptoms; typically 6 exchanges over 2 weeks, with weekly or monthly follow-up.
ThymectomyThe thymus (in the mediastinum) seems to enhance the antibodies, so it may be removed.

MG Collaborative Care

  • Hospital admission is usually for a respiratory infection or a myasthenic crisis.
  • Focus on neurologic deficits and their impact on ADLs.
  • A balanced diet that can be chewed and swallowed: usually semi-solid because of the dysphagia.
  • Schedule medications to peak at mealtimes (enough jaw muscle activation to eat).
  • Diversional activities that need minimal exertion (they fatigue so easily).
  • Teach what causes flares, the meds, the complications, and support groups.

Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

A chronic, inflammatory, autoimmune disorder causing demyelination and axonal injury in the brain and spinal cord. Like MG, it has remissions and exacerbations; it is potentially disabling, with a highly variable pace and many atypical forms. Lhermitte's sign is a specific cue of MS (a student reported it on a HESI).

Cues (plentiful and all over the place)

  • Changes in limb sensation; visual changes, diplopia, nystagmus (eyes moving back and forth).
  • Motor changes, gait and balance problems, limb ataxia; vertigo; facial sensations.
  • Spasticity (spastic, not flaccid, muscles).
  • Intention tremors: worse with purposeful movement, not at rest (the opposite of Parkinson's).
  • Bladder and bowel problems; sexual dysfunction.
  • Cognitive problems: subtle personality changes, memory loss; depression, fatigue.
  • Ringing in the ears and hearing loss; pain (uncommon).
  • Heat intolerance: heat causes flares (no more hot yoga).

Screening and diagnosis

  • Medical history and an in-depth neuro exam (gait, posture, coordination, balance).
  • MRI to reveal MS lesions.
  • Evoked potentials (the visual checkerboard test).
  • Lumbar puncture: the CSF may show increased WBCs and/or protein.
  • Requires a pattern, such as two or more exacerbations, separated by a month, each lasting more than 24 hours, with recovery; or a defined history of flares and remissions; or slow progression over months.
  • Often a story of uncertainty and frustration and multiple doctors before the diagnosis.

MS Plan of Care

  • Drug therapy is not tested in 405: it's highly individualized. The three goals: modify the disease, treat the relapses, manage the symptoms.
  • PT and OT: adapting daily tasks, mobility assistance.
  • Counseling and coping strategies for the patient and family; maintain normal activities, friends, hobbies, support groups.
  • Teach self-care: learn the flare triggers and avoid them; extra rest to reduce fatigue and flares.
  • Regular aerobic exercise (not lying in bed): muscle tone, balance, coordination, depression. Swimming is excellent.
  • Environmental control: avoid very hot weather; they do better in cooler environments.
  • No special diet: just well balanced.

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

Lou Gehrig's disease: a relentlessly progressive, presently incurable neurodegenerative disorder causing muscle weakness, disability and eventually death. Median survival is 3 to 5 years; 10% live 10 years or longer. A devastating trajectory: the patient remains cognitively intact while physically wasting away.

Cues

  • Asymmetric limb weakness is the most common presentation (one side, not the other).
  • Upper extremity: often a weak hand or shoulder girdle. Lower extremity: often weak dorsiflexion (foot drop: they stumble).
  • Symptoms spread within the area of onset, then to other regions in a fairly predictable pattern.
  • Fatigue; dysarthria (garbled speech) and dysphagia (difficulty swallowing).
  • Muscle atrophy and spasticity; fasciculations (twitching, as in the face or tongue).
  • Ultimately respiratory muscle weakness (intercostals, diaphragm): life-threatening.

Plan of care

  • Riluzole: the only FDA-approved drug for ALS; not a cure, but it extends survival.
  • PT consult: moderate-intensity endurance exercises for the trunk and limbs to reduce spasticity.
  • Support cognitive and emotional function; facilitate communication with speech therapy.
  • Keep the patient NPO once the risk for aspiration begins.
  • Pain assessment: they may not move well, but they still have sensation and still feel pain.
  • Minimize injury risk; diversional activities; anticipatory grieving for the patient and family.

Seizure Disorders

Seizures are transient, uncontrolled electrical discharges of neurons in the brain that interrupt normal function. Many things cause seizures; if the underlying cause can be removed, it isn't called a seizure disorder or epilepsy.

Types of Seizures

TypeWhat It Looks Like
Generalized: tonic-clonicBoth sides of the brain. Stiffening and jerking; loss of consciousness, falling, possible salivation and incontinence. Then the postictal phase: no memory, nearly unarousable. Keep them safe and let them rest (don't shake them awake).
Generalized: absenceUsually young people; rare beyond adolescence. A brief staring spell that looks like daydreaming; unresponsive during it. Hard to diagnose (a teacher may think the child isn't paying attention).
Focal onset with retained awarenessOne side of the brain. No loss of consciousness; brief; unusual sensations in many forms.
Focal onset without retained awarenessEyes open but loss of consciousness or changed awareness, as if daydreaming; movements may look purposeful but you can't interact with them (they may walk into traffic or take off their clothes). 1 to 2 minutes; afterward confused and very fatigued.

The Four Phases

PhaseExamples
Prodromal (some patients)A sensation or behavior hours to days before: insomnia, euphoria, disorientation, light sensitivity.
AuraA sensory warning that a seizure is about to happen: zigzag lines in the vision, a metallic taste, sudden nausea.
IctalThe seizure itself, from the first symptoms to the end of the seizure activity.
PostictalThe recovery period after the seizure.

Diagnosis

  • An accurate, comprehensive description of the seizure activity; health history.
  • An EEG, ideally as soon as possible after the seizure; it only helps diagnostically if the results are abnormal.
  • Rule out metabolic disorders and other causes.

During and After a Seizure

First aid during the seizure

  • ABCs: a patent airway; suction handy; breathing assistance afterward if needed.
  • Some agencies pad the rails.
  • Don't force anything into the mouth (no tongue blade).
  • Don't restrain unless something places the patient in danger.
  • Stay with the patient until the seizure ends.
  • Turn the head to the side if possible, but don't force it.

Monitor and document

  • Glasgow Coma Scale (eyes, motor, verbal) and pupil size and reactivity.
  • Onset and duration of the seizure.
  • Cyanosis (seen with tonic-clonic; usually self-limiting, but note it).
  • Let the person rest until fully awake; be reassuring and supportive.
Status epilepticus is a medical emergency: a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or repeated (back-to-back) seizures over 30 minutes. The airway is the number one concern; call the rapid response team (ICU if unresolved). The drugs of choice are benzodiazepines: lorazepam or diazepam ("-pam"). Seizures lasting uninterrupted more than 10 minutes can cause death, so the seizure activity must be interrupted.

Long-Term Management

Drugs and safety

  • A patient at risk for seizures in the hospital needs IV access (like a patient at risk for dysrhythmias); a medical alert ID.
  • Antiepileptic drugs: start with one and titrate to control the seizures.
  • Older drugs have a narrow therapeutic index, so drug levels are drawn. The classic example is phenytoin (Dilantin): a favorite of the NCLEX and HESI.
  • Phenytoin side effect to remember: gingival hyperplasia.
  • Teach adherence and never stop abruptly (that can cause a seizure). Half of ER seizure visits in one study were from non-adherence.

Surgery and psychosocial care

  • Surgical options: resecting part of the brain, or a vagal nerve stimulator ("a pacemaker for the brain") under the skin that sends regular mild pulses through the vagus nerve to prevent seizures.
  • Psychosocial ramifications are the most common complication of living with seizures: loss of independence, employment, insurance, the ability to drive, self-esteem.
  • Driving laws vary by state (all require a period without driving after a seizure); not tested.
  • Referrals, counseling, epilepsy support groups.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

An acute, immune-mediated polyneuritis: progressive, usually ascending, fairly symmetric muscle weakness with absent or depressed deep tendon reflexes (DTRs, not mentioned with the other disorders). Very often preceded by a viral infection, immunization, trauma or surgery.

Cues and course

  • Patients present a few days to a week after symptoms start; weakness ranges from mild difficulty walking to nearly complete paralysis.
  • Paresthesia in the hands and feet is very common; pain in the back and extremities.
  • Dysautonomia (the "automatic" nervous system): tachycardia, labile vital signs, ileus, urinary retention.
  • Progresses over about 2 weeks; by 4 weeks, 9 of 10 have reached the nadir (the peak).

Diagnosis

  • History and physical.
  • CSF from a spinal tap shows changes only after 1 week of onset.
  • Nerve conduction tests / EMG: confirm the diagnosis and help with the prognosis.
GBS can deteriorate quickly: 15 to 30% end up on mechanical ventilation. Vigilance: assess the gag and corneal reflexes, take frequent vital signs (autonomic changes), and expect ICU care if very hemodynamically labile.

GBS Care and Treatment

  • Nutrition: careful with any dysphagia; otherwise a nutrient-dense diet.
  • Neuropathic pain management (likely medication).
  • Monitor that the patient is voiding (autonomic disruption); support and encouragement through a variable recovery.
  • Plasmapheresis (removes circulating antibodies, complement and soluble biologic response modifiers): every other day over 10 days.
  • IVIG over 5 days. Both have equal efficacy, and must be given within 4 weeks of onset: time is of the essence.
  • Prognosis is very good: 95% survive, 75% recover completely, 25 to 30% have residual weakness after 3 years, and only 3% have weakness and tingling many years later.

Types of Stroke

Also called a CVA (cerebrovascular accident) or a "brain attack" (the National Stroke Association's term, to convey urgency like a heart attack). Stroke is an umbrella term, like cancer: about 87% of strokes are ischemic.

Risk Factors and TIA

Modifiable risk factors (teach these)

  • High blood pressure, heart disease, diabetes, smoking, obesity (often with sleep apnea), metabolic syndrome.
  • Lack of exercise, poor diet, drug and alcohol use.
  • They're interconnected: weight loss, for example, can improve diabetes and sleep apnea.
  • Kentucky sits in the Stroke Belt ("the belt buckle").

Transient ischemic attack (TIA)

  • A TIA is to a stroke as angina is to an MI: transient ischemia, a warning, with no tissue death.
  • Symptoms usually last less than an hour.
  • Unpredictable: one third never have another, one third have more TIAs, and one third progress to stroke.

Clinical Manifestations

The cues depend on the location of the stroke, not on whether it is ischemic or hemorrhagic. A stroke on one side of the brain causes weakness on the opposite side of the body (right brain, left-sided weakness, and vice versa). Right-brain strokes may make patients impulsive (dangerous: they act without thinking); left-brain strokes involve more emotion. (The side-specific details won't be teased out on the exam.)

AreaCues
MotorImpaired mobility, breathing, swallowing, talking; impaired gag reflex; self-care deficits. Akinesia (loss of voluntary movement).

Prevention and Acute Treatment

Prevention

  • Health promotion: healthy diet, weight control, exercise, no smoking, limited alcohol, BP management, routine checkups.
  • Antiplatelet drugs after a TIA: usually aspirin 81 mg.
  • For a TIA from carotid disease: carotid endarterectomy (removing the plaque-filled lining), carotid stenting, or transluminal angioplasty.
  • Patients do better on a dedicated stroke unit.

tPA for ischemic stroke

  • Most strokes are treatable, and time is of the essence; the time of onset is critical to collect (triage).
  • The fibrinolytic alteplase (recombinant tPA) re-establishes blood flow through the blocked artery to prevent cell death.
  • FDA-approved within 3 hours; the American Stroke Association endorses up to 4.5 hours under certain criteria. Patients are carefully screened.
  • Blood on the CT means no tPA ("you can't see a hemorrhage and give tPA").
  • Newer formulations are an IV push (the original was a bolus plus an infusion over an hour).

Ischemic stroke: the acute phase

  • Goals: preserve life, prevent further brain damage, reduce disability.
  • Circulation, airway and breathing; a baseline neuro assessment, then close monitoring: many patients get worse in the first 24 to 48 hours.
  • Elevated BP is common right after a stroke (possibly protective, maintaining cerebral perfusion): expect specific BP guidelines from the provider.
  • Fluid and electrolyte balance, adequate hydration; measures to manage ICP.
  • Once stable: anticoagulants and platelet inhibitors to prevent more clots, and statins (atherosclerosis is a big risk factor).
  • Mechanical thrombectomy with a stent retriever (see below).

Hemorrhagic stroke

  • The same goals and ABCs; these patients often have unstable intracranial pressures.
  • Surgery depends on the cause: evacuation of the hematoma, resection, radiosurgery, or clipping or coiling an aneurysm.
  • Clipping cuts off the blood supply to the aneurysm so it's less likely to rupture.
  • Coiling (a GD detachable coil): soft platinum coils threaded from a vessel in the leg pack the aneurysm until it's filled, so blood can't circulate in it and the rupture risk drops.

Acute Care Nursing Assessment

  • Primary assessment: cardiac, respiratory and neurologic status. If stable: the onset, duration, nature and changes of the illness; prior similar events; current meds; risk factors; family history of stroke, aneurysm or cardiovascular disease.
  • Secondary assessment: a comprehensive neuro exam: LOC; the NIH Stroke Scale (cognition and motor abilities; know what it is, no specific scoring); cranial nerves; sensation; proprioception (finger to nose, heel to shin: awareness of position and movement); cerebellar function (motor control); deep tendon reflexes.

Discharge is to home, long-term care or rehab (targeting physical, mental and social well-being: preventing further loss, balance training, coping strategies), plus stroke support groups.

Neurointerventional Radiology (NIR)

Percutaneous procedures done through the blood vessels to fix problems in the brain or in the circulation feeding the brain and spinal cord ("glorified plumbers"). NIR treats: stroke (thrombectomy), carotid stenosis (stenting), aneurysms, AV malformations and fistulas, venous problems (venous stents), and chronic subdural hematomas (MMA embolization), and performs diagnostic angiograms (contrast and pictures of the brain's vessels).

Stroke Numbers and Scales

Why speed matters

  • Over 800,000 strokes a year in the US, close to 6 million survivors; a leading cause of disability in adults.
  • A large vessel occlusion (LVO) is stroke's equivalent of a STEMI: the patient loses almost 2 million neurons per minute, and the chance of a good outcome drops quickly.

The "stroke alphabet soup"

  • NIH Stroke Scale: the most important; a regimented neuro assessment scored from 0 (no stroke) up into the 20s to 40s (severe).
  • Modified Rankin Scale (mRS): outcome at 90 days. 0 to 2 is an OK outcome; at 3 and above the main thing is they can't walk.
  • TICI score: how well the blocked vessel was reopened.
  • Cincinnati (prehospital) Stroke Scale: a quick EMS field screen, not used in the hospital.

tPA and Mechanical Thrombectomy

  • tPA (tissue plasminogen activator, a clot-busting drug) arrived in the 1990s: a 55% relative increase in good outcomes at 3 months. It works well for small vessels, but not so much for the large vessels at the base of the brain.
  • Number needed to treat (NNT): how many patients must be treated for one to get the full benefit; the lower the NNT, the more effective the treatment.
  • Thrombectomy for large vessels: like snaking a drain, catheters are guided into the brain and the clot is removed with suction or a stent retriever (the stent expands into the clot, and the stent and clot are pulled out together).
  • In 2015, three trials were halted early because thrombectomy was so clearly beneficial that withholding it was no longer ethical. Patients presenting up to 24 hours after onset can still benefit.
  • Myths dispelled: a patient who got full-dose tPA can still have a thrombectomy; intubating a stroke patient is fine (you can examine an intubated patient, and not oxygenating makes the stroke worse); not every stroke needs a comprehensive center.
  • An AI-powered app sends CT scans from referring hospitals straight to the team's phones, cutting image review from about an hour to 12 minutes.

Primary stroke centerTakes patients in; discharge care coordination; required imaging, tPA, an operating room; leadership and performance improvement teams. Most patients can be treated well here.
Thrombectomy-capable ("primary plus")Primary stroke centers that do thrombectomy: few and far between.
Comprehensive stroke centerThe highest, most complex level (only four in Kentucky, including UK): every type of vascular imaging, a dedicated neuro ICU, stroke research, neurointerventional cases 24/7/365 with backup for simultaneous thrombectomies, and cerebrovascular neurosurgeons. Patients are returned to community providers after the acute period.

Other NIR Procedures

ProblemKey Points
Carotid stenosisPlaque builds up where the carotid bifurcates. Treated with a carotid endarterectomy (in the OR) or a carotid stent. Inflating the balloon stimulates nerve endings that regulate heart rate and BP and can make the heart stop, so the patient is premedicated with glycopyrrolate.
Subarachnoid hemorrhageMost often from trauma; when non-traumatic, most often an aneurysm. 30% die, and half the survivors have deficits. Classic cue: a "thunderclap," worst headache of my life, with nausea, vomiting, neck pain and rigidity, lethargy.
Brain aneurysmAn outpouching of a weak vessel wall, usually at turns or branch points; about 5% of adults have one. Care: ABCs first, then CT; possibly a drain or ventriculostomy to divert CSF and relieve pressure; an angiogram; then surgical clipping (craniotomy) or, more commonly now, endovascular occlusion (coils, a WEB device, a pipeline stent), often through a needle stick in the wrist.
AVMs and AV fistulasTangles of vessels (AVMs are usually from birth; fistulas can follow trauma) that can bleed; shut down with a micro-catheter and a liquid glue (Onyx).
Chronic subdural hematomaCommon in older patients. Draining works but they can come back; MMA embolization closes the middle meningeal artery (which feeds the dura, not the brain) so the collection can go away.

The NIR Nurse's Role

The neuro exam is key. Unlike most post-op patients, stroke and NIR patients can have major exam changes, and it is often the nurses who catch problems first through routine use of the NIH Stroke Scale. Also monitor the access site (often the femoral artery).
  • Stent patients need aspirin and Plavix so the stent doesn't clot off.
  • Many patients (especially in Kentucky) don't respond normally to Plavix. A VerifyNow test checks the response: a high value means not responding (switch to Brilinta and load), and a very low value means a hyper-responder (very sensitive; may need a different drug or rescheduling).

Self-Test Flashcards

Mixed across all four Week 6 topics, built around the look-alikes that are easy to mix up. Click a card to flip it.

Parkinson's tremor vs MS tremor?
Parkinson's: a rest tremor that improves with purposeful activity. MS: an intention tremor, worse with purposeful movement.
Which two Week 6 disorders have remissions and exacerbations?
Myasthenia gravis and multiple sclerosis.
ALS weakness vs Guillain-Barré weakness?
ALS: asymmetric limb weakness. GBS: fairly symmetric, ascending weakness with absent or depressed DTRs.
Which two disorders are treated with plasmapheresis and IVIG?
Myasthenia gravis and Guillain-Barré syndrome.
Which test helps diagnose MS, and which helps diagnose MG?
MS: evoked potentials (also MRI, LP). MG: Tensilon or ice pack test, repetitive nerve stimulation, single fiber EMG.
Which disorders in Week 6 carry respiratory failure as the big danger?
Myasthenia gravis (crisis), ALS and Guillain-Barré (15 to 30% ventilated).
Which Week 6 disorders cause dysarthria (garbled speech)?
Parkinson's, myasthenia gravis, ALS and stroke.
Dysphagia appears in which Week 6 disorders?
Parkinson's, MG, ALS, GBS and stroke.
What is the time-sensitive treatment for ischemic stroke, and for GBS?
Stroke: tPA within 3 to 4.5 hours. GBS: plasmapheresis or IVIG within 4 weeks.
What does an EEG diagnose, and what must be held for 8 hours?
Seizures; stimulants are held for 8 hours.
Which Week 6 disorder keeps cognition intact while the body wastes away?
ALS.
Which disorder's drug therapy is NOT tested in 405?
Multiple sclerosis.
