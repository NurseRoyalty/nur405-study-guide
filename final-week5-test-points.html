Fractures, Devices & Amputations

Know for the exam

  • ★ The 5 P's: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis. Pain is the first sign: increasing pain despite pain medication.
  • Neurovascular checks distal to a cast every hour for 24 hours; a finger or two fits inside; ice 24 to 36 hours.
  • Airway trumps a healing mandible: a wired jaw goes home with pliers.
  • Emergency care: ABCs and a head injury check, then splint in alignment, immobilize, elevate, cold.
  • Skin traction holds only 5 to 10 lb; skeletal traction holds more but risks osteomyelitis. Weights hang free; never interrupt the line of pull.
  • Pin care: serous drainage OK, purulent means infection; half-strength peroxide; no ointments.
  • ★ Be vigilant for compartment syndrome (a limb can be lost in one shift): severe pain not fixed with opioids; fasciotomy.
  • ★ Know whether the patient is weight bearing or non-weight bearing, and give around-the-clock pain dosing (talk about pain control first).
  • Fat embolism after long bone fractures: neuro changes come 6 to 12 hours before respiratory ones; petechiae; supportive care.
  • Amputation: check under the limb for bleeding (rising HR); phantom pain is real; prone 30 minutes 4 times a day for an AKA; compression bandage at all times except PT and bathing, figure-eight.

Joint Surgery, Osteomyelitis, Discs & Osteoporosis

Know for the exam

  • Myelogram: lie flat (spinal headache) and force fluids. DEXA measures density; a bone scan uses a radioisotope (increased uptake with osteomyelitis; decreased with avascular necrosis).
  • Who gets a DEXA: all women over 65, younger women with risk factors, long-term steroids, drug response, men with risk factors.
  • VTE is the number one complication of joint replacement; only the surgeon removes the original dressing.
  • ★ "You do need to know which type of procedure your patient has had": posterior hip, no internal rotation, adduction or flexion past 90°; anterior hip, no hyperextension only.
  • Weight-bearing levels: non, toe-touch, partial (30 to 50%), as tolerated, full. Hip ORIF: limited weight bearing 6 to 12 weeks.
  • Osteomyelitis (Staph aureus): pain worse with activity, not relieved by rest; x-ray normal until about 10 days; vigorous IV antibiotics.
  • Back surgery: lie flat, log roll with three people; headache with clear light-yellow drainage is a CSF leak; no twisting.
  • Osteoporosis: silent; wrist, hip, vertebrae; kyphosis and height loss. Calcium chewable with food plus vitamin D. Alendronate: water, empty stomach, upright 30 minutes.

Not Tested

  • DEXA T-score numbers.

OA, RA, Gout & SLE

Know for the exam

  • ★ The OA vs RA comparison "would be a great select all that apply question" (which cues are specific to OA?).
  • OA: local, asymmetric, morning stiffness under 30 minutes, Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes, no biomarkers, ESR normal; no systemic steroids.
  • Acetaminophen first for OA: 3 g a day limit (4 g only short term and supervised). NSAIDs: kidneys, bleeding, ulcers. Joint injections no more than 3 or 4 a year.
  • RA: autoimmune, symmetric, systemic (fatigue, weight loss), prolonged morning stiffness; ulnar deviation, swan neck; RF 80%, ACPA more specific and early.
  • ★ ACPA (anti-CCP) is "a very important test for diagnosing rheumatoid arthritis."
  • RA drugs: DMARDs early (methotrexate 4 to 6 weeks); NSAIDs as a bridge; steroids short term at the lowest dose.
  • ★ Gout: colchicine for the acute attack; allopurinol (know the generic name) prevents recurrent flares over 2 to 6 weeks. Sheets off the toes, fluids.
  • SLE: "look good, feel bad"; anti-DNA is the most specific test; complement low; hydroxychloroquine is the cornerstone.
  • Lupus nephritis: monitor BUN, creatinine and urine output. Teach: avoid sun and infection, daily temperature, never stop drugs abruptly, pregnancy counseling.

Not Tested

  • The specific drugs that cause secondary gout.
