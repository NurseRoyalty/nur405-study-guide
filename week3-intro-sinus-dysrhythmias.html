Conduction Review

The chambers

  • Atria on top, ventricles on the bottom. Right side pumps to the lungs; left side pumps to the body.
  • The left ventricle is the powerhouse: it's where cardiac output (stroke volume) happens.
  • We can live with quivering atria (atrial fibrillation). We cannot live with quivering ventricles (V-fib).
  • Electrical conduction drives the mechanical pumping; without it, the heart does not pump.

The pathway

  • SA (sinoatrial) node: the initiator, in the right atrium. Intrinsic rate 60 to 100 per minute. Below 60 = bradycardia; above 100 = tachycardia.
  • It fires, a tiny delay, then the atria contract.
  • The impulse reaches the AV (atrioventricular) node, the gateway to the ventricles; a tiny pause.
  • Then the bundle of His and Purkinje fibers, and the ventricles contract.

Dysrhythmias are abnormal cardiac rhythms (the name changed from "arrhythmia," which implies no rhythm at all). They require a prompt assessment, and the nurse decides whether to do something about it.

The EKG Waveform

An EKG (ECG, electrocardiogram) is the graphic tracing of the heart's electrical impulses; the waveforms represent charged ions moving across the myocardial cell membranes. "Very fundamental and necessary to know" for any nurse caring for a patient on telemetry.

P waveAtrial contraction (depolarization): the SA node fired and the atria "kick."A little bump, since the atrial kick is small.
PR interval (PRI)Starts at the beginning of the P; covers the pause while the impulse travels through the AV node.0.12 to 0.20 seconds ("do you need to remember that? Yes"). Longer than 0.20 implies a conduction delay between the atria and the ventricles.

Depolarization = contraction. Repolarization = relaxation. Atrial relaxation happens but is buried in the QRS, so you can't see it on the strip.

Reading the Strip: Boxes and Six Seconds

The boxes

  • Each tiny box = 0.04 seconds (you need to know this).
  • Five tiny boxes (one big box) = 0.20 seconds.
  • Used to measure the PR interval and QRS width.

Heart rate from a strip

  • Every test strip is a 6-second strip; the marks at the top are 3 seconds apart, so two of them make 6 seconds.
  • Count the QRSs and multiply by 10: 9 QRSs = a heart rate of 90.

Telemetry

12-lead EKG vs telemetry (diagnose vs monitor, contiguous leads) covered again here — review in CV Labs & Diagnostics if needed

Electrode placement (5-lead)

  • White on the right: right below the right clavicle.
  • Black ("smoke"): just below the clavicle on the MCL.
  • Red ("fire"): smoke over fire.
  • Green ("grass"): lower chest, just to the right of the belly button. Snow (white) over grass.
  • Brown ("chocolate close to my heart"): 4th ICS, right sternal border.
  • Telemetry is 3-lead or 5-lead. Students can put electrodes on and replace ones that come off.

Leads and monitoring

  • Changing the lead at the monitor (lead I, II, III) just changes the "camera angle"; it does nothing to the patient. Lead I reads left to right; lead II (right shoulder to groin) is the usual choice.
  • A strip that looks strange may just be in lead III: change the lead to get a better look.
  • Central monitoring (as at UK) and alarms are not fail-proof.
Something changes on the monitor? Go check on your patient. It might be a lead that fell off, but something else could be going on. Artifact (brushing teeth, chest physiotherapy, moving a lot in bed, a low battery) looks scary but isn't the patient's heart: check the patient, the connections and the electrodes, and replace them if needed.

Classifying Dysrhythmias

Two ways: by early, slow or fast (premature complexes, bradydysrhythmias below 60, tachydysrhythmias above 100), or by the site of origin in the heart (sinus, atrial, ventricular).

Bradydysrhythmia (HR below 60)

  • ☺ Reduces myocardial oxygen demand (why beta blockers slow the rate on purpose).
  • ☺ Longer diastole = more time to perfuse the coronary arteries (they perfuse during diastole).
  • ☹ If too slow, coronary perfusion falls (angina).
  • May be tolerated if the BP is OK (an athlete with a resting HR of 54). If BP isn't adequate: myocardial ischemia or infarct, dysrhythmias, hypotension, heart failure.

Tachydysrhythmia (HR above 100)

  • ☹ Shortens diastole, so less coronary perfusion time (angina).
  • ☺ At first may raise cardiac output and BP (compensation).
  • ☹ If sustained: less ventricular filling, so stroke volume, cardiac output and BP fall. About 104 is usually tolerable; at 120 to 130 this starts happening.
  • ☹ More cardiac workload = more myocardial oxygen demand. Can lead to MI, unstable angina, dysrhythmias, low BP, heart failure.

Key Features of Sustained Tachy- and Bradydysrhythmias

Sinus Rhythms

All four start in the SA node. Normal sinus rhythm and sinus arrhythmia are not dysrhythmias at all; nurses often just say the patient is "in sinus."

RhythmRateR to RKey Points
Normal sinus rhythm60 to 100RegularP before every QRS; every P, QRS and T looks the same. March out the R to R to confirm it's regular.
Sinus arrhythmiaNormal (the example strip: 8 QRSs = 80)Not quite regularFrom changes in intrathoracic pressure with breathing (inspiration and expiration). Normal physiology, seen in young healthy people. Skinny QRSs, a P before every QRS. Not treated: just an assessment finding.

Sinus tachycardiaAbove 100 (example: 12 QRSs = 120)RegularUsually a compensation for something else.

Sinus Bradycardia

Causes

  • Vagal (parasympathetic) stimulation: carotid sinus massage (a provider-only procedure), vomiting or gagging, the Valsalva maneuver (bearing down), applying eyeball pressure.
  • Parasympathomimetic drugs (given on purpose to slow the rate).
  • Digoxin toxicity (narrow therapeutic index; can be life threatening).
  • Hyperkalemia (slows depolarization).
  • Myocardial infarction.

What the nurse does

  • HR of 52? Go check on the patient and take a full set of vital signs (BP is the indirect measure of cardiac output).
  • Asymptomatic ("I'm feeling fine") with OK vitals: monitor, don't treat.
  • Symptomatic (lightheaded, nauseous, "I feel like I'm going to pass out," low BP): give atropine.
Symptomatic bradycardia: atropine 1 mg IV every 3 to 5 minutes, 3 mg max (a vagolytic: it blocks the vagus nerve). Still symptomatic? Transcutaneous pacing right here, right now: pads on, push the button, and you essentially create the SA node through the skin. Persistent bradycardia that doesn't respond may eventually need a permanent pacemaker (under the skin below the clavicle; fires when the SA node isn't cooperating, every beat or only some).

Sinus Tachycardia

Causes

  • Exertion (running up the stairs), anxiety, pain, stress, fever.
  • Anemia (not enough "buses," so the heart speeds up to circulate the ones you have); hypoxia.
  • Compensation for decreased BP: cardiac output = heart rate x stroke volume, so low stroke volume raises the HR (for example MI, heart failure).

Treatment: treat the cause

  • Not one size fits all, unlike atropine for bradycardia.
  • Low fluid volume: replace fluid. Pain: analgesic. Fever: antipyretic. Panic attack: anxiolytic (a benzodiazepine).
  • Beta blockers reduce HR and myocardial oxygen demand, but try to treat the cause first.
  • Symptoms if not tolerated: the key-features table (palpitations are the tachy-specific one).
