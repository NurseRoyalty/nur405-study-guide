Part 1 — Endocrine Labs & Dx: The Endocrine Review

From Endocrine Labs & Dx: The Endocrine Review. The Negative Feedback System

The endocrine system works on a negative feedback system that creates homeostasis, like the thermostat in your living room: set it at 72, the temperature drops below 72, the heater kicks on until it reaches 72, then shuts off. "An important concept to understand with this content": every hormone works this way.

StepThe HPA Axis (Hypothalamus, Pituitary, Adrenal Cortex)
1. StressorA test, a move, a wedding, illness, major surgery.
2. HypothalamusReleases CRF (corticotropin releasing factor). Target: the anterior pituitary.
3. Anterior pituitarySecretes ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone). Target: the adrenal cortex.
4. Adrenal cortexReleases cortisol into the blood (measurable with a lab draw).
5. EffectCortisol increases blood sugar for fight or flight: why stress drives blood sugar up.

ADH: The Regulator of Pure Water

  • ADH (antidiuretic hormone, also called vasopressin) comes from the posterior pituitary. It is "the regulator of pure water supply" ("write that down").
  • The loop: serum osmolality rises (concentrated blood), ADH is released, the kidney tubules reabsorb water, plasma volume rises and osmolality falls, and the normal osmolality then inhibits ADH.
ADH moves pure water, not potassium. Retaining water from ADH does not retain potassium; losing water from too little ADH (diabetes insipidus) does not make the patient hypokalemic. It's not like diuresing with Lasix. "That's going to trip you up if you don't stop and understand this."

Thyroid and Parathyroid

Thyroid

  • Regulated by TSH from the anterior pituitary.
  • Secretes T3 and T4: T4 is the most abundant; T3 is the most potent.
  • Also calcitonin (not discussed much in this course).

Parathyroids

  • A different gland, buried inside the thyroid; regulate calcium and phosphorus.
  • If they're inadvertently removed during a thyroidectomy, the patient can become hypocalcemic (Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs).

Adrenal Gland and Pancreas

Adrenal gland

  • Two glands in one: the cortex covers (outside) and the medulla is in the middle.
  • They do totally different things (see Adrenal Disorders).

Pancreas

  • The islets of Langerhans secrete insulin.
  • In diabetes mellitus, the origin of the problem is the pancreas.

Part 2 — ADH Disorders: SIADH & Diabetes Insipidus

From ADH Disorders: SIADH & Diabetes Insipidus. Built from the Week 4 ADH disorders voiceover. Too much ADH is SIADH; not enough is diabetes insipidus (DI). "If you understand what ADH does, all you have to do is think about what happens when I have too much and what happens when I don't have enough." Don't confuse diabetes insipidus with diabetes mellitus: two totally different things.

Sodium, Osmolality and ADH

  • Sodium makes up most of the serum osmolality: hypernatremic = hyperosmolar; hyponatremic = hypo-osmolar. Cells shrink or swell, and a sodium that's far enough off means a confused patient.
  • High osmolality or decreased circulating volume triggers two things: thirst (you drink more) and ADH secretion (you hold on to water). Circulating volume rises, which turns off ADH and thirst.
  • ADH, along with the thirst mechanism, is the major regulator of the body's water. Potassium is not impacted by ADH.

SIADH vs Diabetes Insipidus at a Glance

"It's really important that you understand these arrows with the endocrine stuff": half-understanding leads to choosing the up arrow when it should be down.

SIADH

Cause

  • Most common: a type of lung cancer that secretes ectopic hormones (a paraneoplastic disorder: the tumor secretes "random things," including ADH).
  • Sometimes SIADH is how the lung cancer is found.

Nursing care

  • Watch for cues of fluid volume overload; monitor mental status changes.
  • Restrict fluids (re-concentrates the serum); daily weights; I&O.
  • IV fluids per order, monitored very closely so they aren't over-administered.
  • Safety (confused patients); lots of patient and family support.

SIADH Collaborative Care

Immediate goal: restore normal fluid volume and normal osmolality.

SeverityTreatment
Mild symptoms, mild hyponatremiaFluid restriction, for example about 1 L a day.
Severe symptoms or severe hyponatremiaHypertonic saline (usually 3%; 5% is even more hypertonic), given slowly: too fast causes rapid fluid shifts in the brain that can be fatal. A stronger fluid restriction, such as 500 mL a day.
Chronic SIADH onlyDrug therapy (not first line): diuretics, or demeclocycline, which blocks the effects of ADH.

Diabetes Insipidus

Neurogenic (central) DI

  • The pituitary is damaged, for example by head trauma (a closed head injury from a car accident).
  • Abrupt onset; usually self-limiting (resolves on its own), but the symptoms must be treated while it lasts.

Nephrogenic DI

  • The pituitary is OK, but the kidneys don't respond to ADH.
  • Most common cause: lithium.
  • Less abrupt onset and less severe symptoms. (A third type, psychogenic, isn't covered.)

DI Nursing Care and Treatment

  • Maintain fluid and electrolyte status: IV fluids, I&O, daily weights, labs.
  • Notify the provider of an increase in urine volume with a decrease in specific gravity: the disease is getting worse, and the drug dose may need to go up.
  • Desmopressin (DDAVP): an exogenous, synthetic form of vasopressin (ADH), in very small doses: nasal spray, PO, IV or subQ (usually IV in the hospital).
  • Hormone therapy and fluid restriction are the cornerstones of treatment for neurogenic DI.
Labs worth memorizing: urine specific gravity 1.010 to 1.030; serum osmolality 270 to 290; serum sodium 135 to 145.

Part 3 — Adrenal Disorders: Cushing's, Addison's & Pheochromocytoma

From Adrenal Disorders: Cushing's, Addison's & Pheochromocytoma. Built from the Week 4 adrenal disorders voiceover. Too much adrenal cortex hormone is Cushing's; not enough is Addison's disease; too much from the adrenal medulla (often a tumor) is a pheochromocytoma. Adrenal (Addisonian) crisis is covered in pathopharm with the endocrine emergencies.

Adrenal Hormones

Adrenal medulla (in the middle)

  • Secretes the catecholamines: epinephrine and norepinephrine.

Adrenal cortex (covers)

  • Glucocorticoids: the primary one is cortisol, which responds to stress by raising blood sugar and making energy available.
  • Mineralocorticoids: the primary one is aldosterone, the regulator of sodium. More aldosterone = more sodium = more water retention.
  • Sex steroids (androgens; not focused on here).
Aldosterone moves potassium; ADH doesn't. When aldosterone rises, sodium rises and potassium falls. ADH retains or loses pure water with no effect on potassium. ("Commit that to memory.")

Cushing's Syndrome

  • Cushing's disease (usually a tumor) is very rare. Most of the time it's Cushing's syndrome, a collection of signs and symptoms caused by long-term oral systemic steroids.
  • Steroids are wonderful anti-inflammatories and immunosuppressants, and sometimes nothing else works, but these are the side effects. (Why NSAIDs are loved: they don't cause Cushing's.)
  • Why each sign happens: excess glucose availability (hyperglycemia); fragile capillaries (bruising); protein breakdown and fat redistribution (truncal obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, skinny extremities, osteoporosis); suppressed immune and inflammatory response (poor healing, infection); CNS excitability (moods, insomnia); high aldosterone (sodium and fluid retention, hypertension, hypokalemia).

Priority problems

  • Risk for infection (immunosuppression).
  • Fluid balance; skin integrity (thin skin; any break is an infection risk).
  • Risk for injury (thin bones).
  • Ineffective coping (moods); body image.
  • Unstable blood glucose; potential hypokalemia.

Nursing and collaborative care

  • Rebalance the hormones. Disease (a tumor): adrenalectomy, or removing a tumor secreting ectopic ACTH (another paraneoplastic disorder). Drugs for the disease: not tested.
  • Syndrome (steroids for RA or COPD, for example): gradually decrease the drug, reduce the dose, or try every-other-day dosing, all provider supervised.
  • Monitor fluid balance, glucose, BP, infection risk and mood.
  • Diet: increase protein and potassium; decrease calories and sodium.

Addison's Disease

  • An insufficiency of the glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids (cortisol and aldosterone, plus androgens). The famous example: John F. Kennedy (tan, bronze appearance; thin frame).
  • Usually a slow degenerative destruction: subtle and insidious. Occasionally rapid onset: severe, abrupt, even life-threatening.
  • Low aldosterone: low sodium, low blood volume, low BP, salt cravings, dehydration, high potassium. Low cortisol: no energy, hypoglycemia, weakness, fatigue. Unsuppressed ACTH from the anterior pituitary causes the hyperpigmentation.

Treatment and Teaching

Hormone replacement (the mainstay)

  • Hydrocortisone (cortisol): two-thirds in the morning, one-third in the late afternoon, matching the diurnal rhythm (more cortisol in the morning).
  • Fludrocortisone (Florinef) for missing aldosterone, taken in the morning.
  • Possibly salt additives for heat and humidity.

Teaching is critical

  • Follow the dosing schedule closely; never stop abruptly. Replacement is forever.
  • The 3 x 3 rule: when stressed (sick, a big exam, a marathon), take 3 times the dose for 3 days, depending on the severity of the stress.
  • Keep an emergency supply (an emergency kit with IM hydrocortisone) and wear a medic alert bracelet.
Addisonian crisis: a major, abrupt adrenal insufficiency (for example a big stressor without the cortisol supplement) that can be fatal untreated. Treatment: IV cortisol replacement, saline and dextrose; oral hydrocortisone once they can take it, until the maintenance dose is reached, plus fludrocortisone if aldosterone is low. Nursing: these patients can be extremely hemodynamically unstable, so trend the vital signs closely, and keep the environment calm and stress-free.

Pheochromocytoma

What it is

  • A tumor of the adrenal medulla that secretes too much epinephrine and norepinephrine.
  • Often missed at first: only 0.1% of high blood pressures are caused by one.
  • Diagnosis is quite easy once suspected: serum catecholamines, possibly a 24-hour urine.

Cues and treatment

  • Intractable hypertension (can't be controlled).
  • The triad: palpitations, headache, sweating (diaphoresis).
  • Treatment: remove the tumor.

Part 4 — Thyroid Disorders: Hyperthyroidism & Hypothyroidism

From Thyroid Disorders: Hyperthyroidism & Hypothyroidism. Built from the Week 4 thyroid disorders voiceover. A happy thyroid is a euthyroid state. The extremes, thyroid storm and myxedema coma, are covered in pathopharm with the endocrine emergencies. "Think metabolism when I think thyroid."

The Feedback Loop and Key Terms

The anterior pituitary secretes TSH, which triggers the thyroid to secrete T3 and T4; circulating T3 and T4 tell the pituitary to shut off TSH. "You better not get confused about whether the arrow goes up or down."

FeatureHyperthyroidismHypothyroidism
T3 and T4↑ Too much circulating↓ Low
TSH↓ The pituitary tries to shut the thyroid off, but it isn't working↑ High: trying to get the "lazy thyroid" to work
Most common causeGraves' disease (autoimmune), three-fourths of the time; also toxic multinodular goiterHashimoto's thyroiditis (autoimmune)
The extremeThyroid storm (acute thyrotoxicosis, thyrotoxic crisis)Myxedema coma

Goiter

  • Simply a large thyroid gland, often visible in the neck, from an overactive or underactive thyroid.
  • Toxic goiter: large from hyperthyroidism. Nontoxic goiter: large with a normal thyroid level.

Iodine

  • Needed to synthesize thyroid hormones; only the thyroid can take it up.
  • Deficiency is a worldwide problem but not so much in the US, because iodized salt is the norm (buy iodized unless you react to iodine).

Thyroid storm (acute thyrotoxicosis) is hyperthyroidism to the extreme: rare, and death is rare when treated early. It results from stressors in someone with pre-existing hyperthyroidism, for example the manipulation of the gland during a thyroidectomy. All the hyperthyroid symptoms become prominent and severe.

Managing Hyperthyroidism

TreatmentKey Points
MethimazoleDecreases thyroid activity, but slowly: 1 to 2 weeks to kick in, 4 to 8 weeks for good results.
Beta blockers (PO)Treat the symptoms in the meantime, until the other therapies take effect.
Iodine therapy (SSKI, Lugol's solution)Used to prepare for a thyroidectomy: decreases the gland's vascularity and the risk of bleeding after surgery.

Deliberately kills off the overactive thyroid (ablation) over 6 to 18 weeks, so lifelong thyroid replacement follows. An unsealed radiation source: excretions are radioactive. The radioactivity is gone in a few days; minimize direct contact for 2 to 3 days, double flush the toilet, keep things separate.
Subtotal thyroidectomyRemoves up to 90% of the gland, for those who don't respond to anything else; removing too much can create hypothyroidism. Endoscopic thyroidectomy also exists.

After a Thyroidectomy

A risky surgery, so close to the airway. Watch for hemorrhage (the thyroid is very vascular) and tracheal compression. Always look behind the neck: blood follows gravity, so a clean front dressing doesn't rule out bleeding. Watch the vital signs.
  • Hoarseness is expected for 3 to 4 days.
  • Semi-Fowler's; avoid flexion or tension on the suture lines.
  • If the parathyroids are removed: hypocalcemia (tingling, muscle twitching, apprehension; Trousseau's carpopedal spasm with a BP cuff, Chvostek's cheek twitch when tapped).

Managing Hypothyroidism

  • Levothyroxine (thyroid replacement); monitor thyroid levels and adjust as needed.
  • Labs: T3 and T4 low, TSH high. Antibodies present = autoimmune (Hashimoto's).
  • Nutrition to promote weight loss if they gained weight from the hypothyroidism.
Myxedema coma is severe hypothyroidism and a medical emergency: mental status changes, possibly comatose. Treated with IV thyroid medication and steroids.

Symptoms

  • The three P's: polydipsia (excessive thirst), polyphagia (excessive hunger), polyuria (excessive urination).
  • Fatigue; recurrent infections (lower immune function); slow wound healing.

Type 2 risk factors

  • Non-modifiable: family history; over 45; African American, Hispanic, American Indian and Pacific Islander (higher risk than Caucasians); a history of gestational diabetes.
  • Modifiable (the target): physical inactivity, overweight or high body fat, high blood pressure, high cholesterol.

Diabetes Labs & Diagnosis

LabWhat the Lecture Says
Fasting blood glucoseNo food or drink for 8 hours. Normal is less than 126 (as stated); over that is a warning sign of diabetes.
Casual (random) blood glucoseNormal is less than 200; over 200 may indicate diabetes.
Urine ketonesPositive or high ketones relate to hyperglycemia; greater than 300 is a medical emergency (a clinic would send them to the ED and let the provider know).
Lipid profileLDLs and triglycerides may be elevated; HDLs lower.
Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)Used more for gestational diabetes, not usually type 1 or 2. Fasting level first, drink the glucose, then levels every 30 minutes for 2 hours. Goals: before, under 110; 1 hour, under 180; 2 hours, under 140.
Hemoglobin A1C (glycosylated hemoglobin)"This is huge." The average glucose over the past 120 days (about 3 months). Used to diagnose and to evaluate whether treatment is working (fasting glucose isn't used for that). Normal 4 to 6%; 6.5% or greater: possibly diabetes; the target for routine care is 7%.

Diagnosis (ADA criteria, mg/dL)

  • At least one: A1C 6.5 or greater; fasting glucose over 126; OGTT 200 or greater; or classic hyperglycemia symptoms with a random glucose over 200 or a hyperglycemic crisis.
  • Not from one lab: a fasting glucose over 126 is repeated within the next week; an A1C over 6.5 gets lifestyle changes and a recheck within 3 months.
  • The mmol/L values don't need memorizing.

Prediabetes

  • Impaired glucose tolerance, impaired fasting glucose, or both. High risk for type 2; usually no symptoms, but damage from insulin resistance may already be happening.
  • Nursing is mostly teaching: lifestyle modification, monitor glucose and A1C, follow up with the primary care provider, avoid sugary foods, count and monitor carbohydrates. Insulin resistance can be reversed.

Medications

Oral medications

  • Used more in type 2; started low and increased gradually based on the A1C and fasting (morning) glucose.
  • In the hospital, oral diabetes meds are often stopped and replaced with insulin while acutely ill (sugar is hard to control). Explain this, and that they go back to oral meds at home.
  • Bring forward pathopharm concepts, such as holding metformin before specific procedures and the drug classes (for example, know that an SGLT-2 inhibitor treats diabetes). No specific side-effect questions.

Insulin: mimic the body

  • The body spikes insulin with each meal. A basal-bolus regimen copies this: a basal (long-acting) insulin once a day, usually at bedtime (glargine), plus rapid or short-acting boluses before each meal.
  • Inhaled insulin: be aware of it; not tested.

Insulin is a high-alert medication. Never give insulin without knowing the current blood glucose ("always, always, always"), and know the normal range. Check the diet order and oral intake (no food for 24 hours changes things; scheduled doses may need adjusting with the provider). Know when the insulin peaks, how long it lasts and which type it is. Know the signs of hypoglycemia. NPO with scheduled insulin: follow hospital policy (for example 50% of the scheduled dose, or holding boluses). Ate, got insulin, then vomited: be ready to treat hypoglycemia.
  • Teaching matters most with insulin. Long-time diabetics often know their bodies: if they say "my sugar's about to drop," listen (recheck in 15 minutes).
  • Newly diagnosed: watch them self-administer. Timing is crucial: when it was given, when it will act, and when to expect a hypoglycemic reaction.

Monitoring and Sick Days

Self-monitoring blood glucose

  • A crucial part of management; frequency depends on the glycemic goal, the type, the regimen, supplies and willingness. A new type 2 on metformin: maybe morning and bedtime. On insulin: multiple times a day.
  • CGM (continuous glucose monitor): reads every 1 to 5 minutes to a phone or watch; more common in type 1; better control but expensive.
  • Insulin pumps: a continuous subQ infusion of rapid or regular insulin (a basal rate, plus self-given boluses). Still check fingersticks at least 4 times a day (before meals and bedtime), unless using a CGM.

Insulin pumps in the hospital

  • Acute care: deactivate the pump and switch to a sliding scale for tighter control.
  • Check the sites for infection (high glucose raises infection risk: "bacteria love to eat sugar").
  • Risk of DKA: a malfunctioning pump may go unnoticed while sugars climb.

Sick-Day Management

Illness is a stress that releases hormones that raise blood sugar, and steroids (oral or IV, common when acutely ill) can raise it dramatically, so home insulin may need adjusting. Sick patients are more prone to DKA (or HHS, the type 2 equivalent).

Teach the patient to

  • Let the provider know they're sick.
  • Check blood sugar more often, even every 2 to 4 hours.
  • Keep taking their medications, even insulin as long as the blood sugar tolerates it (oral meds work long-term, so take them even with a stomach virus).
  • Prevent dehydration; meet carbohydrate needs with food or liquids; rest.

Call the provider if

  • Urine is positive for ketones.
  • Blood sugar is greater than 250.
  • A fever not getting better with Tylenol.
  • Confused, disoriented, or rapid breathing.
  • Ongoing nausea, vomiting and diarrhea; can't tolerate liquids; illness lasting longer than 2 days.

This list is patient teaching for home. A hospitalized patient with a blood sugar over 250 on a test question doesn't automatically mean "call the provider."

Hypoglycemia and Hyperglycemia

Hypoglycemia (below 70)

  • Patients can have symptoms above 70 too, especially if they usually run high.
  • Sweating, blurry vision, irritability, shakiness; they can feel like they're going to die. Untreated: seizures and death.
  • Common after rapid-acting insulin when they then don't eat or go for a procedure.
  • An unresponsive patient: after the airway, check the blood sugar.

Hyperglycemia

  • Causes: illness, infection, self-management problems (running out of insulin, which is expensive), extreme stress, corticosteroids.
  • Weak, very tired, blurry vision, headache, upset stomach. Usually gradual and vague, so patients often don't notice until a crisis.
  • Usually means over 250, even 300; check urine ketones. Treat with insulin, fluids to prevent dehydration, and teaching (you may need more insulin when sick).
  • DKA and HHS (sugars in the 600s and 700s) are emergencies covered later: the electrolyte abnormalities can be fatal.
The rule of 15: confirm with a fingerstick (below 70). If awake and able to swallow, give 15 grams of simple carbohydrate: 4 to 6 oz of orange juice or regular soda, 3 glucose tabs, 1 tablespoon of honey, or 5 to 8 Lifesavers (no candy bars: fat delays absorption). This should raise the sugar about 50 in 15 minutes (54 to about 104). Recheck in 15 minutes; still below 70, repeat; once stable, eat a regular meal. Unresponsive or nearly passed out: IM glucagon or IV dextrose (25 to 50 mL); at home, a glucose gel under the tongue.

Chronic Complications

Caused by long-term hyperglycemia damaging the blood vessels (angiopathy), the usual cause of death in diabetes: about 68% cardiovascular and about 16% stroke.

Macrovascular (large vessels)

  • Coronary, cerebral and peripheral vessels. A much higher cardiovascular risk: women 4 to 6 times, men 2 to 3 times.
  • Prevent: stop smoking, control BP, modify high-fat diets, keep the A1C as low as possible, treat all the conditions (together they make metabolic syndrome, which raises the risk of death).

Microvascular (small vessels)

  • Eyes: frequent eye exams.
  • Kidneys: diabetes is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease in the US; treat hypertension too.
  • Nerves: neuropathy in about 60 to 70%, the major contributor to amputation. Only real treatment: early screening and lower blood sugars.

Neuropathy and Foot Care

  • Loss of protective sensation: a cut or a too-tight shoe goes unnoticed, especially on the bottoms of the feet. Ulcers can become neuro-ischemic (lost blood flow), won't heal without nutrients and white cells, and can lead to losing toes. Smoking and PAD compound the risk.
  • Know diabetic foot care (expect a select-all question): for example no commercial remedies for calluses; a mild foot powder or lanolin is OK; well-fitting shoes.
  • Monofilament test (a bit outdated): measures how much sensation is lost on the bottom of the feet.

Nutrition, Exercise and the Hospitalized Patient

Nutrition

  • A balanced, high-fiber, low-fat, low-cholesterol diet (especially type 2).
  • Don't avoid carbs: about 45 to 65% of daily calories, from whole grains, fruits, legumes and milk; limit simple carbs like pasta and bread (never "never eat pasta").
  • Low saturated and trans fat; polyunsaturated fats (fish, nuts) are best.
  • Fiber (beans, vegetables, oats, whole grains) improves carbohydrate metabolism and lowers cholesterol.
  • Protein about 15 to 20%, from lean meats like chicken and fish (red meat has a higher fat content).
  • Alcohol: limit to 1 a day for women, 2 for men; it can raise sugar and then cause a rebound drop.
  • Alternative sweeteners don't raise blood sugar and are acceptable.
  • UK uses consistent carbohydrate diets; type 1 patients often carb count (dosing insulin per 15 g of carbs).

Exercise and the hospital

  • Exercise is incredibly important, but it can lower blood sugar. Don't exercise below 80 or above 250. Best after a meal; a carbohydrate snack first if it's been over an hour since eating or before high-intensity exercise.
  • Medical alert bracelet; well-fitting shoes.
  • Hospitalized: stress and surgery push sugars much higher than at home (reassure them it's normal). Wound healing is impaired and infection risk is higher: watch the WBC count, fever and abnormal exudate.
  • Skin changes from uncontrolled hyperglycemia (reddish-brown spots on the legs, brown-black thickening in skin folds, red patches around blood vessels): not life-threatening, but preventable, and in someone undiagnosed, a reason to check the A1C.
