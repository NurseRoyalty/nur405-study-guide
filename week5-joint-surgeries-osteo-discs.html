Bone and Spine Diagnostic Tests

TestWhat It IsNursing Responsibilities
MyelogramPathology of the spinal cord; a sensitive test for nerve impingement (sciatic symptoms down the leg). Contrast is injected into the subarachnoid space, then real-time x-rays (fluoroscopy) on a tilt table move the contrast up and down the spinal column. Sedation as needed.Force fluids afterward (contrast, nephrotoxicity). Have the patient lie flat for a period of time to prevent a spinal headache (horrific; should resolve within a day or two). An intractable spinal headache can be treated with a blood patch: blood drawn from the periphery is injected into the spine to fill the space the myelogram left open.
DEXA scan (bone density scan)Dual energy x-ray absorptiometry: measures bone mineral density to assess for osteoporosis. Scans the spine, hips and wrist. Reported as T-scores (compared to a standard deviation; you don't need the numbers). Mild porosity is osteopenia (the early stage, before osteoporosis).Explain that it is noninvasive, painless and doesn't take long.
Bone scan (not the same as a DEXA)A radioisotope injection taken up by the bone. Increased uptake: osteomyelitis, osteoporosis, bone malignancies, certain fractures. Decreased uptake: avascular necrosis (bone died from lack of blood flow).The radioisotope is given about 2 hours before; the procedure is about an hour of lying still. Teach that the radioisotope is not harmful. Force fluids afterward.
EMG (electromyogram)Needles inserted into the muscles evaluate the electrical potential of skeletal muscle contractions; done in an EMG lab by a specialized technician.The patient must cooperate with commands and voluntary movements. It can be quite painful. Avoid stimulants and sedatives beforehand (a good baseline).
Know the difference: a bone density (DEXA) scan measures bone density for osteoporosis; a bone scan uses a radioisotope and shows increased or decreased uptake.

Who Should Get a Bone Density Scan

  • All women over 65.
  • Women under 65 with risk factors in addition to menopause.
  • Anyone on long-term steroids (porous bones, risk of breaks).
  • To measure the response to osteoporosis drug therapy.
  • Men with risk factors too: women are at greater risk, but men get bone density scans.

Joint Surgeries (Arthroplasty)

Why and who

  • An arthroplasty is a reconstruction or replacement of a joint. The hip and knee are the most common.
  • It often comes down to the patient being ready because of pain: the x-ray may not look bad but the patient has had it, or the reverse (bone on bone, doing fine, so they hold off).
  • Goals: relieve pain, improve joint motion, correct deformity or malalignment, remove intra-articular causes of erosion.
  • Candidates: osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, congenital deformities.

Nursing problems

  • Ongoing, predictable pain; impaired physical mobility; risk for injury; impaired skin integrity.
  • Risk for peripheral neurovascular dysfunction: always check perfusion distal to the joint.
  • The number one post-op complication of a joint replacement is venous thromboembolism (DVT/PE), from manipulation of the bone.
  • The original surgical dressing is removed only by the surgeon.

Knee Replacement

  • Priority post-procedure: restoring strength, muscle tone and range of motion.
  • A patient who can't do (or comply with) their own ROM may go on a continuous passive motion (CPM) machine: range and speed are dialed in and it moves the leg continuously (passive ROM).
  • PT exercises to build up the quads in the hospital, then regularly at home.

You need to know which approach your patient had. A hip fracture fixed with an ORIF (not a replacement) has no ROM limits like these; the only limitation is limited weight bearing for 6 to 12 weeks.

Osteomyelitis

A severe infection of the bone, bone marrow and surrounding soft tissue. Circulating blood doesn't reach bone well, so it is hard to treat. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common organism. Delayed identification can lead to chronic pain, infection and drainage, loss of limb function, amputation, or death from sepsis.

Risk factors

  • History of drug use, diabetes, immunosuppression, blood infections.
  • Pressure ulcers or chronic wounds.
  • Malnutrition, alcoholism, liver failure.
  • Entry: indirect (through the bloodstream) or direct (into the wound).

Acute vs chronic

  • Acute (less than a month): local constant bone pain that worsens with activity and isn't relieved by rest, swelling, tenderness, warmth, restricted movement; systemic fever, chills, night sweats, nausea, malaise, restlessness.
  • Chronic: has been there a long time or failed initial antibiotics; continuous, or remissions and flares. Fewer systemic signs, but the local signs remain.

Diagnosis

  • Bone and tissue biopsy to find the causative organism; wound and blood cultures.
  • WBC and ESR elevated.
  • X-rays don't change until about 10 days after the infection: less than a week in, the x-ray may not show it.
  • Bone scan: increased uptake. MRI.

Treatment

  • Vigorous IV antibiotics, likely through a central line (often going home with it).
  • Wound debridement; antibiotic beads implanted near the bone.
  • Mobilization: maybe bed rest at first, then up if it's long term (provider preference).
  • Pain control.

Herniated Disc

The disc is a spongy cushion between the vertebrae that prevents bone on bone, allows movement, and lets the nerves exit the vertebral column. Degenerative, bulging, herniated and thinning discs, plus osteophytes, create bone on bone. The consequence is pressure on the nerve root and radiating neuropathies (lumbar disease pressing on the sciatic nerve: pain down the back of the buttock and leg). The bump at the base of the neck is the spinous process of C7.

Diagnosis: MRI, CT scan, myelogram (shows the stenosis).

Conservative measures (try first)

  • Brace or corset.
  • Local heat and ice, massage, possibly traction.
  • A TENS unit (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulator): a non-drug intervention placed where it hurts, intensity dialed up or down.
  • NSAIDs, short-term narcotics, muscle relaxants.
  • Back and core strengthening exercises.

Surgery if conservative care fails

  • Laminectomy: removes the lamina (posterior arch) to free up space for the nerves and reach the protruding disc.
  • Microsurgical discectomy (MSD): removes part of the disc to decompress the nerve root.
  • Spinal fusion with a bone graft (synthetic or tissue) to keep bone off bone and stabilize; recovery takes longer.

After Back Surgery

  • The patient initially lies flat (sitting up puts pressure on the spinal column).
  • Log roll to keep body alignment (no twisting): three people; the person at the head holds the head stable and calls the count, and the two helpers move at exactly the same time.
  • Pain management.
  • CSF leak: the patient complains of a headache, and drainage is clear, light yellow.
  • Peripheral neurovascular checks; monitor a donor site such as the iliac crest.
  • No sitting or standing for long periods until healed; avoid twisting (teach how to get in and out of the car).

Osteoporosis

Bone is dynamic tissue that is constantly remodeling. Osteoporosis is loss of bone density ("porous" bone), which breaks more easily (pathological fractures). It is a silent disease (you can't feel it), and it hits the wrist, hip and vertebral column: the same three places a DEXA scans.

Who is at risk

  • Women are 8 times more likely than men (lower calcium content and bone mass, smaller frames, menopause, longer life), but men get it too.
  • Family history; low body weight; steroids for 3 months or more; advancing age.
  • Diet low in calcium; lack of load-bearing exercise.
  • Cigarettes and excessive alcohol.

Clinical manifestations

  • Compression fractures: porous vertebrae collapse and crumble on one another.
  • Progressive vertebral deformities: kyphosis and getting shorter with age.
  • Vertebrae review: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic (don't move much; rib attachments), then lumbar, sacrum, coccyx. The lumbar and cervical are the most movable, so they have the most problems.

Prevention and Management

  • The disease is preventable, and prevention is easier than treatment.
  • Calcium supplements: carbonate or citrate; chewable is recommended, taken with food (mega doses are hard to absorb). Vitamin D is always recommended with calcium.
  • Load-bearing exercise (walking, running, weight lifting), stated in the voiceover as 30 minutes three times a day.
  • Avoid tobacco and excessive alcohol; a corset helps prevent vertebral collapse.
Alendronate: take with water on an empty stomach and stay upright for 30 minutes afterward, or it causes esophagitis. Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) are also used.
