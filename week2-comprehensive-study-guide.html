Part 1 — Visual Labs & Dx, Cataracts, Retinopathy, Retinal Detachment, Macular Degeneration & Glaucoma

Built from the five Week 2 eye voiceovers: visual labs and diagnostics, cataracts, retinopathy and retinal detachment, macular degeneration, and glaucoma. Cataracts

The lens becomes cloudy and stiff (often with age), decreasing visual acuity and the ability to accommodate. Vision is consistently fuzzy, "like there's Vaseline on my eyeball," with trouble from glare (light scatter from the opacities), especially driving. Cataract surgery is the most common surgical procedure for Americans older than 65.

Before surgery: watch and adapt

  • Adjust the glasses prescription; read with extra light; do things close up.
  • Limit driving to daytime (night glare).

When surgery is chosen

  • Those measures stop working and safety is affected.
  • The lens is raising the IOP (which can cause blindness and leads to glaucoma).
  • The cataract blocks the view of the retina in someone who needs regular diabetic retinopathy exams.

Cataract Surgery: Pre-op and Post-op

Pre-op

  • Outpatient; no general anesthesia (anti-anxiety meds are given; it goes quickly).
  • Mydriatic, cycloplegic and anti-inflammatory drops; topical antibiotics may be ordered.
  • NPO 6 to 8 hours.
  • Medical history matters: a history of closed-angle glaucoma means no dilating drops.

Post-op and teaching

  • Topical antibiotics and possibly steroids; a shield over the eye until the checkup, usually within 24 hours (sometimes worn at night after that so the patient doesn't scratch).
  • Avoid anything that raises IOP: bending at the waist, stooping, bearing down, lifting, coughing.
  • Vision may not improve right away; it can take time, so teach this so the patient doesn't panic.
  • Drops are tapered and stopped as the eye heals; follow-ups next day, next week, next month (IOP, acuity, corneal clarity).
  • Teach hygiene and eye care, signs of infection, restrictions, and correct drop instillation.

Diabetic Retinopathy

  • A problem of the retina, usually from diabetes (microvascular changes); the two most common causes are diabetes and hypertension.
  • Prevent it by controlling blood sugar and blood pressure.
  • Vision is splotchy haziness (cataracts are constant haziness).
  • Care is about monitoring for progression and preventing it.

Retinal Detachment

Retinal detachment is urgent: time is of the essence. The retina tears away from the back of the eye. Cues: sudden floaters or flashing lights, then a curtain coming over the field of vision. It is painless.

Laser surgeryLaser helps the retina re-adhere.Details not needed, just that they exist.

About 90% of repairs succeed; prognosis depends on the extent, length and area of the detachment. Discharge teaching: hygiene and eye care, signs of infection, activity restrictions, eye drops, pain control.

Macular Degeneration (AMD)

Age-related macular degeneration, from retinal aging: the patient loses central vision; the periphery stays intact. A devastating loss.

Collaborative care: not much can be done, but antioxidants, vitamins and zinc may slow progression, and smoking cessation is strongly encouraged (smoking increases the risk).

Glaucoma

Central vs peripheral: AMD takes the central vision (noticed). Glaucoma takes the peripheral vision (often unnoticed, insidious, painless).

An eye disorder of increased intraocular pressure; a major cause of blindness (second behind cataracts, as stated). Peripheral field testing (the visual fields you practiced in lab) is how it's assessed.

FeatureOpen-Angle (Chronic)Closed-Angle (Acute Angle Closure)
Problem"A clogged kitchen sink": the angle is open and aqueous humor circulates, but it can't drain through the trabecular meshwork (a reabsorption problem).A structural problem: the closed angle keeps aqueous humor from even reaching the meshwork.
PresentationUsually asymptomatic, painless.Painful red eye; about 10% of glaucomas.

First-line treatmentEye drops (some decrease aqueous production; some cause miosis and open the meshwork). Then argon laser trabeculoplasty ("the plumber" opening the clogged sink): outpatient, topical anesthetic.Immediate relief: topical beta blocker, oral carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, and miotic drops to constrict the pupil. Once IOP is stable: laser peripheral iridotomy (holes in the iris) or iridectomy (a piece of iris removed) so aqueous humor can drain.
Acute angle-closure glaucoma care: treat pain and nausea aggressively (pain, nausea and vomiting raise IOP). Avoid activity, keep calm, patch the affected eye, compress to the forehead, quiet private room. Give the miotic drops before darkening the room: a dark room dilates the pupil, which is the opposite of what you want.

The big teaching point (open-angle)

  • Adherence: drops may be needed several times a day, and because the disease is asymptomatic, patients forget or don't see the point.

Comprehensive eye exams (American Academy of Ophthalmology)

  • Age 40 to 60: every 3 to 5 years.
  • Older than 60: every 1 to 2 years.
  • African Americans: periodic exams from 20 to 39 (higher incidence, more aggressive forms).

Part 2 — Prostate Labs & Dx, Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) & Prostate Cancer

Prostate Labs & Diagnostics

The prostate encircles the entire urethra, so an enlarged prostate squeezes the urethra and can cause urinary retention. BPH develops over time with aging and does not predispose to prostate cancer.

TestWhat the Lecture Says
History and physicalThe starting point.
Digital rectal exam (DRE)The prostate sits right next to the rectum; a gloved finger estimates size, symmetry and consistency. In BPH the prostate is symmetrically enlarged, firm and smooth.
Urinalysis, culture and sensitivityLooks for a UTI.
Urinary diagnostics, serum creatinineChecks for hydronephrosis (urine backing up and causing kidney problems) and that kidney function is OK.
PSA (serum prostate-specific antigen)A protein made solely by prostate cells: highly specific to the prostate but not specific to prostate cancer (BPH or an inflamed prostate also change it).
Transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)Through the rectum; helps tell BPH from cancer when both the DRE and PSA are abnormal.
TRUS with biopsyThe only definitive way to diagnose prostate cancer.

The PSA Screening Controversy

Benefits

  • A small survival benefit in randomized trials.
  • When PSA screening first came out (way back in the 90s), its use was associated with a 50% drop in prostate cancer.
  • Early detection of aggressive cancer saves lives.

Harms

  • 48 diagnoses to prevent one death, so 47 men were potentially treated unnecessarily.
  • Only 1 in 3 men with a positive PSA has prostate cancer.
  • Risks of the biopsy and of treatment (surgery, radiation) can outweigh benefits.
  • Overdiagnosis: cancer that never becomes clinically relevant. Many men live and die with prostate cancer, not from it.
Nursing implication: make sure the patient is informed of the risks and benefits of PSA screening before deciding. American Cancer Society: average-risk men begin screening at 50; not recommended over 75 (harms outweigh benefits).

Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

The prostate enlarges and grows inward, narrowing the urethra and disrupting urine flow. Caused by age and androgens; very prevalent ("if a male patient lives long enough, he's going to have BPH").

If the prostate gets large enough, the patient cannot urinate: an emergency.

Irritative symptoms (inflammation, infection)

  • Nocturia, often the first symptom (up 5 or 6 times a night).
  • Frequency, urgency, dysuria, bladder pain, incontinence.

Obstructive symptoms (bladder straining against a narrow urethra)

  • Decreased caliber and force of the stream.
  • Difficulty starting; intermittency (stopping and starting).
  • Dribbling at the end.

Together these are called lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS).

BPH Management

Watch and teach

  • Monitor (labs and diagnostics) unless it gets worse.
  • Avoid decongestants and antihistamines: they tighten the prostate muscles so urine can't easily leave the bladder.
  • Restrict evening fluids (nocturia).
  • Double voiding: void, wait a moment, then try again to empty the rest.

Medications and herbs

  • Finasteride and tamsulosin (different mechanisms), or a combination. Specific drug details are not on this instructor's test (pathopharm).
  • Saw palmetto: no FDA evidence it helps, but it's fine if the provider knows (check for drug-herb interactions).
  • Minimally invasive procedures in Lewis: not on the exam.

TURP and Continuous Bladder Irrigation

Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) is the gold standard surgical treatment and the one standardized tests (HESI, NCLEX) use most: commit it to memory. A resectoscope goes through the urethra and excises prostate tissue ("a little Roto-Rooter"). General or spinal anesthesia; inpatient; the patient returns with a three-way catheter.

Three-Way Catheter PortPurpose
Balloon portInflates a 30 mL balloon (a regular Foley's is about 10 mL).
Drainage portUrine (and returning irrigation) out to the bag.
Irrigation portNormal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) in to irrigate the bladder.

Running CBI

  • Purpose: keep the urethra patent and prevent obstruction from clots or mucus while the area is inflamed or bleeding.
  • The nurse regulates the rate by the urine color (roller clamp, sometimes an IV pump). Goal: continuous flow, light pink, few clots.
  • More clots or darker urine (for example after the first walk): increase the rate. Light pink back in bed: slow it down.
  • Clots are expected the first day or two. A large amount of bright red blood may mean hemorrhage; if color worsens for no apparent reason, notify the provider.

Calculating urine output

  • CBI only irrigates the bladder; it never enters the intravascular space, so there is no risk of fluid volume excess from changing the rate.
  • True urine = total drained minus irrigation infused. Example: 400 mL in the bag with 200 mL of irrigation = 200 mL urine.
  • Women can also have CBI if needed.

Prostate Cancer

Incidence rises rapidly after 50; 80% of men who get it are diagnosed after 65. Among the most common cancers in men, but most grows very slowly and many cases never become clinically relevant (men die of other causes). PSA screening was widely adopted before the evidence supported it.

Screening and risk

  • Informed decision about screening at 50.
  • Higher risk: discuss at 45: a first-degree relative with prostate cancer before 65, and African Americans.
  • Screening = PSA with or without a DRE.

Treatment

  • Early stage: watchful waiting with repeated DREs and trended PSAs.
  • Radical prostatectomy (removal of the prostate). Adverse outcomes: erectile dysfunction (gradually returns over a couple of years) and urinary incontinence (gradually returns over a couple of months, but not always). Teach that dribbling can happen.
  • Radiation, alone or with surgery: internal brachytherapy (seeds) and external teletherapy.
  • Androgen deprivation therapy; chemotherapy if metastatic.

Part 3 — STDs: Sexually Transmitted Infections

From STDs: Sexually Transmitted Infections. Built from the two Week 2 STI voiceovers: chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomoniasis, genital herpes, genital warts (HPV) and syphilis, plus nursing management. The lecture uses STI and STD interchangeably.

STI Basics

Infectious diseases spread through sexual contact (penis, vagina, mouth) or sexual fluids. Typically very treatable and ultimately curable, but complications can be serious (infertility, cancer) and the psychosocial impact is large. The nurse educates, counsels and refers.

How they spread

  • Mucosa and genitals most susceptible; also rectum and mouth.
  • Direct skin-to-skin contact; blood products; mother to baby through the birth canal.
  • Autoinoculation: touching an infected area, then another, spreads it within your own body.
  • Not spread by casual contact.

Key facts

  • Very common in the US; having one STI increases the risk of others (you can have more than one).
  • Incubation period: infectious before symptoms or a positive test, so asymptomatic spread happens.
  • The male condom is the best protection (besides abstinence, which is unrealistic for most, so safer sex is pushed). Hormonal birth control and IUDs do not prevent STIs.
Three reportable STIs in the US: gonorrhea, chlamydia and syphilis (the three bacterial ones). The lab or provider must notify the health department.

Factors raising incidence

  • Early reproductive maturity, longer sexual lifespan, greater sexual freedom.
  • Inconsistent or incorrect barrier use; media emphasis on sex without safer-sex messages.
  • Substance use (poor decision making).

Higher-risk groups

  • Young people; social or economic disadvantage; drug and alcohol use, shared needles, multiple partners, inconsistent barriers.
  • History of STIs, missed vaccinations, repeated HIV prophylaxis.
  • Men who have sex with men, transgender patients (and, per nursing management, women who have sex with women).
  • Health equity barriers: fear and distrust of providers, poor access to quality care.

Chlamydia

Sites and manifestations

  • Spread through vaginal, anal or oral sex; more likely transmitted man to woman; can be reinfected many times.
  • Men: urethra (urethritis). Women: cervix (cervicitis). Both: rectum and oropharynx.
  • Usually no symptoms (so it keeps spreading).
  • Men sometimes: painful urination, urethral discharge; rarely testicular pain or swelling.
  • Women sometimes: mucopurulent discharge, bleeding, dysuria, pain with intercourse.
  • Rectal: anorectal pain, discharge, bleeding, itching, tenesmus (feeling of incomplete defecation), mucus-coated stools, painful BMs.

Complications

  • Infertility, usually in women, from pelvic inflammatory disease (PID).
  • PID damages the fallopian tubes: ectopic pregnancy risk, chronic pelvic pain, infertility.
  • Regular screening for high-risk populations.

Gonorrhea

Manifestations

  • Prior infection does not prevent reinfection.
  • Men: usually symptomatic within a few days: dysuria, purulent urethral discharge, epididymitis (the epididymis is the duct behind the testes that carries sperm; once on a HESI).
  • Women: usually asymptomatic or minor symptoms (often overlooked): more discharge, burning, frequency, bleeding after sex, red swollen cervix with purulent exudate.
  • Rectal: inflammation, redness, painful BMs.
  • Screen annually (with chlamydia): young sexually active females, men who have sex with men, other high-risk groups.

Complications

  • Men get treated (symptomatic), so fewer complications; possible infertility from epididymitis.
  • Women, being asymptomatic, get more complications: inflamed Bartholin's glands (behind the labia: enlarged, red, irritated labia), PID.
  • Pregnancy: passed to the neonate, who can develop conjunctivitis.
  • Diagnosis: sexual history, exam, culture of any discharge.

Trichomoniasis

Manifestations

  • More common in women; in men three-fourths are asymptomatic and it often resolves within 10 days.
  • Ranges from asymptomatic carrier to acute severe inflammation.
  • Men: burning with urination and ejaculation, urethral discharge.
  • Women: painful urination, vaginal itching, painful intercourse, bleeding after sex, yellow-green, foul-smelling discharge, strawberry cervix (red, inflamed).

Complications and screening

  • Untreated inflammation makes another STI (such as HIV) more likely.
  • Not related to PID.
  • Routine screening for high-risk groups, including those with HIV; screening men not necessarily recommended.

Genital Herpes

HSV-1 above the waist, HSV-2 below (most of the time): HSV-1 = oral-labial disease, cold sores, fever blisters; HSV-2 = anogenital. Both types can infect both sites.

How it behaves

  • Usually spread by someone unaware they're infected. HSV-2 is twice as common in women; Hispanic and Black populations more likely infected.
  • Enters through mucous membranes or broken skin; lives permanently in the dorsal root ganglia and reactivates (outbreaks).
  • Most contagious with symptoms, but asymptomatic viral shedding also transmits it.
  • Lesions: clear, grouped vesicles on an erythematous base; can become pustules and coalesce into ulcers.

Episodes

  • Primary episode (previously seronegative): often no recognizable symptoms; maybe regional lymphadenopathy and flu-like symptoms; about 3 weeks.
  • Recurrences: usually within the first year; less severe, heal faster, fewer over time. Triggers: stress, fatigue, illness, immunosuppression.
  • Greatest transmission risk with active lesions.

Complications: neurologic (blindness, encephalitis, meningitis); genital ulcers raise HIV risk; transfer to the baby in pregnancy; big psychosocial impact (incurable), so counseling. Diagnosed by symptoms, sometimes culture to tell HSV-1 from HSV-2.

Genital Warts (HPV)

  • Benign growths on anogenital skin. HPV is usually transient (clears in 1 to 2 years) but can persist without warts; removing warts doesn't clear the virus or decrease spread.
  • Most people don't know they're infected. Warts are discrete, flesh-colored or hyperpigmented, 1 to 10, can coalesce into a larger mass. Men: penis or scrotum. Women: inner thighs, vulva, vagina.
  • Early lesions are hard to see; warts can be confused with other STI lesions (secondary syphilis can look similar), so rule out the others.
  • Few long-term concerns from warts themselves, but high-risk HPV strains are linked to cancer: more screening.
  • HPV vaccine covers 90% of genital warts and 70 to 90% of the strains that cause cervical cancer; now recommended for everyone. Anal warts are harder to treat than vulvar ones.

Spread by direct contact with a syphilitic ulcer, the chancre (external or internal); can be transmitted in pregnancy. Can have serious long-term complications if not treated early.

Nursing Management of STIs

Problems and assessment

  • Lack of knowledge; big psychosocial issues (depression, anxiety, isolation); risk for infection.
  • History taking is so important: be aware of gender identity, current anatomy and sexual practices.
  • Be warm, non-judgmental and reassuring.

Health promotion

  • Promote HPV vaccines, routine testing and prevention.
  • Know your health department's reporting requirements.
