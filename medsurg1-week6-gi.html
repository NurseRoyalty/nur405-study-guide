GERD & Hiatal Hernia

GERD: backward flow of acidic stomach contents into the esophagus through a loose lower esophageal sphincter (LES). Heartburn is the most common symptom; regurgitation; respiratory symptoms (night coughing spells).

Complications and diagnosis

  • Esophagitis; Barrett's esophagus; cough and bronchospasm.
  • Barium swallow or EGD, but often just a high-dose PPI for about two weeks: if symptoms respond, that's the diagnosis and invasive tests aren't needed.

Lifestyle teaching

  • Identify and avoid triggers. Quit smoking: LES strength improves immediately.
  • Avoid fatty foods; small meals, no fluids with meals (drink between); no late-night snacking.
  • Sit up 2 to 3 hours after meals; raise the head of the bed on 4 to 6 inch blocks ("gravity is not reflux's friend").
  • Weight loss if large (eases intra-abdominal pressure); medications.
Refractory GERD ProcedureHow It Works
LINX deviceEndoscopic. A ring of rare-earth magnets around the LES: increases closure but lets food pass.
Nissen fundoplicationSurgical (laparoscopic or open). The fundus is wrapped and sutured into a collar around the LES ("a big bear hug"). Laparoscopic preferred over a big incision when there's a choice.

Hiatal hernia

  • Part of the stomach herniates up through the diaphragm (diaphragmatic or esophageal hernia). Not reflux: a herniated area above the LES.
  • Often structural: increased abdominal pressure (pregnancy, obesity).
  • May be asymptomatic; symptoms and treatment similar to GERD.

Barrett's esophagus

  • Long-term esophagitis that changes cells (metaplasia). Can follow GERD or hiatal hernia; not everyone gets it.
  • Rare; confirmed by biopsy.
  • 30 to 40 fold increased risk of esophageal cancer.

Peptic Ulcer Disease

Erosion of GI mucosa from hydrochloric acid and pepsin. Causes: H. pylori and drugs, number one being NSAIDs. NSAIDs plus H. pylori = "double trouble." Also alcohol, smoking, family history. Stress aggravates an existing ulcer but hasn't been shown to cause one.

Commit to memory: NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) = GI upset and kidney problems. Acetaminophen (not an NSAID) = hepatotoxicity.

Types

  • Gastric: usually the antrum. Duodenal: just past the pyloric sphincter; 80% of ulcers are duodenal.
  • Most ulcers have H. pylori (the percentage was garbled in the transcript as "90 to 90%").
  • Acute: fewer layers, shorter, resolves. Chronic: 4 times more common, more severe, long-term.

Diagnosis

  • EGD.
  • Urea breath test and stool test: non-invasive H. pylori tests. Biopsy is the gold standard (invasive).
  • CBC (H&H for bleeding) and stool for occult blood (may be dark and tarry or invisible).

Complications: HOP

Collaborative care

  • Rest; quit smoking; no special diet (individualized).
  • Stop aspirin and NSAIDs for 4 to 6 weeks; if aspirin must continue, give it with a PPI.
  • H. pylori: antibiotics plus PPIs; maybe sucralfate.
  • Refractory PUD: Billroth I or II reconstruction after vagotomy (cuts the vagus nerve to decrease HCl) and antrectomy. Know it exists, not the differences.

Dumping syndrome

  • After part of the stomach is removed: PUD surgery, gastrectomy, fundoplication, Roux-en-Y.
  • A hyperosmolar (concentrated sugar) bolus dumps into the small intestine; fluid shifts out of the vascular space into the gut: diarrhea, dizziness, sweating, palpitations soon after eating.
  • Later, postprandial hypoglycemia about 2 hours after eating (insulin surge).
  • Self-limiting: a few months up to a year.
Dumping syndrome diet: six small meals, never a big one · no fluids with meals · avoid concentrated simple sugars · protein and fat encouraged · rest after eating.

Upper GI Bleed

FindingWhat It Means
Hematemesis, bright red (frank)Recent or ongoing bleed.
Coffee-ground emesisBlood partly digested: older bleeding or bleeding has stopped. Malodorous.
MelenaBlack, tarry, malodorous stool; up to 8 hours of digestion.
Occult bloodNot visible; positive guaiac. Never expected unless a GI bleed is already diagnosed.

Causes

  • Ulcers: 50% of upper GI bleeds. The two most common causes of bleeding ulcers: H. pylori and NSAIDs. Also aspirin and steroids.
  • Stress-related mucosal disease (SRMD): in 70 to 90% of ICU patients, so ICU patients get a PPI (IV pantoprazole).
  • Esophageal varices (portal hypertension from end-stage liver disease): bleeding can be uncontrollable. A Blakemore tube (balloon pressure on the bleed) is an emergency, ICU-bound measure.

Nursing

  • Vital signs vs baseline; orthostatic hypotension; signs of hypovolemia: rising HR, insatiable thirst, cool clammy skin, restlessness (always investigate).
  • Rigid, board-like abdomen = perforation; distended with guarding = peritonitis.
  • Labs: H&H and BUN (a GI bleed raises BUN).
  • 18-gauge IV (anticipate PRBCs, fluid resuscitation).
  • NG lavage (effectiveness questionable, but on standardized exams).
  • Endoscopy with hemostasis is first line: nothing else helps until the bleed is stopped.

Bariatric Surgery

Currently the only treatment with lasting weight loss in severe obesity. Criteria: BMI 40 or higher, or 35 with one or more severe obesity-related complications (hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, sleep apnea). Extensive psychological, physical and behavioral screening.

ProcedureTypeKey Points
Sleeve gastrectomyRestrictiveGreater curvature removed and stapled: a tubular stomach. Permanent.
Intragastric balloonRestrictiveMinimally invasive, temporary: a saline balloon of about 400 to 700 mL promotes satiety. Usually not left more than 6 months (leak risk).
Roux-en-Y gastric bypassRestrictive and malabsorptiveGold standard and most common. Pouch only 15 mL (a medicine cup is 30). Rest of the stomach disconnected, not removed. Stay 2 to 3 days; 60 to 70% weight loss in 1 to 2 years.

Outcomes

  • Better: glucose tolerance, diabetes, blood pressure, cholesterol and triglycerides, GERD, sleep apnea.
  • Adverse: malabsorption of vitamins and iron, so lifelong vitamin supplementation; dumping syndrome.

Post-op care

  • Control pain to enhance mobility.
  • Vigilant for wound infection, dehiscence and evisceration (higher risk with obesity).
  • NG to wall suction at first; then water and sugar-free liquids in tiny amounts, high-protein liquids, pureed, then solids.
  • Long-term: the DASH diet, in smaller portions.

Both: chronic intestinal inflammation with remissions and flares; cause unknown; autoimmune; no cure (except colectomy for UC). Diarrhea, weight loss, abdominal pain, fever, fatigue. Colon cancer risk increased in both (the lecture said "not as much with UC").

During a flare: rest the bowel. NPO with IV hydration, control inflammation, treat infection, correct malnutrition, reduce stress.

Nutrition

  • Malnutrition: poor intake, high metabolic rate, steroids, malabsorption, protein-losing enteropathy (proteins leak into the GI tract).
  • When eating resumes: high calorie, high protein, low residue (low fiber keeps the gut calm), plus vitamin and iron supplements.
  • No universal triggers: keep a food diary. Quit smoking.

Drugs

  • Sulfasalazine (GI anti-inflammatory) still the drug of choice; aminosalicylates decrease inflammation.
  • Antimicrobials, steroids (great anti-inflammatories, many side effects), immunosuppressants, immunomodulators (TNF).
  • UC: drug therapy induces remission 80% of the time; 1 in 5 eventually need surgery. Crohn's: most need surgery at some point.
  • Specific IBD drugs: not on this instructor's test (they are in pathopharm).

TPN

If the gut works, use it. A comatose patient with a working gut gets tube feedings, not TPN. TPN is for a gut that can't be used (severe flare, severe vomiting, recovering bowel resection).
  • Total parenteral nutrition is IV and nutritionally complete; positive nitrogen balance while the bowel rests. Not ideal long-term: an unused gut atrophies.
  • Very hypertonic (stated as D10 or D20, and later D15, D20, D25): needs a central line and a dedicated line. Lipids (milky) run separately.
  • Blood sugar checks every 6 hours; may need insulin coverage.
  • Start slowly and stop slowly (for example 10, 20, 30, 50 mL/hr up; 50, 40, 30, 20 down) so the pancreas can adapt.
  • PPN (partial) exists but is rarely used.

Bowel Ostomies

Location tells you the output. Ileostomy: right lower quadrant, very liquid (no time for water reabsorption), caustic, oozes almost continuously, always needs a bag. Left lower quadrant (descending or sigmoid) colostomy: almost like a normal BM.
StomaStructureNotes
End stomaBowel cut; the proximal end brought out as one stoma.Distal portion removed (permanent) or sewn closed and left in as a Hartmann's pouch, so it can be reconnected later (a takedown, a provider decision).
Loop stomaA loop brought to the skin, slit on the anterior wall, held by a plastic rod.Proximal opening = stool; distal opening = mucus. Usually temporary.
Double-barrel stomaBowel divided; both ends brought out as two separate stomas (no rod).Proximal = stool; distal = mucous fistula. Usually temporary.

Stoma care

  • Know whether it's temporary or permanent before you go in.
  • Assess color, swelling, bleeding.
  • Mild soap and warm water; skin barrier, then a snug pouch cut to the exact stoma size so no skin touches the drainage.
  • Empty the bag often; release gas; don't make faces. Ostomy nurses are a great resource.

Other points

  • A colostomy near the rectum (solid stool) may be irrigated like an enema through the stoma, every day or two.
  • Old stool and mucus may still pass from the anus.
  • Teaching may go slowly: a patient not yet willing to look at the stoma at discharge isn't uncommon.

Colorectal Cancer

Risks and signs

  • Red or processed meat, overweight, inactivity, alcohol, smoking, few fruits and vegetables, IBD history, genetics.
  • Insidious: often found advanced. Anemia (low H&H), rectal bleeding, abdominal pain, change in bowel habits, obstruction or perforation.
  • Rectal: discomfort, blood in stool. Descending: bowel change, bright red stool. Ascending: subtler (anemia).

Screening

  • Colonoscopy is the gold standard: average risk starts at 45, every 10 years; earlier and more often with strong family history or IBD. Remove precancerous polyps.
  • Fecal occult blood or stool DNA test: less favorable but acceptable, yearly.
Rectal Cancer SurgeryResult
Abdominoperineal resection (APR)Tumor and part of the peritoneum removed; permanent colostomy (descending).
Low anterior resection (LAR)Two steps with a temporary ostomy and Hartmann's pouch, then reconnected: sphincter preserved, normal control of defecation.

Diverticulosis & Diverticulitis

Diverticulosis (the condition)

  • Outpouchings of the intestinal mucosa. Usually asymptomatic; maybe pain, bloating, gas, bowel changes.
  • Prevent: high fiber, low fat, low red meat, activity. With it: keep eating high fiber.
  • No need to avoid nuts and seeds (no evidence) unless they cause a flare.
  • Weight reduction; avoid raised intra-abdominal pressure (constipation).

Diverticulitis (the flare)

  • Left lower quadrant pain, palpable mass, signs of infection (an older adult may only be confused).
  • NPO to rest the gut; if severe, admit for IV fluids and antibiotics.
  • Watch for abscess, bleeding, peritonitis (a ruptured diverticulum spills stool into the peritoneum).
  • Complications may need resection and a temporary ostomy.

Intestinal Obstruction

Mechanical (most obstructions)

  • Mostly small intestine: adhesions, hernias, strictures.
  • Surgical adhesions are the number one cause, days to years post-op.

Non-mechanical: paralytic ileus

  • About 1 in 10. Peristalsis stops, most often after the bowel has been manipulated.
  • NG to low wall suction and wait for peristalsis, or the patient keeps vomiting.

Care: decompress (NG to low wall suction), correct fluids and electrolytes, pain control, remove the obstruction if needed (bowel resection with end-to-end anastomosis: no stoma).
