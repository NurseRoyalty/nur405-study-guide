Types of Fractures

TypeWhat It Is

Where the break is determines the treatment. A pelvic fracture can't be casted (a pelvic wrap until surgery, then an internal device); a clavicle fracture is just immobilized to heal.

A wired-shut jaw (mandibular fracture): airway trumps a healing mandible. If the patient vomits they could aspirate, so they are often sent home with a pair of pliers (wire cutters) just in case. They can use their gut (sucking through a straw).

Emergency Care and the Three Goals

Emergency care

  • Airway, breathing, circulation; assess for a head injury.
  • Splint the fracture as quickly as possible, in normal body alignment, and immobilize it.
  • Elevate the body part if possible (venous return, less swelling).
  • Cold pack: vasoconstriction decreases the (often severe) swelling.

Reduce, immobilize, restore function

  • Reduce: put the bone fragments back in their anatomic position.
  • Closed reduction (preferred): non-surgical, manual realignment without an incision, often in the ED, possibly with local anesthesia.
  • Open reduction: through an incision, often coupled with internal fixation (nails, screws, plates): ORIF.
  • Immobilize afterward so the approximated bones can heal (not a quick process), with external or internal devices.

Peripheral Neurovascular Assessment

For anyone with a bone break (casted or not) and anyone with an internal device, assess distal to the break. "Very vigilant."

The 5 P's: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis. Pain is the first sign: increasing pain despite pain medication (anything trending up is significant). By the time there's no pulse, pallor or paralysis, "you've missed the boat."

External Devices

Casts

What they are

  • Circumferential (all the way around the limb), temporary immobilization; common after a closed reduction. Arm, leg, body (body spica) casts.
  • Fiberglass today, but standardized exams still ask about plaster of Paris: it's heated, handled with wet palms (no finger imprints; it takes a while to set), and the edges are petaled (turned outward so they don't dig into the skin).
  • Until set: don't cover it (ventilation), and reposition every 1 to 2 hours.

After it goes on

  • Neurovascular checks distal to the cast every hour for 24 hours: swelling can increase and the hard cast won't expand.
  • Rule of thumb: a finger or two should fit inside.
  • Ice for the first 24 to 36 hours (vasoconstriction, less swelling).
  • Complications: infection, circulation problems, peripheral nerve damage, complications of immobility, and pressure areas over bony prominences (medial malleolus, olecranon process, tibial tuberosity): listen to complaints of pain and discomfort.

Splints, Immobilizers and Traction

Splints and immobilizers (a Thomas leg splint, braces; some can dial in the allowed flexion) are removable, unlike a cast. Traction applies a pulling force on the fractured extremity, and it is continuous unless the provider orders otherwise.

Buck's traction

  • The simplest form: a straight pull on the affected extremity: running traction (one direction), so the patient constantly slides down to the foot of the bed.
  • Temporary: for muscle spasms, or to immobilize before surgery (buys time for the swelling to go down before an ORIF the next day).

Russell's traction

  • Still skin traction, but it pulls both ways: counter traction.
  • Lets the patient move a little in bed and permits knee flexion; helps relieve muscle spasms and back pain. Also temporary.

Balanced suspension traction (Russell's or skeletal) is preferred: counter-traction pulls in opposite directions, so the patient doesn't slide down; moving the bed or patient doesn't change the pull; more mobility and autonomy. A trapeze bar lets patients lift themselves and pull up in bed.

Caring for a patient in traction: maintain constant traction with no interruption in the weights; the weights always hang free, off the floor; and never let anything interrupt the line of pull.

External Fixators and Pin Care

  • An external fixator ("ex-fix") is used with an open reduction but without suspended skeletal traction. It looks horrific, but the patient is not confined to bed (can get in a wheelchair, though not necessarily bearing weight). Examples: arm, jaw, halo, hand, leg.
  • Ex-fix and skeletal traction both break the skin, so infection is the concern; infection in the bone is a real problem.
  • Assess pin sites: the pin shouldn't wiggle; clear serous drainage is OK; purulent drainage likely means infection.
  • Pin care: typically half-strength peroxide with a sterile swab. No ointments or anything gooey (attracts bacteria). Teach the patient not to touch it.

Internal Devices and Nursing Care After a Fracture

Pins, screws and plates mean the patient had surgery: an open reduction with internal fixation (ORIF). The neurovascular assessment applies again.

Neurovascular dysfunction and pain

  • Elevate the extremity above the heart for the first day or two if not contraindicated; ice compresses.
  • Notify the provider immediately if pain increases and is unrelieved by medication; teach the signs of neurovascular dysfunction.
  • Fracture pain is ongoing and predictable, so give around-the-clock dosing: be proactive, talk about the pain regimen at the start of the shift (many patients don't ask). Non-drug measures supplement, not replace, medication for severe pain.

Infection, skin and mobility

  • Watch pin sites; strict aseptic technique; culture anything suspicious; antibiotics per order; watch temperature and WBC.
  • Petal plaster edges. Nothing inside the cast (no coat hangers to scratch, even when healing itches). Report warmth, worsening pain or odor; keep ventilation; turn if allowed (friction, shearing).
  • Pain control before range of motion; active ROM preferred, passive if they can't, or passive-assistive; PT.
  • Know whether the patient is weight bearing or non-weight bearing (check, double check, triple check the mobility orders); teach assistive devices.

Compartment Syndrome and Fasciotomy

There are about 26 compartments in a leg. Swelling inside a compartment has nowhere to go because the surrounding fascia (the body's internal "cling wrap") won't budge, and it can cause vascular compromise very quickly: miss it on the morning shift and the patient could lose the limb by night.

Compartment syndrome is an emergency. The classic cue is severe pain not fixed with opioids. The procedure that relieves it is a fasciotomy (opening the fascia); once the swelling goes down, it is closed.

Fat Embolism Syndrome

  • Long bone fractures (the femur, the humerus) put patients at risk for clots, including a fat embolism: a fat globule released from the fracture.
  • Prompt surgical stabilization of the fracture reduces its occurrence.
  • Red flags: rising respiratory rate, cyanosis, anxiety, tachycardia, falling O2 sat, chest pain.
  • Neurologic features often precede the pulmonary ones by 6 to 12 hours: LOC changes, confusion, apprehension.
  • A petechial rash (chest, eyes); fluffy shadows on the chest x-ray.
  • Treatment is mainly supportive: oxygen, fluids.

Amputations

Terms and candidates

  • Most often the lower extremity: BKA (below the knee), AKA (above the knee), disarticulation (right at the joint).
  • A residual limb matters for a prosthetic: a BKA keeps the knee joint, a big plus over an AKA.
  • Candidates: arterial insufficiency / peripheral vascular disease (often years of uncontrolled diabetes, older patients); peripheral neuropathy (an unfelt foot sore becomes infected); trauma; thermal injuries; osteomyelitis; tumors; congenital limb disorders.

Types

  • Open (guillotine): the wound bed is left open, for an infected area that must become aseptic before the edges are approximated. Closed is the alternative.
  • Elective (planned, time for anticipatory grieving) vs traumatic (life-saving; the patient may wake up without a limb). Both are very difficult to process.

Who Should Get a Bone Density Scan

  • All women over 65.
  • Women under 65 with risk factors in addition to menopause.
  • Anyone on long-term steroids (porous bones, risk of breaks).
  • To measure the response to osteoporosis drug therapy.
  • Men with risk factors too: women are at greater risk, but men get bone density scans.

Joint Surgeries (Arthroplasty)

Why and who

  • An arthroplasty is a reconstruction or replacement of a joint. The hip and knee are the most common.
  • It often comes down to the patient being ready because of pain: the x-ray may not look bad but the patient has had it, or the reverse (bone on bone, doing fine, so they hold off).
  • Goals: relieve pain, improve joint motion, correct deformity or malalignment, remove intra-articular causes of erosion.
  • Candidates: osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, congenital deformities.

Nursing problems

  • Ongoing, predictable pain; impaired physical mobility; risk for injury; impaired skin integrity.
  • Risk for peripheral neurovascular dysfunction: always check perfusion distal to the joint.
  • The number one post-op complication of a joint replacement is venous thromboembolism (DVT/PE), from manipulation of the bone.
  • The original surgical dressing is removed only by the surgeon.

Knee Replacement

  • Priority post-procedure: restoring strength, muscle tone and range of motion.
  • A patient who can't do (or comply with) their own ROM may go on a continuous passive motion (CPM) machine: range and speed are dialed in and it moves the leg continuously (passive ROM).
  • PT exercises to build up the quads in the hospital, then regularly at home.

Osteomyelitis

A severe infection of the bone, bone marrow and surrounding soft tissue. Circulating blood doesn't reach bone well, so it is hard to treat. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common organism. Delayed identification can lead to chronic pain, infection and drainage, loss of limb function, amputation, or death from sepsis.

Risk factors

  • History of drug use, diabetes, immunosuppression, blood infections.
  • Pressure ulcers or chronic wounds.
  • Malnutrition, alcoholism, liver failure.
  • Entry: indirect (through the bloodstream) or direct (into the wound).

Acute vs chronic

  • Acute (less than a month): local constant bone pain that worsens with activity and isn't relieved by rest, swelling, tenderness, warmth, restricted movement; systemic fever, chills, night sweats, nausea, malaise, restlessness.
  • Chronic: has been there a long time or failed initial antibiotics; continuous, or remissions and flares. Fewer systemic signs, but the local signs remain.

Diagnosis

  • Bone and tissue biopsy to find the causative organism; wound and blood cultures.
  • WBC and ESR elevated.
  • X-rays don't change until about 10 days after the infection: less than a week in, the x-ray may not show it.
  • Bone scan: increased uptake. MRI.

Treatment

  • Vigorous IV antibiotics, likely through a central line (often going home with it).
  • Wound debridement; antibiotic beads implanted near the bone.
  • Mobilization: maybe bed rest at first, then up if it's long term (provider preference).
  • Pain control.

Herniated Disc

The disc is a spongy cushion between the vertebrae that prevents bone on bone, allows movement, and lets the nerves exit the vertebral column. Degenerative, bulging, herniated and thinning discs, plus osteophytes, create bone on bone. The consequence is pressure on the nerve root and radiating neuropathies (lumbar disease pressing on the sciatic nerve: pain down the back of the buttock and leg). The bump at the base of the neck is the spinous process of C7.

Diagnosis: MRI, CT scan, myelogram (shows the stenosis).

Conservative measures (try first)

  • Brace or corset.
  • Local heat and ice, massage, possibly traction.
  • A TENS unit (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulator): a non-drug intervention placed where it hurts, intensity dialed up or down.
  • NSAIDs, short-term narcotics, muscle relaxants.
  • Back and core strengthening exercises.

Surgery if conservative care fails

  • Laminectomy: removes the lamina (posterior arch) to free up space for the nerves and reach the protruding disc.
  • Microsurgical discectomy (MSD): removes part of the disc to decompress the nerve root.
  • Spinal fusion with a bone graft (synthetic or tissue) to keep bone off bone and stabilize; recovery takes longer.

After Back Surgery

  • The patient initially lies flat (sitting up puts pressure on the spinal column).
  • Log roll to keep body alignment (no twisting): three people; the person at the head holds the head stable and calls the count, and the two helpers move at exactly the same time.
  • Pain management.
  • CSF leak: the patient complains of a headache, and drainage is clear, light yellow.
  • Peripheral neurovascular checks; monitor a donor site such as the iliac crest.
  • No sitting or standing for long periods until healed; avoid twisting (teach how to get in and out of the car).

Osteoporosis

Bone is dynamic tissue that is constantly remodeling. Osteoporosis is loss of bone density ("porous" bone), which breaks more easily (pathological fractures). It is a silent disease (you can't feel it), and it hits the wrist, hip and vertebral column: the same three places a DEXA scans.

Who is at risk

  • Women are 8 times more likely than men (lower calcium content and bone mass, smaller frames, menopause, longer life), but men get it too.
  • Family history; low body weight; steroids for 3 months or more; advancing age.
  • Diet low in calcium; lack of load-bearing exercise.
  • Cigarettes and excessive alcohol.

Clinical manifestations

  • Compression fractures: porous vertebrae collapse and crumble on one another.
  • Progressive vertebral deformities: kyphosis and getting shorter with age.
  • Vertebrae review: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic (don't move much; rib attachments), then lumbar, sacrum, coccyx. The lumbar and cervical are the most movable, so they have the most problems.

Prevention and Management

  • The disease is preventable, and prevention is easier than treatment.
  • Calcium supplements: carbonate or citrate; chewable is recommended, taken with food (mega doses are hard to absorb). Vitamin D is always recommended with calcium.
  • Load-bearing exercise (walking, running, weight lifting), stated in the voiceover as 30 minutes three times a day.
  • Avoid tobacco and excessive alcohol; a corset helps prevent vertebral collapse.
Alendronate: take with water on an empty stomach and stay upright for 30 minutes afterward, or it causes esophagitis. Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) are also used.

Rheumatic Disorders: The Big Split

Osteoarthritis is a local joint disorder with only minimal local inflammation. Rheumatoid arthritis and lupus are systemic, with a lot of inflammation. Gout (gouty arthritis) is the fourth.

Osteoarthritis (OA)

Progressive (more symptomatic over time), with a low level of destructive inflammation at the joint, but no systemic manifestations: it's all local. Not a normal part of aging: there are things you can do to minimize and prevent it.

Cues

  • Joint pain (why they seek care) that worsens with use and is initially relieved by rest.
  • Temperature and barometric pressure changes trigger pain ("the rain is coming").
  • Stiffness worsens with inactivity; morning stiffness resolves quickly once they move (unlike RA).
  • Crepitus (snap, crackle, pop); osteophytes; a bowlegged appearance if bad enough; typically asymmetrical (unlike RA).
  • Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes (not inflamed, not tender); the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint; squaring off of the thumb joint.
  • Body parts: the neck and lower back (cervical and lumbar spine are the most mobile) and other joints.

Diagnosis

  • Bone scans, CT, MRI can show early changes.
  • X-rays stage the progression (bone on bone, osteophytes) but don't correlate with the degree of pain.
  • No biomarkers for OA.
  • ESR is normal unless there's synovial inflammation.
  • Synovial fluid is clear yellow with no signs of inflammation (unlike RA's synovitis).

OA Collaborative Care

  • A thorough multidimensional baseline pain assessment.
  • Non-drug interventions are the foundation: orthotics, assistive devices, avoid prolonged immobilization (no long bed rest: a balance of activity and rest), heat or cold, weight reduction, exercise and aerobic exercise, yoga, complementary therapies.
  • Glucosamine and chondroitin (OTC): fine to try, but no hard evidence they work.
  • Nursing problems: acute and chronic pain, impaired mobility, self-care deficit, overweight, depression.
  • Team: rheumatologists, PT, OT, nutritionists; arthritis.org for resources.

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)

Autoimmune, symmetrical, progressive and insidious: a highly inflammatory connective tissue disorder. Early symptoms are systemic: nonspecific fatigue, not wanting to eat, weight loss, generalized stiffness, then pain, worsening stiffness, limited motion, deformities and possibly disability. Morning stiffness is prolonged (OA improves in less than 30 minutes). RA has remissions and exacerbations. Often young patients, which can limit their ADLs and roles.

Extra-articular changes

  • Sjögren's syndrome (about 1 in 10 with RA): diminished lacrimal and salivary secretions: dry, gritty eyes, decreased tearing, dry mouth; very photosensitive. Also seen with other rheumatic disorders (lupus).
  • Rheumatoid nodules (2 or 3 in 10): benign, but a nuisance and a body image issue; on the elbow they risk impaired skin integrity.

Hand deformities

  • Ulnar deviation, knuckle and wrist subluxation (partial dislocation).
  • Swan neck fingers, boutonnière deformity, Z-shaped thumbs.
  • Joints: the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) and metacarpophalangeal (MCP, the knuckles), plus the wrists and feet (never mentioned with OA).

Diagnosing RA

  • Inflammatory arthritis in 3 or more joints; symptoms lasting more than 6 weeks.
  • RF positive in 80% (poorly specific, but providers order it).
  • ACPA (anti-CCP): more specific; may be positive very early.
  • ESR and CRP elevated.

RA Collaborative Care

Non-drug care

  • Rest inflamed joints, but maintain physical fitness: a happy medium.
  • 8 to 10 hours of sleep plus a nap.
  • Regular exercise (pain makes them not want to move, but mobility gets worse): ROM, isometric, isotonic and isokinetic exercise; swimming, cycling, walking for endurance.
  • Hand and finger splints (maintain alignment; may be worn while sleeping); PT and OT.
  • Heat and cold, most effective alternating, limited to 20 minutes or less.
  • Good dietary habits; biofeedback and cognitive behavioral therapy (education reduces depression and disability).

Drugs: time is of the essence

  • Drugs are the cornerstone of active RA treatment: goal is remission and preventing further damage. The earlier, the better.
  • DMARDs (disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs) reduce inflammation, reduce and prevent joint damage, preserve structure.
  • Methotrexate: improves symptoms in 4 to 6 weeks; start as soon as possible (much less effective if you wait).
  • Hydroxychloroquine: fullest benefit takes longer, about 2 to 3 months.
  • NSAIDs: more immediate pain relief but don't reduce long-term damage; must be taken continuously, around the clock, for several weeks for the anti-inflammatory effect; discontinued once the DMARDs are working.
  • Steroids: wonderful anti-inflammatories, but short term, early, the least possible dose for the shortest time (PO, IV or into the joint).

Gout

Terms

  • Gout: the metabolic problem, a disruption in uric acid metabolism, with urate salt deposits in tissues in and around joints.
  • Gouty arthritis: how gout affects the joint: acute, painful inflammation; recurring; often the great toe (not always).
  • Chronic tophaceous gout (less common, advanced): about 10 years after onset; tophi are small white nodules of urate crystals that are painless (body image, loss of hand use).
  • Cause: too much uric acid production or not enough excretion (kidneys), or lots of purine foods (purine is metabolized to uric acid). Primary (most) or secondary (some drugs; don't need to know which).

A flare

  • The joint looks "angry": very intense pain; even touching the bedsheets or wearing socks hurts.
  • Usually the great toe, usually in the early morning.
  • Edema, swelling, tenderness, erythema; possible fever and malaise.
  • Peaks in 1 to 2 days; the flare takes 5 to 10 days to dissipate.

Modifiable Risk Factors and Diet

  • Modifiable: hyperuricemia, obesity, high blood pressure, hyperlipidemia, cardiac disease, diabetes, CKD, dietary factors, alcohol: all teaching opportunities.
  • Weight loss decreases gout (proven better outcomes): a calorie-restricted diet, more protein, complex carbs in place of refined, less saturated fat.
  • Protein from low-fat dairy and plant sources, not red meat and fish.
  • Avoid frequent sugar-sweetened beverages, fatty meals, organ-rich foods, beer and distilled spirits, and anything that causes a flare.
  • A strict low-purine diet only lowers urate 15 to 20%, so the trend is weight loss, medication and the diet above: most gout patients will be on medication anyway.

Diagnosis, Care and Drugs

Diagnosis and nursing care

  • Serum uric acid elevated; a 24-hour urine for uric acid shows whether it's decreased renal excretion or overproduction.
  • Synovial fluid aspiration (needle-like crystals) is the gold standard, but usually not done: 80% are diagnosed without it.
  • Address modifiable risks; pain control; joint immobilization during a flare.
  • Keep the sheets off the toes with a little tent; no socks.
  • Encourage fluids to increase urine volume and prevent uric acid precipitation in the renal tubules (dehydration on a hot day triggered a flare).

Drugs (primary therapy)

  • Colchicine for an acute attack: usually dramatic relief within a day.
  • NSAIDs (ibuprofen) also for acute gout.
  • Allopurinol: prophylactic, for recurrent flares; takes 2 to 6 weeks to decrease flares.

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

The "look good, feel bad" disease. Autoimmune, multisystem and inflammatory; far more in women aged 20 to 40 (a productive phase of life); genetic, hormonal, environmental and immunologic components. Chronic and unpredictable, with remissions and flares. Affects the skin, joints, serous membranes (pleural and pericardial effusions), kidneys, blood and nervous system. African Americans have an increased risk; 50% develop damage in one or more organs. Patients are usually hospitalized for the complications, not the lupus itself.

Diagnosing SLE

  • Based on criteria from history, physical exam and labs: no single test definitively diagnoses SLE.
  • ANA positive in virtually all patients at some point.
  • Anti-DNA antibody: more specific (rarely found in other disorders).
  • ESR and CRP elevated; serum complement decreased.
  • CBC: low RBCs, WBCs and platelets.
  • Urinalysis with lupus nephritis: proteinuria, hematuria, blood cell casts.

SLE Drugs and Nursing Care

Drug therapy (highly individualized)

  • Hydroxychloroquine is the cornerstone, used in nearly all patients.
  • Mild symptoms: NSAIDs, plus short-term low-dose prednisone (kept under 7.5 mg a day), tapered once hydroxychloroquine takes effect.
  • A steroid-sparing immunosuppressant: methotrexate.
  • Severe, life-threatening disease: intensive immunosuppressants and the highest-dose steroids to halt tissue injury.

Collaborative care

  • Build rapport with the patient and family; be sympathetic to the challenges of a chronic, unpredictable disease.
  • During exacerbations: fever, joint inflammation, limited movement, discomfort, fatigue, anxiety, and steroid adverse effects.
  • Space out activities; extra rest and naps (10+ hours at night plus a nap or two).
  • Monitor neuro status (lupus cerebritis; steroids can also cause mental changes).
  • Risk for infection (low WBC) and bleeding (low platelets); impaired skin integrity, body image, role performance.
SLE teaching: avoid triggers such as sunlight; don't stop the drugs abruptly; avoid infections (they cause flares); lupus doesn't go away; pregnancy and sexual counseling before becoming pregnant.
