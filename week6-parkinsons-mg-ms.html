Parkinson's Disease, Myasthenia Gravis & Multiple Sclerosis

Built from the Week 6 Parkinson's disease, myasthenia gravis and multiple sclerosis voiceovers. The tests named here (PET, EMG, repetitive nerve stimulation, single fiber EMG, evoked potentials, lumbar puncture) are explained on Neuro Labs & Dx.

Parkinson's Disease (PD)

Chronic, progressive, insidious (it happens over time without being noticed) and neurodegenerative. The pathology: not enough dopamine, so dopamine and acetylcholine are out of balance.

The Four Cardinal Features

FeatureKey Points
1. TremorA pill-rolling rest tremor: most notable when not doing purposeful activity, and it gets better with purposeful activity (unlike the action tremors of essential tremor or MS). The least debilitating cardinal feature. Present in about half; an internal tremulousness others can't see may come first. Starts on one side and spreads to the other over several years.
2. Muscle rigidityIncreased resistance to passive range of motion: "cogwheel," jerky or stuck when the elbow is flexed and extended. Also starts on one side and spreads.
3. BradykinesiaGeneralized slowness of movement, including the flat affect. Arguably the most major cause of disability, and the hardest for patients to describe ("I feel weak and uncoordinated," "super tired").
4. Postural instabilityComes later (the first three happen early). They lose their balance easily: high fall risk.

Non-motor cues (97% have them; average of 8 each)

  • Cognitive dysfunction and dementia; psychosis and hallucinations; mood disorders.
  • Olfactory dysfunction may come before the motor symptoms.
  • Sleep disturbances; insomnia is one of the most disturbing complaints.
  • Autonomic dysfunction (GI tract, kidneys); sexual dysfunction; pain (face, abdomen, genitals, joints) and sensory disorders.

What a PD patient looks like

  • Flat affect, blank facial expression; tilting forward.
  • Soft, slow, monotonous speech; dysarthria (slurred speech); hypophonia (soft voice).
  • Tremors and a shuffling gait; freezing while walking.
  • Micrographia: progressively shrinking handwriting (smaller when "off," bigger with effective medication).

Diagnosis and Drug Therapy

  • No specific diagnostic test: based on history and clinical features; at least two or three signs of the classic triad.
  • The ultimate confirmation is a trial of anti-Parkinson's drugs: do they respond?
  • No cure: treatment relieves symptoms by correcting the neurotransmitter imbalance (enhance dopamine release or antagonize the cholinergic neurons).
  • Levodopa is the drug of choice and the most effective for symptomatic PD (especially bradykinesia), with many side effects. It only works for a certain time (typically wearing off within about 5 years), so the provider is very particular about when to start it.
  • Also dopamine agonists as monotherapy and/or anticholinergics.

The on-off phenomenon

  • The invariable consequence of long-term levodopa: "on" = mobile, "off" = immobile.
  • At first, wearing off happens at the end of dosing intervals; eventually it becomes unpredictable.
  • Managed by shortening the interdose interval, controlled-release medications, and avoiding high-protein meals.

Deep brain stimulation

  • The most common surgical treatment for advanced PD.
  • Looks like a pacemaker, with leads into the brain; adjustable to control symptoms, and reversible.

Nursing Care for PD

Care and the team

  • Extra time to respond to questions and for ADLs (OT); a listening ear.
  • Give medications promptly and watch for side effects.
  • Prevent complications of immobility: constipation, contractures, skin breakdown (PT).
  • Speech therapy for dysphagia and hypophonia; prevent aspiration.
  • Dietitian: calorie-dense, nutrient-rich foods, more fluids, bite-sized pieces, small frequent meals.
  • Assess for depression and anxiety; psychosocial support; caregiver burden; support groups.

Teaching for freezing while walking

  • Consciously think about stepping over an imaginary line (or a small object, like a rice kernel).
  • Rock side to side to get momentum going.
  • Lift the toes when stepping.
  • Take one step backward, then two forward.
  • Remove rugs and other tripping hazards.

Myasthenia Gravis (MG)

"Grave muscle weakness." Acquired, autoimmune, progressive, characterized by muscle weakness from a reduction of acetylcholine receptors. It has remissions and exacerbations (flares), triggered by anything that stresses the person: pregnancy, illness, heat. Typically peaks within 2 years of onset. Severity ranges from mild to respiratory failure from muscle weakness. Takeaway: grave muscle weakness in the head area.

Presenting symptoms

  • Ocular MG (eyes and face) is the majority; the other type is generalized muscle weakness.
  • 50% present with ocular symptoms: ptosis (a droopy eyelid from muscle weakness, not sleepiness; one or both sides, can switch eyes, fluctuates through the day) and diplopia (blurry, then double vision).
  • 15% have bulbar symptoms (midbrain, pons, medulla): dysarthria (garbled speech), dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), fatigue when chewing (one patient desaturated every time he ate).
  • Less than 5% have proximal weakness alone.

Diagnosis

  • Bedside tests for ptosis: easy and sensitive, but many false positives.
  • Tensilon test: an IV acetylcholinesterase inhibitor with a very quick onset, lasting 5 to 10 minutes; watch whether the ptosis improves.
  • Ice pack test: ice in a surgical glove on the eyelid for 2 minutes; ptosis improves (neuromuscular transmission improves at lower muscle temperatures).
  • Labs for autoantibodies; EMG and nerve conduction tests.

MG Treatment

TreatmentKey Points
Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (first line)Relief in minutes; dosing may vary day to day with the symptoms. The main drug: pyridostigmine bromide ("the only one I would commit to memory").
ImmunotherapySuppresses the immune system: prednisone, Imuran, cyclosporine.
Short-term treatmentsUntil the medication takes effect, after surgery, or after a myasthenic crisis.
IVIGDials down the person's production of their own antibodies, calming the body until the medications take effect.
PlasmapheresisA plasma exchange (looks like hemodialysis) that removes the antibodies from the plasma to decrease symptoms; typically 6 exchanges over 2 weeks, with weekly or monthly follow-up.
ThymectomyThe thymus (in the mediastinum) seems to enhance the antibodies, so it may be removed.

MG Collaborative Care

  • Hospital admission is usually for a respiratory infection or a myasthenic crisis.
  • Focus on neurologic deficits and their impact on ADLs.
  • A balanced diet that can be chewed and swallowed: usually semi-solid because of the dysphagia.
  • Schedule medications to peak at mealtimes (enough jaw muscle activation to eat).
  • Diversional activities that need minimal exertion (they fatigue so easily).
  • Teach what causes flares, the meds, the complications, and support groups.

Myasthenic Crisis vs Cholinergic Crisis

FeatureMyasthenic CrisisCholinergic Crisis
CauseNot enough medication (ran out)Too much anticholinesterase medication
Shared cuesBoth are crises: apprehension, restlessness, dyspnea, dysphagia, weakness, respiratory failure
Unique cues—Flaccid paralysis, hypersecretion, abdominal cramps
Tensilon testThe patient gets betterThe patient gets worse

Knowing the patient's history (too much vs not enough medication) helps tell them apart.

Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

A chronic, inflammatory, autoimmune disorder causing demyelination and axonal injury in the brain and spinal cord. Like MG, it has remissions and exacerbations; it is potentially disabling, with a highly variable pace and many atypical forms. Lhermitte's sign is a specific cue of MS (a student reported it on a HESI).

Cues (plentiful and all over the place)

  • Changes in limb sensation; visual changes, diplopia, nystagmus (eyes moving back and forth).
  • Motor changes, gait and balance problems, limb ataxia; vertigo; facial sensations.
  • Spasticity (spastic, not flaccid, muscles).
  • Intention tremors: worse with purposeful movement, not at rest (the opposite of Parkinson's).
  • Bladder and bowel problems; sexual dysfunction.
  • Cognitive problems: subtle personality changes, memory loss; depression, fatigue.
  • Ringing in the ears and hearing loss; pain (uncommon).
  • Heat intolerance: heat causes flares (no more hot yoga).

Screening and diagnosis

  • Medical history and an in-depth neuro exam (gait, posture, coordination, balance).
  • MRI to reveal MS lesions.
  • Evoked potentials (the visual checkerboard test).
  • Lumbar puncture: the CSF may show increased WBCs and/or protein.
  • Requires a pattern, such as two or more exacerbations, separated by a month, each lasting more than 24 hours, with recovery; or a defined history of flares and remissions; or slow progression over months.
  • Often a story of uncertainty and frustration and multiple doctors before the diagnosis.

MS Plan of Care

  • Drug therapy is not tested in 405: it's highly individualized. The three goals: modify the disease, treat the relapses, manage the symptoms.
  • PT and OT: adapting daily tasks, mobility assistance.
  • Counseling and coping strategies for the patient and family; maintain normal activities, friends, hobbies, support groups.
  • Teach self-care: learn the flare triggers and avoid them; extra rest to reduce fatigue and flares.
  • Regular aerobic exercise (not lying in bed): muscle tone, balance, coordination, depression. Swimming is excellent.
  • Environmental control: avoid very hot weather; they do better in cooler environments.
  • No special diet: just well balanced.
