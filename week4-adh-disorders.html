ADH Disorders: SIADH & Diabetes Insipidus

Built from the Week 4 ADH disorders voiceover. Too much ADH is SIADH; not enough is diabetes insipidus (DI). "If you understand what ADH does, all you have to do is think about what happens when I have too much and what happens when I don't have enough." Don't confuse diabetes insipidus with diabetes mellitus: two totally different things.

Sodium, Osmolality and ADH

  • Sodium makes up most of the serum osmolality: hypernatremic = hyperosmolar; hyponatremic = hypo-osmolar. Cells shrink or swell, and a sodium that's far enough off means a confused patient.
  • High osmolality or decreased circulating volume triggers two things: thirst (you drink more) and ADH secretion (you hold on to water). Circulating volume rises, which turns off ADH and thirst.
  • ADH, along with the thirst mechanism, is the major regulator of the body's water. Potassium is not impacted by ADH.

SIADH

Cause

  • Most common: a type of lung cancer that secretes ectopic hormones (a paraneoplastic disorder: the tumor secretes "random things," including ADH).
  • Sometimes SIADH is how the lung cancer is found.

Nursing care

  • Watch for cues of fluid volume overload; monitor mental status changes.
  • Restrict fluids (re-concentrates the serum); daily weights; I&O.
  • IV fluids per order, monitored very closely so they aren't over-administered.
  • Safety (confused patients); lots of patient and family support.

SIADH Collaborative Care

Immediate goal: restore normal fluid volume and normal osmolality.

SeverityTreatment
Mild symptoms, mild hyponatremiaFluid restriction, for example about 1 L a day.
Severe symptoms or severe hyponatremiaHypertonic saline (usually 3%; 5% is even more hypertonic), given slowly: too fast causes rapid fluid shifts in the brain that can be fatal. A stronger fluid restriction, such as 500 mL a day.
Chronic SIADH onlyDrug therapy (not first line): diuretics, or demeclocycline, which blocks the effects of ADH.

Diabetes Insipidus

Neurogenic (central) DI

  • The pituitary is damaged, for example by head trauma (a closed head injury from a car accident).
  • Abrupt onset; usually self-limiting (resolves on its own), but the symptoms must be treated while it lasts.

Nephrogenic DI

  • The pituitary is OK, but the kidneys don't respond to ADH.
  • Most common cause: lithium.
  • Less abrupt onset and less severe symptoms. (A third type, psychogenic, isn't covered.)

DI Nursing Care and Treatment

  • Maintain fluid and electrolyte status: IV fluids, I&O, daily weights, labs.
  • Notify the provider of an increase in urine volume with a decrease in specific gravity: the disease is getting worse, and the drug dose may need to go up.
  • Desmopressin (DDAVP): an exogenous, synthetic form of vasopressin (ADH), in very small doses: nasal spray, PO, IV or subQ (usually IV in the hospital).
  • Hormone therapy and fluid restriction are the cornerstones of treatment for neurogenic DI.
Labs worth memorizing: urine specific gravity 1.010 to 1.030; serum osmolality 270 to 290; serum sodium 135 to 145.
